## Does HexClad contain PTFE?

HexClad has published more than one answer. The current FAQ says the cookware coating is a ceramic it calls PTFE-free. Archived copies of the same FAQ say the coating contains PTFE, and a later archive says some products still did.

On November 18, 2026, [HexClad's FAQ](https://hexclad.com/pages/faq) answered "What type of nonstick coating does HexClad use?" with this sentence: "Our cookware products use our proprietary HEXCLAD TerraBond™ ceramic nonstick coating, which is PTFE-free and PFAS-free."

The [May 29, 2023 capture](https://web.archive.org/web/20230529151348/https://hexclad.com/pages/faq) on the Internet Archive asks a different question and gives a different answer: "Does HexClad contain PTFE? Our nonstick coating contains PTFE, which is listed in the Biomonitoring California Priority Chemicals List." That capture links to the California Department of Toxic Substances Control's [authoritative-lists page](https://dtsc.ca.gov/scp/authoritative-lists/). The May 2023 capture does not use the words TerraBond, ceramic, or PFAS in the text this guide could read.

The [September 12, 2024 capture](https://web.archive.org/web/20240912215117/https://hexclad.com/pages/faq) is the in-between page. It says: "Most of our products use our proprietary HEXCLAD TERRABOND™ coating, which is free of PTFE, to achieve their non-stick qualities. Some of our products contain PTFE, which is FDA certified as safe for use on cookware. PTFE is listed in the Biomonitoring California Priority Chemicals List." It tells the reader to contact HexClad for further information. It does not name which products still contained PTFE.

The [November 14, 2024 capture](https://web.archive.org/web/20241114051318/https://hexclad.com/pages/faq) matches the sentence on the live FAQ: cookware products use TerraBond ceramic nonstick, "which is PTFE-free and PFAS-free." ## What does a "PFOA-free" claim mean on a PTFE pan?

It is a claim about PFOA, perfluorooctanoic acid. It is not a claim that the pan has no PTFE. PTFE is the fluoropolymer used as nonstick. Minnesota's statute puts a fluorinated chemical with at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom inside the PFAS class. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration treats nonstick coatings on pots and pans as one authorized PFAS use.

Minnesota Statutes [section 116.943](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/116.943) defines "perfluoroalkyl and polyfluoroalkyl substances" or PFAS as "a class of fluorinated organic chemicals containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom." HexClad's 2023 and September 2024 FAQs say PTFE is listed on the Biomonitoring California Priority Chemicals List. That is HexClad pointing at California's list. It is the same PTFE the May 2023 FAQ says is in the coating.

The FDA's page on [authorized uses of PFAS in food contact](https://www.fda.gov/food/process-contaminants-food/authorized-uses-pfas-food-contact-applications) puts nonstick coatings in their own group, separate from grease-proofing on paper. For nonstick on pots and pans, the FDA says the PFAS molecules are polymerized and applied at very high temperatures so the coating is tightly bound, that the process drives off virtually all of the smaller molecules that could migrate, and that studies show negligible amounts of PFAS in this coating can migrate to food. A footnote says polymerized or large-molecule PFAS are not absorbed by the human body when ingested. The page says current data indicate that only the paper and paperboard grease-proofers would result in dietary exposure that may raise a potential safety concern, and that those grease-proofers are no longer sold for U.S. food-contact use.

HexClad's September 12, 2024 FAQ says PTFE "is FDA certified as safe for use on cookware." The FDA page read here says "authorized," and it describes polymerization and negligible migration. It does not use the word "certified" for a cookware coating. The migration paragraph is the FDA's statement about that kind of coating. It is not a test of one HexClad pan, and it does not say a PTFE coating is outside the PFAS class Minnesota defines.

PFOA is a different chemical from the PTFE polymer. In January 2006 the U.S. EPA asked eight companies to join the [2010/2015 PFOA Stewardship Program](https://www.epa.gov/assessing-and-managing-chemicals-under-tsca/fact-sheet-20102015-pfoa-stewardship-program). The goals were a 95 percent reduction in facility emissions and product content of PFOA by 2010, and work toward eliminating PFOA from emissions and product content by 2015. EPA's fact sheet says all eight companies submitted commitments, and that all participating companies state that they met the goals. A PTFE coating made after that program can be described as PFOA-free and still be PTFE. The May 2023 HexClad FAQ is an example of a page that names PTFE in the coating. A "PFOA-free" line on a box does not answer the PTFE question.

## What did the HexClad lawsuits claim?

Buyers alleged that HexClad marketed hybrid cookware as non-toxic and free of PFOA and PFAS while the nonstick coating contained PTFE. HexClad denies that. One federal case was dismissed without prejudice. The claims continued in Los Angeles Superior Court, which is the case the settlement agreement belongs to.

On June 27, 2023, Khushbu Didwania, Pratikkumar Patel, and Benjamin Adams filed a proposed class action in the U.S. District Court for the Central District of California, case [2:23-cv-05110](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/67533935/khuschbu-didwania-v-hexclad-cookware-inc/), against HexClad Cookware, Inc. The [complaint](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cacd.889622/gov.uscourts.cacd.889622.1.0.pdf) says HexClad "claims that their products are free from PFOA and PFAS 'forever chemicals' while also admitting they are coated with polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE)." It alleges breach of express warranty, negligent misrepresentation, violations of California's Consumer Legal Remedies Act, False Advertising Law, and Unfair Competition Law, and unjust enrichment. The complaint discusses a Consumer Reports test of other nonstick pans. It does not report a Consumer Reports test of a HexClad pan. A [stipulation filed December 22, 2023](https://truthinadvertising.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Didwania-v-Hexclad-Cookware-stipulation-of-voluntary-dismissal.pdf) dismissed that federal case without prejudice. The CourtListener docket lists the case as terminated on January 4, 2024.

The settlement agreement recites a second case. On November 17, 2023, Mandy Cliburn, Matthew Cliburn, Randi Gurka, Dana Swoyer, and Lori Cimonetti filed in the Superior Court of California for the County of Los Angeles, case 23STCV28390, against One Source to Market, LLC, doing business as Hexclad Cookware. The agreement says that complaint, and the federal one, concerned advertising about the nonstick coating, "including, but not limited to, claims that the products were 'non-toxic', 'metal utensil safe', 'PFAS Free', 'PFOA Free' or otherwise free from certain chemicals." The [settlement website](https://www.hexcladsettlement.com/) uses the same description and says HexClad "has denied any and all allegations of wrongdoing, fault, liability, or damage of any kind."

The agreement's denial is in the same document as the payment terms. HexClad "denies any and all allegations of wrongdoing, fault, liability, or damage of any kind to Plaintiffs and the Class" and "further denies that it acted improperly or wrongfully in any way." The agreement is a settlement of marketing claims. It is not a judgment that a PTFE pan is unsafe to cook with, and it is not a laboratory finding about TerraBond.

## What does the settlement agreement provide?

The posted agreement says HexClad will pay $2.5 million into a settlement fund if the court approves the settlement, and it says HexClad will stop using "PFAS free," "PFOA free," and "non-toxic" for products that contain PTFE or another PFAS. The official settlement site still says the court has not made that decision. A proposed approval order on the site leaves the judge's signature line blank.

The [settlement agreement](https://www.hexcladsettlement.com/media/5774841/settlement_agreement.pdf) defines the class as all persons and entities in the United States, its territories, and its possessions who purchased one or more of the eligible products. It sets the class period as February 1, 2022 through March 31, 2024. The settlement website says you are included if you bought an eligible product between those dates. Eligible products, in the agreement's list, include the 7-inch, 8-inch, 10-inch, 12-inch, and 14-inch hybrid fry pans, hybrid woks, 12-inch and 13-inch hybrid griddles, hybrid pots from 1 quart through 10 quarts, two hybrid deep sauté pans, and sets that include those pans, such as the Hybrid Perfect Pots and Pans Set. The list also covers "any such variations of these pans or sets." Lids, knives, and other non-pan pieces are excluded when the administrator calculates a claim on a set.

Subject to court approval, the agreement says the defendant will pay $2,500,000 to create a non-reversionary gross settlement fund. Notice and administration, any attorneys' fees and expenses the court orders, and any service awards come out of that fund before cash payments. A class member who submits a valid claim gets a pro rata share. That share cannot exceed what the person spent on the pots and pans. If claims exceed the money left, each award is reduced. The agreement says class counsel may ask for fees up to one-third of the fund plus costs, and for a service award of $2,500 for each named plaintiff. Those amounts are requests. They are not, in the agreement, amounts the court has ordered.

The injunctive section says the defendant will cease to market or advertise any product containing PTFE, or any chemical in the PFAS family, as "PFAS free" or "PFOA free," and will cease to market or advertise any such product as "non-toxic." It says nothing prevents HexClad from continuing to say a product is "metal utensil safe." It also says nothing prevents HexClad from marketing a product that does not contain PTFE or another PFAS chemical as "non-toxic" or "PFOA-free," and it gives "pans with ceramic or other coating(s) that don't contain PTFE" as the example.

The [settlement homepage](https://www.hexcladsettlement.com/), read for this guide, still says: "The Court in charge of this case still has to decide whether to approve the Settlement. If the Court denies final approval, the Settlement will be null and void and the litigation will continue with HexClad." The claim deadline printed there is November 14, 2025. The [claim page](https://hexcladsettlement.com/file-claim.aspx) says the deadline to submit a claim form has passed. A PDF on the settlement site, [captioned as a proposed order](https://www.hexcladsettlement.com/media/6777763/hxk_order_granting_final_approval.pdf) granting final approval, says the court entered preliminary approval on April 22, 2025, refers to fairness hearings on September 15, 2025, February 10, 2026, and February 20, 2026, and would approve a settlement "in the gross amount of $2,500,000.00." The copy posted there is headed "[PROPOSED]," and the line under "IT IS SO ORDERED" is "Dated:" with no date filled in. This guide did not find a signed final-approval order on that site. Until a signed order is the document in front of you, the binding description is the one the settlement website still prints: the settlement is proposed, and the court still has to decide.

## Do Minnesota, Colorado, or California ban this kind of pan?

Minnesota and Colorado restrict the sale of cookware that contains intentionally added PFAS. California's cookware law, in the sections below, requires disclosure and limits "free of" claims. A later California bill that would have banned the sale was vetoed. None of these rules is a recall of a pan already in a kitchen, and a brand's PFAS-free sentence is not, by itself, a state agency's test of that pan.

**Minnesota.** [Section 116.943](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/116.943), also called Amara's Law, says that beginning January 1, 2025, a person may not sell, offer for sale, or distribute for sale in the state cookware if the product contains intentionally added PFAS. Cookware means durable houseware used to prepare, dispense, or store food, foodstuffs, or beverages, including pots, pans, skillets, grills, baking sheets, baking molds, trays, bowls, and cooking utensils. "Intentionally added" means PFAS deliberately added where the continued presence is desired in the final product or a component to perform a specific function. The prohibition does not apply to a product that contains intentionally added PFAS only in electronic components or internal components. The section does not apply to the sale or resale of a used product. A PTFE nonstick layer that is there to release food is the use those definitions describe. A coating the maker says contains no PFAS is a different product claim. The statute is a sales rule. It does not order a household to discard a pan.

**Colorado.** Senate Bill 24-081, the [act signed May 1, 2024](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2024a_081_signed.pdf), adds a sale prohibition: on and after January 1, 2026, a person shall not sell, offer for sale, distribute for sale, or distribute for use in the state cookware that contains intentionally added PFAS chemicals. [Section 25-15-603](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_25-15-603) defines cookware as a durable houseware product used in residences or kitchens to prepare, dispense, or store food or beverages, including pots, pans, skillets, grills, baking sheets, baking molds, trays, bowls, and cooking utensils. The signed act says cookware does not include food equipment intended primarily for commercial settings. The same act repeals, effective January 1, 2026, the earlier requirement that cookware labels list intentionally added PFAS in the handle or in a food-contact surface. The sale prohibition is the rule that took that subsection's place.

**California.** [Health and Safety Code section 109010](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=HSC&sectionNum=109010) defines cookware the same way, as durable houseware used in homes and restaurants, including pots, pans, and the other items in the Minnesota list. [Section 109011](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=HSC&sectionNum=109011) says that beginning January 1, 2024, a manufacturer of cookware sold in the state that contains an intentionally added designated-list chemical in the handle, or in any surface that touches food, must list those chemicals on the product label, with an English and Spanish statement, a web address, and a machine-readable code. The statement also has to appear on the online listing. [Section 109013](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=HSC&sectionNum=109013) says a manufacturer shall not claim the cookware is free of a specific chemical if that chemical belongs to a chemical group or class on the designated list, unless no individual chemical from that group is intentionally added. The website version of that limit began January 1, 2023, and the package version began January 1, 2024. HexClad's older FAQ identified PTFE as a chemical on the Biomonitoring California Priority Chemicals List, which is one of the lists behind that designated-chemical system. The sections read here do not ban the sale of cookware that contains PTFE.

Senate Bill 682 would have added a later sale ban. The [bill-status page](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billStatusClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260SB682) lists the bill as inactive and vetoed. The governor vetoed it on October 13, 2025. The Senate sustained the veto and struck the bill from the file on March 2, 2026. The veto message on that page says the bill would have prohibited sale of cookware containing intentionally added PFAS beginning January 1, 2030, and that the governor sent it back because of the effect on affordable cookware. That bill is not law.

A HexClad page that says the current coating is PFAS-free is HexClad's claim about that coating. It is not a determination by Minnesota, Colorado, or California that a particular pan contains no intentionally added PFAS. A pan whose coating HexClad described as PTFE falls under the Minnesota and Colorado sale rules for intentionally added PFAS in cookware, in the states and on the dates those rules cover. The settlement agreement's own example of a product that may still be called non-toxic is a pan with a ceramic or other coating that does not contain PTFE.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the TerraBond coating the same coating HexClad described in 2023?

HexClad's pages do not say that. The May 29, 2023 FAQ says the nonstick coating contains PTFE. The current FAQ says cookware products use TerraBond ceramic nonstick that is PTFE-free and PFAS-free. The September 12, 2024 FAQ says both were on sale at once: most products used TerraBond, and some products contained PTFE. The pan's name can stay the same across that change. The FAQ date is the evidence this guide has.

### Did a court decide that HexClad pans are unsafe?

No decision like that appears in the documents read here. The complaints allege deceptive marketing. The settlement agreement says HexClad denies every allegation of wrongdoing. The FDA page on nonstick coatings says studies show negligible migration from polymerized nonstick into food. Poison Control's warning is about fumes when PTFE is heated above about 500°F or the coating is damaged. Those are different questions from a court finding about one brand.

### Can I still file a HexClad settlement claim?

The settlement website's claim page says the deadline has passed. The homepage lists that deadline as November 14, 2025. The same homepage says the court still has to decide whether to approve the settlement. This guide cannot reopen a claim or predict what the court will do.

### Does "oven-safe to 900°F" apply to a HexClad pan bought in 2023?

This guide did not find a HexClad page that puts the 900°F line on the PTFE coating described in the May 2023 FAQ. The 900°F figure is on the current 10-inch pan page, next to the TerraBond description. That same page says to start at lower heat and says the warranty does not cover excessive heat. For a PTFE-coated pan, Poison Control says breakdown starts above about 500°F. Follow the instructions for the pan you own.

### Is a ceramic nonstick coating a kind of PTFE?

Not by the definitions on the pages above. PTFE is the fluoropolymer the FDA discusses as nonstick and the chemical HexClad's 2023 FAQ said was in its coating. TerraBond, on HexClad's current pages, and Thermolon, on the GreenPan listing in this directory, are described by those brands as ceramic coatings they say are free of PFAS. A brand sentence is not a lab report. An uncoated stainless, cast-iron, or carbon-steel surface does not depend on that sentence.

### Do the state bans apply to a pan I already own?

The Minnesota and Colorado rules restrict selling and distributing cookware that contains intentionally added PFAS. Minnesota's statute says the section does not apply to the sale or resale of a used product. Neither statute, in the text read here, requires you to throw out a pan you already have. California's cookware sections require disclosure and limit "free of" claims. The vetoed bill is not a ban you can rely on.

## Bottom line

HexClad's FAQ today says TerraBond ceramic nonstick is PTFE-free and PFAS-free. HexClad's FAQ on May 29, 2023 said the nonstick coating contains PTFE, and the September 12, 2024 FAQ said some products still did. The Los Angeles settlement agreement is about "non-toxic," "PFAS free," and "PFOA free" marketing of named hybrid products bought from February 1, 2022 through March 31, 2024. It would fund a $2.5 million class settlement and would bar those phrases on a product that still contains PTFE or another PFAS. HexClad denies the allegations, and the settlement website still says the court has to approve the deal. A PFOA-free line does not answer the PTFE question. Minnesota and Colorado now restrict the sale of cookware with intentionally added PFAS. California requires disclosure. For a pan that is not sold as a fluoropolymer coating, use the stainless, cast iron, and carbon steel listings above, or a ceramic coating whose own listing says it is free of PFAS.
