**Quick answer:** Fluoride in tap water and toothpaste is not a PFAS, and it is not a forever chemical. Fluoride is the inorganic ion F-. [NIH's Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Fluoride-HealthProfessional/) describes it as the ionic form of the element fluorine, a mineral already present in soil, water, and food. PFAS, the class nicknamed forever chemicals, are synthetic organic chemicals. The [OECD's 2021 terminology report](https://one.oecd.org/document/ENV/CBC/MONO(2021)25/En/pdf) defines them as fluorinated substances with at least one fully fluorinated methyl carbon (−CF3) or methylene carbon (−CF2−). [EPA's TSCA reporting definition](https://www.epa.gov/assessing-and-managing-chemicals-under-tsca/tsca-section-8a7-reporting-and-recordkeeping) uses three carbon-fluorine structures and says PFAS do not occur naturally. A fluoride ion has no carbon, so it meets neither definition. For community water systems that add fluoride, the [U.S. Public Health Service recommends 0.7 mg/L](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2015/05/01/2015-10201/public-health-service-recommendation-for-fluoride-concentration-in-drinking-water-for-prevention-of). [EPA's enforceable fluoride limit](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/fluoride-drinking-water) is still 4.0 mg/L. [EPA's April 2024 PFAS rule](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas) sets 4.0 parts per trillion for PFOA and 4.0 parts per trillion for PFOS. Those PFAS figures were still the levels printed on EPA's page on November 16, 2026, and the two May 2026 changes were still proposals. The shared element is fluorine. The chemicals, the units, and the filters are different.

PFAS testing of bottled water is in the [bottled water guide](/post/does-bottled-water-contain-pfas-spring-vs-purified). ## What is fluoride?

Fluoride is a single inorganic ion. It is not a chain of carbon and fluorine, and it is not a manufactured polymer.

[NIH](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Fluoride-HealthProfessional/) says fluoride is the ionic form of the element fluorine. Soil, water, plants, and foods contain trace amounts. The [Center for Environmental Health's 2025 explainer](https://ceh.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Fluoride-VS.-PFAS-explainerDIGITAL.pdf) uses the same description: fluoride is the negatively charged ion of fluorine, part of soils and bedrock, and naturally present in groundwater, surface water, and rainwater. The ion is written F-.

When a utility adjusts the level, the additives NIH lists are fluorosilicic acid, sodium fluorosilicate, and sodium fluoride. Those are fluoride compounds used to deliver the F- ion. They are not perfluorinated carbon chains. In toothpaste, NIH says the usual forms are sodium fluoride or monofluorophosphate, most often at 1,000 to 1,100 mg/L. FDA's anticaries monograph also allows stannous fluoride. That ingredient list is in the toothpaste section below.

## What are PFAS?

PFAS are synthetic organic chemicals defined by fully fluorinated carbon. The OECD's 2021 definition says a PFAS has at least one fully fluorinated methyl carbon (−CF3) or methylene carbon (−CF2−). EPA's TSCA reporting rule uses three carbon-fluorine structures and says PFAS do not occur naturally. A lone fluorine atom is not enough.

The OECD report *Reconciling Terminology of the Universe of Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances* (ENV/CBC/MONO(2021)25, 2021) states the general definition in full: PFAS are fluorinated substances that contain at least one fully fluorinated methyl or methylene carbon atom, without any hydrogen, chlorine, bromine, or iodine attached to that carbon. In the report's own shorthand, any chemical with at least a perfluorinated methyl group (−CF3) or a perfluorinated methylene group (−CF2−) is a PFAS, with a few noted exceptions. One exception the report spells out is that a perfluorinated methine carbon alone (>CF−) does not meet the definition. The report also says the name PFAS communicates that shared structural trait. It does not, by itself, say whether a given compound is harmful. The same report places polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) in the PFAS universe, as a fluoropolymer whose backbone is a chain of perfluorinated methylene carbons.

EPA uses more than one working scope. On the [TSCA section 8(a)(7) page](https://www.epa.gov/assessing-and-managing-chemicals-under-tsca/tsca-section-8a7-reporting-and-recordkeeping), EPA says PFAS are synthetic organic compounds that do not occur naturally. For that reporting rule, a substance is a PFAS if it contains at least one of three structures: R-(CF2)-CF(R′)R″, where both the CF2 and CF carbons are saturated; R-CF2OCF2-R′, where R and R′ can be F, O, or saturated carbons; or CF3C(CF3)R′R″, where R′ and R″ can be F or saturated carbons. EPA says the carbon-fluorine bonds make some PFAS resistant to degradation and highly persistent, and that some can bioaccumulate. The same page says EPA had identified at least 1,462 PFAS covered by TSCA that might fall under the rule as of February 2023, of which 770 were on the active Inventory. That count is a regulatory list under one definition. It is not a count of every structure the OECD sentence could cover.

[EPA's PFAS overview](https://www.epa.gov/pfas/pfas-explained) describes the class in public language: widely used, long-lasting chemicals, components of which break down very slowly, found in water, air, fish, and soil, with thousands of PFAS in consumer, commercial, and industrial products. An [EPA training document on the 2024 drinking-water rule](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2026-02/pfas-1tribal-training-jan2026.pdf) describes PFAS as man-made organic chemicals manufactured since the 1940s, containing carbon-fluorine bonds. The six chemicals with enforceable drinking-water limits, listed later in this guide, are individual members of that class. They are not a definition of the whole class.

CEH's 2025 explainer draws the practical line for a non-chemist. It calls fluoride the F- ion and calls PFAS man-made chemicals that contain at least one carbon atom fully bonded to fluorine. That sentence is close to the OECD and EPA structural idea, and it is the distinction this guide is about. CEH's wording is not a substitute for the OECD sentence or for the three structures in the TSCA rule. Those two texts are the definitions to quote.

## Why do fluoride and PFAS get confused?

Both involve fluorine, and both show up in conversations about tap water. The formulas do not match, and the regulated concentrations do not share a unit.

A fluoride ion is one atom of fluorine with a negative charge. It has no carbon. The OECD definition and EPA's TSCA definition each require a carbon-fluorine structure of a specific kind. Without carbon, F- cannot be a PFAS under either text. Sodium fluoride, the salt used in some toothpaste and as one drinking-water additive, is an inorganic salt that releases that ion. Sodium monofluorophosphate binds fluorine to phosphorus, not to a perfluorinated carbon. Stannous fluoride is an inorganic tin salt. None of those three is a perfluorinated methyl or methylene compound.

PFAS persist for a different structural reason. EPA ties that persistence to the carbon-fluorine bond, which it describes as one of the strongest in chemistry, and says some PFAS remain in the body for months to years. Fluoride is handled differently. NIH says about 80% or more of ingested fluoride is absorbed, that adults retain about half of what is absorbed, almost all of that in bones and teeth, and that the rest leaves in urine. Storage in bone is a real fact about fluoride. It is not the carbon-fluorine persistence EPA describes for PFAS.

The numbers in the rules are easy to mash together because both can be spoken as "4.0." They are not the same measurement.

| Benchmark | Chemical | Figure as published | Same figure in nanograms per liter |
|---|---|---|---|
| PHS recommendation for systems that add fluoride | Fluoride ion | 0.7 mg/L | 700,000 ng/L |
| EPA secondary standard, with a required public notice | Fluoride ion | 2.0 mg/L | 2,000,000 ng/L |
| EPA maximum contaminant level and goal | Fluoride ion | 4.0 mg/L | 4,000,000 ng/L |
| EPA maximum contaminant level | PFOA, and separately PFOS | 4.0 ng/L (4.0 ppt) | 4.0 ng/L |

EPA says parts per trillion and nanograms per liter are the same unit for the PFAS limits. One milligram per liter is 1,000,000 nanograms per liter, which is ordinary metric conversion for water. EPA's fluoride MCL of 4.0 mg/L is therefore 4,000,000 ng/L. The PFOA MCL is 4.0 ng/L. The conversion shows why the two "4.0" rules cannot be read as the same stringency. It is not a statement that one chemical is a million times safer, or a million times more toxic, than the other. They are different substances with different standards.

## Where do fluoride and PFAS show up?

Fluoride is a natural ion that some water systems also adjust on purpose. PFAS are industrial chemicals that reach people through products and through contaminated water, food, air, and dust.

On fluoride, the [2015 Public Health Service recommendation](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2015/05/01/2015-10201/public-health-service-recommendation-for-fluoride-concentration-in-drinking-water-for-prevention-of) applies to community water systems that add fluoride. The recommended concentration is 0.7 mg/L. The previous recommendation was a range, 0.7 to 1.2 mg/L, tied to outdoor air temperature. The 2015 update dropped the temperature range. Systems with naturally occurring fluoride above 0.7 mg/L are outside that "add fluoride" recommendation. The Federal Register notice estimated that those natural levels served about 11 million people at the time. NIH, citing 2020 figures, says 62.9% of the U.S. population had access to a fluoridated community water system, and that fluoridated drinking water and the foods and drinks made with it account for about 60% of fluoride intake in the United States. CDC's [fluoridation FAQ](https://www.cdc.gov/fluoridation/faq/) says CDC does not mandate fluoridation. State and local governments decide. Some places already have natural fluoride at a level used for cavity prevention.

PFAS show up in a different inventory. EPA's public pages and the 2026 drinking-water training list firefighting foam, nonstick cookware, food packaging, water-repellent clothing, and stain-resistant fabrics and carpets. EPA also says many PFAS are found at low levels in food and in the environment, and that most people in the United States have been exposed. Drinking water is one route, which is why the 2024 rule exists. It is not the only route.

One product category mixes the two vocabularies. Fluoride toothpaste uses the fluoride salts above. Some dental floss has used PTFE, and PTFE is a PFAS under the OECD definition. Those are different objects. The floss history is in its own section below, and in the [floss guide](/post/does-dental-floss-contain-pfas-pfas-free-floss-swaps) and the [2026 floss roundup](/post/best-pfas-free-dental-floss-2026).

## What is the U.S. rule for fluoride in drinking water?

The enforceable federal ceiling and the public-health recommendation are different numbers, set by different agencies, for different purposes. Neither number was revised by the assessment EPA opened in 2026.

[EPA's fluoride page](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/fluoride-drinking-water), last updated August 3, 2026, says the maximum contaminant level is 4.0 milligrams per liter. EPA says that standard was set in 1986, was most recently reviewed in 2024, and is meant to prevent known or anticipated adverse health effects. The [national primary drinking water regulations table](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations) lists the fluoride MCLG as 4.0 and the MCL as 4.0, and it names bone disease, described as pain and tenderness of the bones, and mottled teeth in children. The potential sources in that table are a water additive used to promote strong teeth, erosion of natural deposits, and discharge from fertilizer and aluminum factories.

The secondary standard is 2.0 mg/L. [EPA's secondary-standards page](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/drinking-water-regulations-and-contaminants) says secondary standards are non-enforceable federal guidelines for cosmetic or aesthetic effects, and that states may adopt them as enforceable. Fluoride is the exception with a federal notice duty: community water systems that exceed 2.0 mg/L but do not exceed the 4.0 mg/L primary standard must notify people served, no later than 12 months after the system learns of the exceedance, under 40 CFR 141.208. [CDC's FAQ](https://www.cdc.gov/fluoridation/faq/) describes the 2.0 mg/L guideline as protection against dental fluorosis while teeth are forming, up to about age 8, and says less than 1% of the U.S. population has private-well or system water above 2.0 mg/L. CDC says that if water is at or above 4.0 mg/L, people should contact the local public health department and consider a filter that removes fluoride for drinking and cooking. CDC also says fluoride is not absorbed through skin, so water above that level can still be used for cleaning and bathing.

The 0.7 mg/L figure is not that ceiling. The Public Health Service recommendation is guidance for systems that choose to fluoridate. [CDC's recommendations page](https://www.cdc.gov/fluoridation/about/community-water-fluoridation-recommendations.html) says the PHS panel set 0.7 mg/L as the concentration that provides oral-health benefits while limiting dental fluorosis, and that CDC continues to recommend community water fluoridation at that concentration. A [2023 CDC MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/72/wr/mm7222a1.htm) states that water fluoridated at the PHS level reduces cavities by about 25% in children and adults. CDC's FAQ says the recommended level is not an enforceable standard.

A separate line of evidence is about higher exposures and child IQ. The [National Toxicology Program's August 2024 monograph](https://ntp.niehs.nih.gov/research/assessments/noncancer/completed/fluoride) concluded, with moderate confidence, that higher fluoride exposure, such as drinking water containing more than 1.5 mg/L, is associated with lower IQ in children. NTP says the review covered total fluoride exposure from all sources and was not designed to evaluate fluoridated drinking water alone. NTP says there were insufficient data to determine whether 0.7 mg/L, the level recommended for U.S. community water, has a negative effect on children's IQ. The monograph states that it does not address whether fluoride added to drinking water at 0.7 mg/L is associated with a measurable effect on IQ, and that it does not assess oral-health benefits or provide a risk-benefit analysis. NTP also says it found no evidence that fluoride exposure had adverse effects on adult cognition. This guide reports those sentences side by side with CDC's recommendation. It does not turn them into a verdict on fluoridation.

EPA has started a new health review and has not used it to change the 4.0 mg/L MCL. On January 22, 2026, EPA released a preliminary assessment plan and literature survey for public comment through February 27, 2026. On August 3, 2026, EPA released the *Protocol for Developing the Fluoride Human Health Toxicity Assessment* (EPA-822-D-26-001). The fluoride page says a routine six-year review of new science would not have been due until 2030, and that EPA moved the work earlier. The page still states the MCL as 4.0 mg/L. A protocol is a plan for an assessment. It is not a new maximum contaminant level.

[NIH](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Fluoride-HealthProfessional/) notes a related bottled-water point. When fluoride is added to bottled water, FDA says the total, added plus naturally occurring, cannot exceed 0.7 mg/L. NIH says that limit does not apply to bottled water that contains only the fluoride that came with the source and that makes no fluoride claim. PFAS in bottled water is a different rulemaking, covered in the [bottled water guide](/post/does-bottled-water-contain-pfas-spring-vs-purified).

## What is the U.S. rule for PFAS in drinking water?

The enforceable PFAS limits are parts-per-trillion maximum contaminant levels for six named chemicals and one mixture. They apply to public water systems. They say nothing about fluoride.

[EPA's PFAS drinking-water page](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas), reviewed for this guide on November 16, 2026, still summarizes the final rule announced on April 10, 2024. The enforceable levels on that page are:

| Compound | MCLG | Enforceable MCL |
|---|---|---|
| PFOA | Zero | 4.0 ppt (ng/L) |
| PFOS | Zero | 4.0 ppt |
| PFHxS | 10 ppt | 10 ppt |
| PFNA | 10 ppt | 10 ppt |
| HFPO-DA (GenX chemicals) | 10 ppt | 10 ppt |
| Mixture of two or more of PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and PFBS | Hazard index of 1 | Hazard index of 1 |

The [Federal Register notice of the final rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/04/26/2024-07773/pfas-national-primary-drinking-water-regulation) sets the effective date as June 25, 2024. It requires initial monitoring by April 26, 2027, and compliance with the MCLs by April 26, 2029. EPA's page describes the same timeline in years: three years to finish initial monitoring, by 2027, and five years, by 2029, to install treatment when averages exceed a limit. Compliance uses a running annual average at the sampling point.

On May 18, 2026, EPA announced two proposed rules. The page reviewed on November 16, 2026 still calls both of them proposals. The [PFOA and PFOS extension proposal](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/proposed-pfoa-and-pfos-compliance-extension-rule) would keep the 4.0 ppt limits and let a system request two more years, to 2031. The [Federal Register notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/05/20/2026-10086/extending-the-compliance-deadline-for-the-pfoa-and-pfos-maximum-contaminant-levels), 91 FR 29425 (May 20, 2026), says the extension from April 26, 2029 to April 26, 2031 would apply to systems that submit a request. It is not an automatic change of the date. The [rescission proposal](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/proposed-pfas-rescission-rule) would remove the drinking-water regulations for PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and the hazard-index mixture of those three plus PFBS. The comment period on both proposals closed on July 20, 2026. This guide does not treat either proposal as a repeal of the 4.0 ppt PFOA and PFOS limits. EPA's page still prints all six 2024 limits above the proposal text.

Those limits are for the named PFAS at public water systems. They are not a fluoride standard, not a toothpaste standard, and not a bottled-water standard. The bottled-water status of PFAS is in the [bottled water guide](/post/does-bottled-water-contain-pfas-spring-vs-purified).

## Which home water filters reduce fluoride, and which reduce PFAS?

Match the technology to the chemical. A filter that reduces many PFAS can leave fluoride in place. A filter certified for fluoride is not automatically certified for PFOA or PFOS.

[EPA's home-filter page](https://www.epa.gov/cleanups/reducing-pfas-your-drinking-water-home-filter) says granular activated carbon, reverse osmosis, and ion exchange can reduce PFAS, and that not every filter does. The check it names is certification to NSF/ANSI 53 or NSF/ANSI 58 for PFAS reduction. EPA says those certifications, as of April 2024, focus on PFOA and PFOS and do not yet show removal down to the drinking-water limits. The page does not mention fluoride.

Fluoride has its own record. [CDC's home-treatment page](https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/about/about-home-water-treatment-systems.html), dated April 10, 2024, says reverse osmosis may reduce fluoride, along with arsenic, radium, sulfate, and several other ions, and that the label is the list that matters for a given system. The [American Dental Association's Fluoridation Facts](https://ebooks.ada.org/fluoridationfacts/45) (2025) says that, when they are working properly, almost all reverse osmosis systems and distillation units remove most of the fluoride. It says most carbon filters do not remove fluoride. It says some filters that contain activated alumina can remove significant amounts of fluoride, and some filters that contain bone char can too. It says each filter should be judged on its own, because the result depends on the type, the quality, the condition, and the age. A [University of Nebraska–Lincoln extension guide](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/extensionhist/2800) on activated-carbon filtration says carbon filters will not remove fluoride, and groups fluoride with other inorganic contaminants carbon is not used for. "Most" in the ADA sentence and "will not" in the extension guide are the same practical warning: a pitcher sold for chlorine taste is not a fluoride filter unless the certificate says fluoride.

EPA's March 2014 design manual, *Removal of Fluoride from Drinking Water Supplies by Activated Alumina* (EPA/600/R-14/236), identifies activated alumina and reverse osmosis as the best technologies generally available for fluoride removal at a treatment plant. It says activated alumina is the preferred plant process when fluoride is the only target, and reverse osmosis is the recommended process when dissolved solids and other contaminants have to come out as well. Point-of-use reverse osmosis is also listed there as a small-system compliance technology. The manual says bone char, charred animal bone made up of tricalcium phosphate and carbon, has been tested and used in full-scale fluoride removal. It also says bone char can slough phosphate, is more soluble in acidic water than activated alumina, costs more, and has left a bad taste in some cases, and for those reasons the manual does not treat bone char as an economically viable plant option. That is a utility-design judgment from 2014. It does not erase the ADA's later statement that some home bone-char filters remove significant fluoride. It does separate bone char from a plain activated-carbon filter: the manual describes bone char as that phosphate-and-carbon medium, while the ADA and the Nebraska extension guide say ordinary carbon filters are not how fluoride is removed.

The manual also records the NSF/ANSI 58 fluoride test line as it stood in the standard NSF described to EPA: certified devices reduce fluoride from an average influent of 8.0 mg/L to an effluent below 1.5 mg/L. That challenge water is far above the 0.7 mg/L PHS level. A pass at 1.5 mg/L is below EPA's 2.0 mg/L secondary standard and below the 4.0 mg/L MCL. It is not a test result for a kitchen tap that started at 0.7 mg/L, and it is not a promise of zero fluoride.

| Technology | Fluoride, from the sources above | PFAS, from EPA |
|---|---|---|
| Granular activated carbon | ADA: most carbon filters do not remove it. Nebraska extension: carbon filtration will not. | EPA: can reduce PFAS. Look for NSF/ANSI 53 for PFAS reduction. |
| Reverse osmosis | CDC: may reduce it. ADA: almost all systems remove most of it when working. NSF/ANSI 58 can carry a fluoride claim. The published test line is 8.0 mg/L down to below 1.5 mg/L. | EPA: can reduce PFAS. Look for NSF/ANSI 58 for PFAS reduction. A fluoride line on that certificate is not a PFAS line. |
| Activated alumina | EPA design manual: a best available plant technology for fluoride. ADA: some home filters that contain it remove significant fluoride. | Not a technology EPA names for home PFAS reduction. |
| Bone char | ADA: some of these filters remove significant fluoride. EPA's 2014 manual: used at plants, and not the economical plant choice next to activated alumina. | Not a technology EPA names for home PFAS reduction. Ordinary activated carbon and bone char are different media. |
| Distillation | ADA: removes most fluoride when the unit is working. | Not the method EPA lists for home PFAS filters. The [water filters guide](/post/do-water-filters-remove-pfas-best-pfas-water-filters) cites NSF's statement that boiling does not reduce PFAS exposure. |
| Water softener | ADA: softening does not significantly change fluoride. CDC: some softeners may remove fluoride, so read that unit's label. | Not a PFAS technology on EPA's home-filter page. |

Maintenance sits on both sides of the table. EPA says a PFAS filter has to be replaced on the manufacturer's schedule, and that a late change can increase PFAS exposure. ADA says the fluoride result depends on the age and condition of the filter. A certificate names the gallons it was tested for.

## Is fluoride toothpaste a PFAS?

The fluoride ingredient in ordinary anticaries toothpaste is a fluoride salt, not a PFAS. PTFE dental floss is the product in this aisle that has actually been a PFAS.

[FDA's OTC Monograph M021](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/omuf/monographs/OTC%20Monograph%20M021-Anticaries%20Drug%20Products%20for%20OTC%20Human%20Use%2005.02.2023.pdf), posted May 2, 2023, lists three anticaries active ingredients for dentifrices: sodium fluoride, sodium monofluorophosphate, and stannous fluoride. For gels and pastes, the monograph sets a theoretical total fluorine of 850 to 1,150 ppm for the sodium fluoride products, for the sodium monofluorophosphate products in that band, and for the stannous fluoride products in that band. A separate sodium monofluorophosphate paste is listed at 1,500 ppm theoretical total fluorine. [NIH](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Fluoride-HealthProfessional/) says most toothpaste sold in the United States contains sodium fluoride or monofluorophosphate, most commonly at 1,000 to 1,100 mg/L, about 1.3 mg in a quarter teaspoon. [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/oral-health/prevention/about-fluoride.html) says to brush at least twice a day with fluoride toothpaste. Before age 3, CDC says parents should use no more than a smear the size of a grain of rice, and that children younger than 2 should be checked with a doctor or dentist first. From ages 3 to 6, CDC says no more than a pea-sized amount. CDC says parents should watch children brush and teach them to spit the toothpaste out.

Those active ingredients do not meet the OECD or TSCA definitions above. Sodium fluoride and stannous fluoride are inorganic salts. Sodium monofluorophosphate puts fluorine on a phosphorus-oxygen group, not on a fully fluorinated carbon. This guide does not test finished tubes, and it does not claim that every ingredient or every tube resin has been screened for a PFAS. The fluoride on the drug-facts label is not itself a PFAS.

Dental floss is the lookalike. PTFE, sold under the Teflon name, is a fluoropolymer, and the OECD terminology report lists it as a PFAS. [Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/toxic-chemicals-substances/dental-floss-without-pfas-and-other-harmful-chemicals-a9722832754/), in an article updated January 18, 2025, reported that the maker of Oral-B Glide said the floss material contains PTFE. A [2019 paper in the *Journal of Exposure Science & Environmental Epidemiology*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41370-018-0109-y) associated reported Glide use with higher blood PFHxS and found fluorine in the Glide products it screened. The [American Dental Association](https://commons.ada.org/newsreleases/120/) said that evidence was not enough to stop cleaning between teeth. [Silent Spring Institute](https://www.silentspring.org/news/oral-b-stops-using-teflon-its-popular-glide-dental-floss) reported on March 31, 2026 that Oral-B says the updated Glide fiber is no longer made with PTFE and that none of its floss is made with PFAS. The measurements, the ADA response, and the formula update are written out in [Does Dental Floss Contain PFAS?](/post/does-dental-floss-contain-pfas-pfas-free-floss-swaps). Current flosses with a no-PFAS or no-PTFE statement are compared in [Best PFAS-Free Dental Floss (2026)](/post/best-pfas-free-dental-floss-2026).

Fluoride on a toothpaste label and PTFE on a floss label are not two words for one chemical. One is an inorganic ion used as a cavity-preventing drug. The other is a perfluorinated polymer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is fluoride a forever chemical?

No. Forever chemical is the common name for PFAS. Fluoride is the inorganic ion F-. The OECD definition and EPA's TSCA definition both require fluorinated carbon structures. A fluoride ion has no carbon.

### Does a Brita-style carbon pitcher remove fluoride?

The American Dental Association says most carbon filters do not remove fluoride. A University of Nebraska–Lincoln extension guide on activated carbon says these filters will not remove fluoride. EPA says granular activated carbon can reduce PFAS, which is a different claim. Unless the certificate names fluoride, a carbon pitcher is not a fluoride filter.

### Does reverse osmosis remove both fluoride and PFAS?

It can, and the certificate has to say which one. CDC says reverse osmosis may reduce fluoride. EPA lists reverse osmosis among home technologies that can reduce PFAS. NSF/ANSI 58 is the reverse osmosis standard that can carry either claim. The iSpring RCC7AK-BN listing names fluoride and does not name PFAS. The AquaTru Carafe sheet prints a fluoride row and a PFOA/PFOS row, with the pass levels in the product section.

### Are 4.0 mg/L of fluoride and 4.0 ppt of PFOA the same limit?

No. Milligrams per liter and parts per trillion differ by a factor of one million. EPA's fluoride MCL of 4.0 mg/L equals 4,000,000 ng/L. EPA's PFOA MCL is 4.0 ng/L. The Public Health Service recommendation for added fluoride is 0.7 mg/L, which equals 700,000 ng/L. The conversion compares units. It does not compare toxicity.

### Did EPA lower or raise the fluoride MCL in 2026?

The fluoride page updated August 3, 2026 still states the MCL as 4.0 mg/L, set in 1986 and reviewed in 2024. In 2026 EPA released a preliminary assessment plan and then a protocol for a human-health toxicity assessment. Those documents are the review. They are not a revised maximum contaminant level. The secondary standard remains 2.0 mg/L.

### Did EPA drop the 4 ppt PFAS limits in 2026?

The EPA page reviewed on November 16, 2026 does not say that. The April 10, 2024 limits are still printed there, including 4.0 ppt for PFOA and 4.0 ppt for PFOS. Two May 18, 2026 actions were still proposals after the July 20, 2026 comment period. One would let systems request a compliance date of April 26, 2031 instead of April 26, 2029. The other would rescind the limits for PFHxS, PFNA, GenX, and the hazard-index mixture. The extension proposal says the 4.0 ppt limits would stay.

### Is the fluoride in toothpaste a PFAS?

The anticaries ingredients FDA lists are sodium fluoride, sodium monofluorophosphate, and stannous fluoride. Those are inorganic salts or a phosphorus-fluorine compound, not a fully fluorinated carbon chemical. CDC recommends brushing with fluoride toothpaste and using a rice-sized or pea-sized amount for young children. This guide does not claim a laboratory screen of every toothpaste ingredient or tube.

### Is fluoride toothpaste the same issue as PTFE dental floss?

No. PTFE is a fluoropolymer, and the OECD report classifies it as a PFAS. Some floss, including the Glide material described to Consumer Reports, used PTFE. Oral-B's later statement, reported by Silent Spring on March 31, 2026, says the new Glide fiber is not formulated with PTFE. The study and the formula update are in the [dental floss guide](/post/does-dental-floss-contain-pfas-pfas-free-floss-swaps).

## Bottom line

Fluoride and PFAS share the element fluorine and almost nothing else that a drinking-water rule cares about. Fluoride is the inorganic ion F-, found in water on its own and added by some utilities at a recommended 0.7 mg/L. PFAS are synthetic organic chemicals defined by fully fluorinated carbon. EPA's fluoride MCL is still 4.0 mg/L, with a 2.0 mg/L secondary standard that triggers a public notice. EPA's PFAS MCLs are still the April 2024 parts-per-trillion limits, including 4.0 ppt for PFOA and 4.0 ppt for PFOS, and the May 2026 proposals had not replaced that rule on the page reviewed November 16, 2026. Activated carbon is a PFAS technology on EPA's list and, for most carbon filters, not a fluoride technology on the ADA's account. Reverse osmosis is the method those sources put on both sides, if the certificate names the contaminant. In this directory, that is the [iSpring RCC7AK-BN](/products/under-sink-reverse-osmosis-filter) for an NSF/ANSI 58 fluoride claim, and the [AquaTru Carafe](/products/aquatru-countertop-ro-system) for a data sheet that prints both fluoride and PFOA/PFOS, with the pending-FAQ caveat above.
