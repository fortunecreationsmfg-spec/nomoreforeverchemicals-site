**Quick answer:** Grease-proofing agents containing PFAS were applied to paper and paperboard so oil and water would not soak through. On February 28, 2024, the FDA announced that those materials are no longer being sold for use in U.S. food packaging. The agency named fast-food wrappers, microwave popcorn bags, take-out paperboard containers, and pet food bags. A typical pizza box is the take-out paperboard the FDA named. The FDA pages cited here do not publish a separate lab result for pizza boxes. In January 2025 the FDA determined that 35 related food-contact notifications are no longer effective as of January 6, 2025, with a compliance date of June 30, 2025 for certain packaging already produced. A sales stop, plus time to exhaust stock, is not a test of every box on a shelf. Older stock and packaging that never passed through that U.S. sales stop can differ, and the FDA pages cited here do not describe a recall of meals already sold. A 2017 study of 407 pieces of U.S. fast-food packaging, collected in 2014 and 2015, found total fluorine above 16 nmol per square centimeter in 46% of food-contact papers and 20% of food-contact paperboard. That paperboard category included boxes for fries and pizza. The study predates the phase-out, and the authors said the detection rates were not necessarily representative of the marketplace. "PFOA-free" names one chemical. "Compostable" describes breakdown. Neither word is a PFAS-free test. Move greasy takeout into glass before you store or reheat it.

Below is what the FDA said the phase-out covers, what that 2017 study measured, and the storage pieces linked from the [non-toxic products directory](/non-toxic-products). Microwave popcorn bags are covered in the [popcorn guide](/post/is-microwave-popcorn-safe-pfas-in-bags-safer-popping-swaps).

## Do pizza boxes and takeout wrappers contain PFAS?

They were a main use of the grease-proofing agents the FDA has been taking off the U.S. market. The February 28, 2024 constituent update says the PFAS substances were applied to fast-food wrappers, microwave popcorn bags, take-out paperboard containers, pet food bags, and other similar packaging, to resist grease, oil, and water. The constituent update says completing that voluntary phase-out eliminates the primary source of dietary exposure to PFAS from authorized food-contact uses. The press announcement says the major source of dietary exposure from that packaging is being eliminated.

A pizza box sits in the paperboard category. A burger wrapper or a pastry bag sits in the food-contact paper category. The FDA named the categories. It did not, on the pages cited here, print a 2026 test of a named restaurant's box.

Two limits matter before anyone treats a phase-out as a clean bill for the container in hand.

**The action is a stop in sales of the grease-proofing substances, plus later paperwork that those notifications are no longer effective.** The FDA's February 28, 2024 update says the original commitment letters allowed about 18 months to exhaust market supply from the last date of sale, and that most of the companies had left the market before their original phase-out date. The January 2025 notice then set a compliance date for certain packaging already made. None of those sentences is a laboratory slip taped to one box.

**A blank package is not a result.** The FDA's authorized-uses page says PFAS may also occur as an impurity formed during processing, or as a contaminant, for example when manufacturing water already contains PFAS. Highly sensitive tests can find PFAS even when they were not intentionally used, including at levels the FDA says would not be a health concern. The same page says a total-fluorine test only measures fluorine. It cannot tell PFAS apart from other fluorine-containing substances.

## What did the Schaider study find in U.S. fast-food packaging?

The paper is Laurel A. Schaider and colleagues, "Fluorinated Compounds in U.S. Fast Food Packaging," *Environmental Science & Technology Letters* 2017, volume 4, issue 3, pages 105–111. In 2014 and 2015 they collected 407 samples of paper and paperboard food wrappers and related packaging at U.S. fast-food restaurants. The paper says 98% came from five regions: western Washington, eastern Massachusetts, western Michigan, northern California, and metropolitan Washington, D.C. Three hundred ninety-five samples came from 27 large chains. Twelve came from four individual restaurants. The design covered a wide set of package types and places. The paper says it did not necessarily reflect the overall distribution of fluorine in fast-food packaging, and that the detection frequencies are not necessarily representative of the marketplace.

They measured total fluorine with particle-induced gamma-ray emission spectroscopy. The limit of detection was 16 nmol of fluorine per square centimeter. Of 407 samples, 33% were above that line. Concentrations in the detects ranged from 16 to 800 nmol/cm². The limit of quantification was 50 nmol/cm², and values between the two limits are described as estimates.

Table 1 splits the detects by package type:

| Package type in the 2017 paper | Samples | Share above 16 nmol/cm² |
|---|---|---|
| Sandwich, burger, and fried-food papers | 138 | 38% |
| Dessert and bread wrappers | 68 | 56% |
| Tex-Mex food packaging | 42 | 57% |
| Food-contact paper, all of the above | 248 | 46% |
| Food-contact paperboard, including fry and pizza boxes | 80 | 20% |
| Paper that did not touch food | 15 | 0% |
| Paper cups | 30 | 0% |
| Other beverage containers | 25 | 16% |
| Miscellaneous, such as lids and applesauce containers | 9 | 0% |
| All samples | 407 | 33% |

The paper's own examples for food-contact paperboard are boxes for fries and pizza. It does not print a pizza-box-only percentage. Twenty percent is the share for that whole paperboard group of 80 samples. Dessert and bread wrappers and Tex-Mex packaging were the paper categories with the higher shares. Papers that did not touch food, and the paper cups in this set, had no detects. The authors say fluorine was more common in grease-proof products than in products holding liquids or not meant to touch food.

A total-fluorine detect is not a list of named PFAS. To check what the fluorine was, the lab ran liquid chromatography and high-resolution mass spectrometry on a subset of 20 samples: 14 with total fluorine above 200 nmol/cm² and six below the detection limit. The analyst did not know which samples were high or low. The most commonly detected types were perfluorocarboxylates such as PFOA and PFHxA, perfluorosulfonates such as PFBS, and fluorotelomer sulfonates such as 6:2 FTS, along with unidentified polyfluorinated compounds. Six of those 20 samples, all collected in 2014 and 2015, contained detectable PFOA. The paper notes that U.S. manufacturers had agreed in 2011 to stop distributing products containing C8 perfluorinated compounds for food-contact purposes in interstate commerce. These samples predate the FDA's statement that, as of November 2016, long-chain PFAS were no longer used in food-contact applications sold in the United States.

In 10 of the 14 high-fluorine samples, the total peak area for known and unknown PFAS was higher than in the six samples below the detection limit. Four samples above 200 nmol/cm² did not show that elevation. The authors give two readings of that gap. One is that much of the fluorine was inorganic or a non-PFAS organofluorine compound. The other is that the extraction missed a substantial share of the PFAS, including volatile compounds, polymers, or newer replacements. The abstract's summary is that samples with high total fluorine and low measured PFAS may contain volatile PFAS, PFAS polymers, newer replacement PFAS, or other fluorinated compounds.

Read the study as a 2014–2015 snapshot of fluorine in U.S. fast-food packaging, with named PFAS confirmed in a 20-sample subset. It is not a 2026 shelf survey, and it is not a result for one restaurant chain. The paper also says the team asked chains about fluorinated packaging and got substantive answers from only two. Both said they believed or had verified that the packaging did not contain fluorinated chemicals or PFAS, and the researchers still found fluorinated food-contact papers from those chains. The authors treat that as a sign of limited knowledge in the industry at the time, and they say it is hard to conclude much from two replies.

The same paper says PFAS in grease-resistant food packaging can leach into food and increase dietary exposure. It does not estimate a dose from one slice of pizza.

## What did the FDA announce about grease-proofing PFAS?

On February 28, 2024, the FDA announced that grease-proofing materials containing PFAS are no longer being sold for use in food packaging in the United States. The press announcement says this means the major source of dietary exposure to PFAS from packaging such as fast-food wrappers, microwave popcorn bags, take-out paperboard containers, and pet food bags is being eliminated. The constituent update the same day says manufacturers are no longer selling those substances for food-contact use in the U.S. market, and that the voluntary phase-out of the substances used on food-packaging paper and paperboard eliminates the primary source of dietary exposure to PFAS from authorized food-contact uses.

The market phase-out page puts that announcement at the end of a longer sequence.

**Long-chain grease-proofers left first.** In the early 2000s the FDA raised safety questions about PFAS with eight or more carbon atoms, often called C8 or long-chain compounds. The most common types named on that page are PFOA and PFOS. Through voluntary withdrawals and FDA revocations, those long-chain grease-proofers stopped being used for U.S. food contact between 2011 and 2016. In 2016 the FDA revoked the regulations authorizing the remaining uses in food packaging. The FDA says that as of November 2016, long-chain PFAS are no longer used in food-contact applications sold in the United States.

**Short-chain replacements, including 6:2 FTOH, were phased out later.** Short-chain PFAS were used after the long-chain products stopped being sold. In 2020, after a review of data on 6:2 fluorotelomer alcohol, the FDA obtained phase-out commitments from manufacturers of grease-proofing agents that contain that substance. The page says four manufacturers held 15 food-contact notifications for 11 short-chain PFAS that may contain 6:2 FTOH. One manufacturer had already told the FDA in 2019 that it had stopped sales of its food-contact substances that may contain 6:2 FTOH. The three others agreed in July 2020 to a three-year phase-out of sales for U.S. food-contact use, beginning in January 2021. The FDA anticipated that existing stocks of products containing those substances might not be exhausted until June 2025.

**The remaining authorized grease-proofers stopped for business reasons.** In 2023 the FDA received confirmation that manufacturers of all remaining authorized PFAS grease-proofers had ceased selling them for U.S. food-contact use. The FDA says those products were not part of the 2020 commitments because the agency did not have safety concerns for that remaining group.

PFAS authorized for food contact fall into four groups on the FDA's authorized-uses page: nonstick coatings, sealing gaskets for food-processing equipment, manufacturing aids, and grease-proofing agents for paper food packaging. The FDA says current data indicate that only the paper and paperboard agents would result in dietary exposure that may raise a potential safety concern. On those papers, PFAS are attached as smaller side chains. The FDA says that under certain conditions a side chain can detach, so PFAS can migrate into food. The other three uses, the FDA says, leave negligible amounts that can migrate. Cookware is a separate question, covered in the [PFAS-free cookware comparison](/post/best-pfas-free-cookware-nonstick-pan-alternatives-compared).

The February 28, 2024 announcement is about grease-proofing on paper and paperboard. It is not a finding that every PFAS use that can touch food has ended.

## Does the phase-out mean every takeout box was tested?

No. On January 3, 2025, the FDA announced a Federal Register determination that 35 food-contact notifications for PFAS grease-proofing on paper and paperboard are no longer effective. The reason is abandonment: the manufacturers or suppliers had ceased production, supply, or use of the food-contact substances for the intended use. The notifications had authorized grease-proofing coatings applied to paper and paperboard packaging to prevent leaking of oil and water. They are no longer effective as of January 6, 2025.

The FDA set a compliance date of June 30, 2025 for certain paper food packaging produced, supplied, or used before January 6, 2025, so existing stocks could be exhausted. The Federal Register notice applies that compliance date to the substances listed in its second table, for articles made with those substances before the effective date. This guide is dated after June 30, 2025. Passing that date means the exhaustion window the FDA set for those stocks has closed. It does not mean a laboratory tested every pizza box, wrapper, or imported package.

The FDA says it has developed a screening method to detect these grease-proofing agents in paper and paperboard and to monitor the market. The February 2024 update had described that work as moving toward a validated analytical method. Monitoring is a program. It is not a result printed for the container from one order.

Read the dates in order. Sales of the grease-proofing substances had stopped. Commitment letters had allowed time for supply already in the chain to run out. The notifications then ceased to be effective, with a later compliance date for certain packaging already made. A stop in sales, a lag while stocks are exhausted, and a screening program are three different things. Older stock can sit past a sales stop. Packaging made outside that U.S. sales chain is not answered by a sentence about what manufacturers stopped selling into the U.S. market. The pages cited here do not describe a recall.

## Does "PFOA-free" mean takeout packaging is PFAS-free?

No. PFOA is one chemical. The FDA's market phase-out page treats PFOA as a common long-chain, or C8, compound. Those long-chain grease-proofers were out of U.S. food-contact use as of November 2016. Short-chain PFAS grease-proofers, including substances that can contain 6:2 FTOH, were still authorized after that, until the later commitments and the 2023 cessation of the remaining products.

A package that says "PFOA-free" is talking about PFOA. It can be true of paper that used a different PFAS grease-proofer, or of paper that was never fluorinated. PFAS is the family. When the question is the family, look for the word PFAS. A maker's PFAS-free sentence counts for the product it names, and it is that maker's statement unless a cited lab report is attached. This guide does not treat a missing ingredient line as a test.

## Does "compostable" mean the package is PFAS-free?

No. Compostable describes whether a material is claimed to break down in a compost system. It does not, by itself, report a PFAS result.

The 2017 Schaider paper says PFAS should be considered incompatible with compostable food packaging because they persist. It also says that, at the time of that paper, ASTM compostability standards D6400 and D6868 did not consider PFAS, so PFAS were allowed in compostable foodware under those standards. That sentence is about 2017. It is not a description of every certifier's rules in 2026.

The Biodegradable Products Institute, a North American compostability certifier, added its own fluorine rule effective January 1, 2020. BPI's fluorinated-chemicals page says organic fluorinated chemicals, including PFAS, cannot be present in formulations for BPI-certified items. The rule requires safety data sheets showing the formula does not contain fluorinated chemicals, test results from a BPI-approved lab showing a maximum of 100 ppm total fluorine, and a manufacturer-signed statement of no intentionally added fluorinated chemicals. BPI's FAQ says ASTM D6400 and D6868 deal largely with biodegradation, disintegration, heavy metals, and ecotoxicity, and that BPI's fluorine rules go beyond those standards. The same FAQ answers "Are BPI-certified items PFAS-free?" by stating those three requirements. A 100 ppm total-fluorine cap plus a no-intentional-addition statement is not the same thing as a non-detect for every PFAS.

A molded-fiber bowl or a pizza liner that only says "compostable" has not shown you the BPI mark, the fluorine test, or a PFAS-free sentence. Treat the word as a composting claim. Read any PFAS sentence separately.

## What do agencies say about health effects?

The FDA's February 28, 2024 press announcement says exposure to some types of PFAS has been linked to serious health effects. The Schaider abstract says some PFAS have been associated with cancer, developmental toxicity, immunotoxicity, and other health effects, and that PFAS in grease-resistant packaging can leach into food and increase dietary exposure. The FDA says the paper and paperboard grease-proofers are the authorized food-contact use for which current data indicated dietary exposure that may raise a potential safety concern, because side chains can detach and migrate.

Those sentences are why the phase-out exists. They are not a measured dose from one takeout meal, and they are not medical advice. The FDA also says sensitive methods can find PFAS that were not intentionally added, including at levels it says would not be a health concern. A detect, a phase-out, and a personal exposure are three different measurements.

## How should you handle takeout and leftovers?

Move food out of the grease-proof paper before you store it or reheat it. The practical swap is glass, not a different paper slogan.

1. Transfer pizza, fries, sandwiches, and other greasy takeout onto a plate or into a glass container before the microwave or the oven.
2. Take plastic lids off glass before reheating. The glass listing below says the lids are not microwave or oven safe.
3. Prefer a food-contact paper whose own listing says PFAS-free, and read that sentence as the maker's claim unless a report is attached.
4. Do not read "compostable," "PFOA-free," "unbleached," or "chlorine-free" as a PFAS test. Unbleached and chlorine-free describe the sheet. They answer a different question, which the [parchment guide](/post/is-parchment-paper-pfas-free-safer-food-storage-and-baking-swaps) covers for baking paper.
5. Use a beeswax wrap on a bowl or around cooled bread, cheese, or produce. It is not a hot pizza liner.
6. Use reusable produce bags for fruits and vegetables. They are not a container for hot, greasy leftovers.

## Frequently asked questions

### Did the FDA say pizza boxes were tested and found free of PFAS?

No. On February 28, 2024, the FDA said grease-proofing materials containing PFAS are no longer being sold for U.S. food packaging, including take-out paperboard containers. A pizza box is that kind of paperboard. The announcement is about sales of the grease-proofing substances. It is not a lab test of every box.

### Are those 35 food-contact notifications still in effect?

No. The FDA said they are no longer effective as of January 6, 2025, because the manufacturers or suppliers had ceased production, supply, or use. The notifications had authorized PFAS grease-proofing coatings on paper and paperboard to prevent leaking of oil and water. A compliance date of June 30, 2025 applied to certain paper packaging produced, supplied, or used before January 6, 2025.

### Did the 2017 study find fluorine in most pizza boxes?

It did not report a pizza-box-only share. Schaider and colleagues found total fluorine above 16 nmol/cm² in 20% of 80 food-contact paperboard samples, a category they illustrate with fry and pizza boxes, and in 46% of 248 food-contact papers. Overall, 33% of 407 samples were above that line. The samples were collected in 2014 and 2015, and the paper says the rates are not necessarily representative of the marketplace.

### Is a total-fluorine result the same as a named PFAS?

No. The Schaider screen measured total fluorine. Named PFAS were measured in a 20-sample subset. The FDA says total-fluorine methods only measure fluorine and cannot tell PFAS from other fluorine-containing substances. A high fluorine result is a reason to ask what was added. It is not, by itself, a list of chemicals.

### Does "PFOA-free" on food packaging mean it is PFAS-free?

No. PFOA is one long-chain PFAS. The FDA says long-chain grease-proofers, including PFOA, were no longer used in U.S. food-contact applications as of November 2016. Other PFAS grease-proofers remained in use until the later phase-out. A PFOA-free line answers PFOA.

### Does compostable takeout packaging mean it is free of PFAS?

No. Compostable is a breakdown claim. The 2017 paper said the ASTM compostability standards it cited did not consider PFAS. BPI-certified products, since January 1, 2020, must meet a separate rule: no intentionally added fluorinated chemicals, a maximum of 100 ppm total fluorine, and a signed statement. A package that only says compostable has not shown you that rule.

### Should you reheat takeout in the paper box or wrapper?

Move the food into glass first. The FDA says that, under certain conditions, a grease-proofer side chain can detach and PFAS can migrate into food. Heating the meal in the takeout paper keeps the food against that paper. The Razab listing says the glass is microwave and oven safe and the lids are not. Take the lid off before reheating.

### Do beeswax wraps replace a grease-proof pizza box?

No. The Bee's Wrap listing describes a washable roll of beeswax, organic plant oils, and tree resin for covering food. Use it on a bowl or around cooled bread, cheese, or produce. It does not say PFAS-free, and it is not a hot takeout liner.

### Does the phase-out cover microwave popcorn bags and parchment too?

Microwave popcorn bags are one of the packages the FDA named for the same grease-proofing substances. That history is in the [popcorn guide](/post/is-microwave-popcorn-safe-pfas-in-bags-safer-popping-swaps). Retail baking parchment is a different product. The FDA pages cited here do not put it on the grease-proofer list, and they also do not certify every roll. That distinction is in the [parchment guide](/post/is-parchment-paper-pfas-free-safer-food-storage-and-baking-swaps).

PFAS grease-proofing was used on fast-food wrappers and take-out paperboard, including the boxes people call pizza boxes, and those substances are no longer being sold for U.S. food packaging. The 2017 Schaider study found total fluorine in 46% of food-contact papers and 20% of food-contact paperboard collected in 2014 and 2015. "PFOA-free" does not mean PFAS-free, and "compostable" does not mean a PFAS test. Move greasy takeout into glass before you reheat it.
