**Quick answer:** Some sunscreens have contained fluorinated ingredients, and a label that never says PFAS is not a lab test. In a May 31, 2022 University of Colorado Anschutz interview, pharmacy researchers Jed Lampe and Rebecca McCullough told shoppers to look for polyfluoroalkyl phosphate esters, which they abbreviated PAP, and for polytetrafluoroethylene, which the published interview spells "PFTE." McCullough said any ingredient word containing "fluoro" is one to look out for, and that water-resistant formulas tend to have more of those fluorinated chemicals. Lampe said a sunscreen that contains them is still better than no sunscreen. A 2022 study mixed a labeled form of PFOA into sunscreen and applied it once to one man. A fraction of that dose reached his blood. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration's December 29, 2025 report on PFAS in cosmetics does not discuss sunscreen, and it says no federal rule bans intentionally added PFAS in cosmetics. A sunscreen sold to help prevent sunburn is an over-the-counter drug. Washington's cosmetics law, as the state Department of Ecology explains it, still restricts the non-drug ingredients in a sunscreen. Minnesota's statute says its January 1, 2025 prohibition does not apply to an FDA-regulated drug. Use a sunscreen. Read the Drug Facts panel before you treat a bottle as a swap.

Below is what those interviews, papers, the FDA orders, and the state texts actually say, plus three Amazon listings whose printed ingredients do not include a fluorinated name. Makeup is a separate guide, [waterproof mascara and long-wear foundation](/post/does-makeup-contain-pfas-pfas-free-makeup-swaps).

## Does sunscreen contain PFAS?

Some of the sunscreens that have been studied did. A bottle on a shelf in 2026 is not one of those samples unless a source names it.

PFAS, per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, are a large family of fluorinated chemicals. The state statutes checked below define the class as fluorinated organic chemicals containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom. On a sunscreen, the uses these sources describe are not the ultraviolet filter itself. Lampe named PAP and polytetrafluoroethylene as ingredients a shopper who wants to limit PFAS would avoid. The FDA's cosmetics report describes PTFE, the same polymer, as a bulking agent and slip modifier in cosmetics. A zinc oxide or titanium dioxide filter and a fluorinated film-former are different lines on a label. A product can have one, both, or neither.

The record this guide can check is the Colorado interview, two peer-reviewed exposure papers, the FDA's 2025 cosmetics report, the current sunscreen monograph and the proposed order that would amend it, four state statutes, and the ingredient lists on three listings. It is not a census of every sunscreen in a store.

Wear the sunscreen you have if the alternative is bare skin in the sun. Lampe's sentence in that interview is the safety line in his own words: even a sunscreen that has some of these fluoro compounds in it is probably better than no sunscreen at all. The FDA's sunscreen questions-and-answers page says the same thing in sun-protection terms. Broad spectrum sunscreens with an SPF of at least 15 are one part of a strategy that also includes clothing that covers the arms, torso, and legs, a hat, sunglasses, and shade. The page says the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the American Academy of Dermatology, and major physicians' associations endorse similar recommendations. Chemical questions come after that.

## What did University of Colorado Anschutz researchers say to look for?

PAP, polytetrafluoroethylene, and any ingredient name that contains the syllable fluoro. Those are the shopping words in the interview. They are not a measured share of the U.S. market.

The page is "Q&A: Experts Weigh in on Sunscreen Ingredients to Avoid," by Matthew Hastings, published May 31, 2022, by the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus. The university says the interview was edited and condensed. Lampe and McCullough are pharmacy researchers at the Skaggs School of Pharmacy and Pharmaceutical Sciences.

Asked which ingredients to avoid, Lampe named "PAP" (polyfluoroalkyl phosphate esters) and "PFTE" (polytetrafluoroethylene). The chemical name in the parentheses is polytetrafluoroethylene. The FDA's cosmetics report uses the INCI name PTFE for that polymer. McCullough said any word that has "fluoro" in it is related to ingredients to look out for. Lampe added that sunscreens that contain these compounds have them at very low levels, and that a health risk from getting one by mistake is likely to be low. He also said the long-term health effects of low-level PFAS exposure are not understood, and that more research is needed on the formulas that have PFAS in them.

McCullough said she considers mineral sunscreens, the ones that use titanium dioxide and zinc oxide, probably the safest of the types she described. She said those oxides are not considered toxic and are described as very safe by the FDA. That is her summary of the agency, in an edited interview. The FDA documents below are the ones this guide uses for what the agency has actually proposed and what the current order allows. Lampe said mineral sunscreens are very efficacious, and that zinc should be kept out of children's eyes.

On sprays, McCullough said she prefers lotions because she has missed skin and failed to rub a spray in. Lampe said creams are more effective, that aerosols often do not give full coverage, and that he worries about what a child breathes in if a spray is used several times a day. A spray can still be an FDA-legal dosage form. The preference in the interview is about missed skin and inhalation, not a finding that every spray contains PFAS.

## Do water-resistant formulas have more fluorinated ingredients?

That is what McCullough said. She did not publish a count of products in that interview.

Her sentence is that water-resistant formulations tend to have more of the fluorinated chemicals she and Lampe had just named, and that this is a flag for someone who wants to limit exposure. She also said no sunscreen lasts forever, and that reapplication is the protection step. Her interval was every two hours. The FDA labeling order, below, requires that same interval on the Drug Facts panel. A tendency described by two researchers in 2022 is not a test of a bottle bought today. A listing can say "water resistant (80 minutes)" and still print an inactive list with no fluoro name. The three listings later in this guide are in that second group. The interview is why the phrase is worth reading. It is not a verdict on those three.

Lampe also said that if a sunscreen does contain fluoro compounds, using it is probably better than using none. He told the university that Colorado has the highest incidence of skin cancer of any state. That ranking is his statement in the interview. This guide did not rebuild a state-by-state skin-cancer table.

## What did the human sunscreen absorption study measure?

It measured labeled PFOA that the researchers mixed into a sunscreen and spread on one man. It did not measure the PFOA that was already in sunscreens on a store shelf.

The paper is Klaus Abraham and Bernhard H. Monien, "Transdermal absorption of 13C4-perfluorooctanoic acid (13C4-PFOA) from a sunscreen in a male volunteer," *Environment International*, volume 169, November 2022, article 107549. The dose was 110 micrograms of 13C4-PFOA, dried from methanol, redissolved in ethanol, and mixed into 30 grams of a commercial sunscreen. The paper states the resulting concentration as 3.7 micrograms per gram. The volunteer, who is the paper's first author, was 66 years old and weighed 80 kilograms. He applied the mix to his whole skin after a morning shower, stayed undressed for four hours, and showered again 48 hours later. Blood was drawn for 115 days.

Plasma levels of the label were 3 ng/L at 6 hours, 56 ng/L at 3 days, and 118 ng/L at 10 days. The maximum was 132 ng/L at 22 days. The paper says that peak was 9.4 percent of his background PFOA, which was 1,400 ng/L. Levels then fell slightly. The paper estimates a half-life of 1.8 years for the labeled compound in this person. Using a volume of distribution of 0.17 liters per kilogram, the authors' best estimate is that 1.6 percent of the applied dose reached the body. If they assume the label stayed in blood only, the estimate is 0.38 percent. If they assume the blood concentration was the concentration everywhere in an 80-kilogram body, the estimate is 5.6 percent. The 1.6 percent figure is the one they call the best estimate. It is still an estimate from one person and one application.

The paper is explicit that the PFOA was mixed in. The 3.7 micrograms per gram was chosen because it was comparable to the highest PFOA the authors say Fujii and colleagues reported in a sunscreen in 2013, 5.7 micrograms per gram. Abraham and Monien also summarize that 2013 paper: 24 samples were chosen because their labels listed fluorinated compounds, for example PAP. Twenty-one contained perfluoroalkyl carboxylic acids, including 8 of 9 sunscreens. Total perfluoroalkyl carboxylic acids went up to 19.0 micrograms per gram in the sunscreens. Those are Abraham and Monien's figures for a set of products that already listed a fluorinated ingredient. This guide did not re-analyze Fujii's tables. A sample selected because the label said PAP is not a random sunscreen.

The same introduction describes a Norway diary study, Thépaut and colleagues 2021, in which sunscreen use in women was positively associated with several PFAS in the body, including 6:2 diPAP. An association in a diary study is not a measurement of the sunscreen, and it is not the volunteer experiment. The volunteer experiment is the part that shows labeled PFOA, placed in a sunscreen and left on skin, can cross into human blood. One man, a spiked dose, and a 48-hour wait before the next shower are the boundaries of that sentence.

## What did the mouse and PBTK sunscreen study measure?

It built a model of repeated sunscreen application in mice. It did not measure a person's blood after a beach day.

The paper is Qiaoying Chen, Shujun Yi, Yumeng Sun, Yumin Zhu, Kaiyuan Ma, and Lingyan Zhu, "Contribution of Continued Dermal Exposure of PFAS-Containing Sunscreens to Internal Exposure," *Environmental Science & Technology*, published online August 19, 2024. The abstract says PFAS are widely present in sunscreen products as active ingredients or as impurities, and that they may penetrate skin. It does not name brands, and it does not give a percentage of products on the market.

The lab made ten sunscreens, water-in-oil and oil-in-water, and ran an in vitro skin-penetration test to see how four sunscreen ingredients changed PFAS penetration. The abstract does not name those four ingredients. Two formulas, one of each type, that increased PFAS absorption the most were then used for a 30-day exposure in mice. The physiologically based toxicokinetic models fit the mouse tissue concentrations with R-squared values of 0.885 to 0.947. A second 30-day mouse experiment used two commercially available sunscreens that contained PFAS. Those validation fits were R-squared 0.809 to 0.835. The abstract's practical result is that putting the same amount of sunscreen on a larger area of skin lets more PFAS through. The authors say that argues for caution in real use, especially in summer.

That result is a reason to read the ingredient list. It is not a reason to use less sunscreen than the label says, or to leave skin uncovered. The FDA order below tells a label to say "apply liberally" or "apply generously," 15 minutes before sun exposure, and to reapply at least every two hours. Coverage is the sun-protection step. The formula is the PFAS step.

## What did the FDA's 2025 cosmetics report say, and does it cover sunscreen?

The report counted PFAS that companies listed as intentional ingredients in cosmetic products. This guide searched the report for the word sunscreen and did not find it.

The document is "Report on the Use of PFAS in Cosmetic Products and Associated Risks," published December 29, 2025. Section 3506 of the Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act of 2022 required it. The scope sentence limits the report to PFAS intentionally added as ingredients. Contaminants, impurities, and breakdown products are outside that scope. As of August 30, 2024, listing data showed 51 PFAS in 1,744 formulations, 0.41 percent of cosmetic products registered that month. The top categories were eye shadows, leave-on face and neck products, eyeliners, face powders, and foundations. PTFE was the most frequently listed, in 490 products, 28.1 percent of the PFAS-containing formulations. The press release the same day said toxicological data for a majority of these PFAS are incomplete or unavailable.

The report reviewed the 25 most frequently used PFAS, which it says account for over 96 percent of PFAS use in the listings. For 19 of the 25, safety could not be definitively determined because critical toxicology data were missing. Five were judged a low safety concern under the intended conditions of use: PTFE, perfluorodecalin, HC Yellow No. 13, perfluorohexane, and tetrafluoropropene. One ingredient, perfluorohexylethyl triethoxysilane, was flagged as a potential concern at the highest use level in body lotion. The report says dermal contact is the expected main route for cosmetics, because most cosmetics are applied to skin. It also says there are currently no federal regulations that specifically address PFAS in cosmetic products, and that intentionally added PFAS are not currently prohibited and do not, by presence alone, make a cosmetic adulterated or misbranded.

A sunscreen is not automatically one of those cosmetic listings. The FDA's page "Is It a Cosmetic, a Drug, or Both?" says a product is a drug if it is intended to treat or prevent disease or to affect the structure or function of the body. Preventing sunburn is that kind of intended use, which is why sunscreen is regulated as an over-the-counter drug. The same page says a product can be both. Its examples of cosmetic/drug combinations include moisturizers and makeup marketed with sun-protection claims. A tinted moisturizer sold to beautify and to prevent sunburn has to meet both sets of rules. A sunscreen whose purpose on the label is sun protection is a drug under that page. The 0.41 percent figure is a share of registered cosmetic products. It is not a share of sunscreens.

## What does federal sunscreen law actually require?

The current marketing rules are in Over-the-Counter Monograph M020, the deemed final order posted September 24, 2021. A proposed order from the same day would change several of those rules. The proposed order is not in effect.

The deemed final order treats 16 sunscreen active ingredients as generally recognized as safe and effective when the product also meets the rest of the order. Zinc oxide is listed up to 25 percent. Titanium dioxide is listed up to 25 percent. The other actives on that list include avobenzone, homosalate, octinoxate, octisalate, octocrylene, and oxybenzone. The FDA's questions-and-answers page says sunscreens that conform to the deemed final order are deemed GRASE and are not new drugs.

The proposed order, which the same page says is substantively the same as a 2019 proposed rule, would treat zinc oxide and titanium dioxide as GRASE, would treat aminobenzoic acid and trolamine salicylate as not GRASE because of safety concerns, and would treat the other twelve listed actives as not GRASE because more data are needed. The page says the proposal is not a conclusion that those twelve are unsafe. It says the proposed order does not require any sunscreen to be removed from the market. Until a final order changes the deemed final order, a sunscreen marketed without an approved application has to follow the deemed final order.

The labeling rules are the practical part. For every sunscreen, the directions say to apply liberally or generously 15 minutes before sun exposure, and they say children under 6 months of age should be a question for a doctor. A product that passes the water-resistance test says to reapply after 40 minutes or after 80 minutes of swimming or sweating, immediately after towel drying, and at least every 2 hours. A product that does not pass that test says to reapply at least every 2 hours and to use a water-resistant sunscreen if swimming or sweating. The order says the claims "sunblock," "sweatproof," and "waterproof" are false or misleading and make the product misbranded. "Water resistant," with a tested time of 40 or 80 minutes, is the claim the order allows. Colorado's health department tells shoppers to be careful with "waterproof." The FDA's word for that claim is misbranded.

Drug Facts labeling, in 21 CFR 201.66, is where the names sit. Active ingredients are listed with the proportion of each. Inactive ingredients are listed by established name. If the sunscreen is not also a cosmetic, those inactive names go in alphabetical order. If it is also a cosmetic, the cosmetic listing rules apply, and the Drug Facts rules control when the two conflict. The FDA's labeling guidance says that in a product with both drug and cosmetic ingredients, the drug ingredients are the active ingredients and the cosmetic ingredients are the inactive ingredients. PTFE or a PAP, if a company puts one in and lists it, belongs on that inactive list. A missing name is not a fluorine test. The makeup guide covers why a cosmetics screen found fluorine in products that did not list a PFAS. That paper did not test sunscreens.

## Which state laws restrict PFAS in cosmetics, and do they cover sunscreen?

The FDA report's footnote, dated as of May 15, 2025, says eleven states had passed class-wide bans on intentionally added PFAS in cosmetics, with effective dates from 2025 through 2028: California, Colorado, Connecticut, Maine, Minnesota, New Hampshire, New Mexico, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont, and Washington. The footnote's dates are January 1, 2025 for California, Colorado, Minnesota, and Washington; January 1, 2026 for Maine and Vermont; January 1, 2027 for New Hampshire, Oregon, and Rhode Island; and January 1, 2028 for New Mexico and Connecticut. It also says Connecticut can apply earlier, on January 1, 2026, unless the manufacturer notifies the state and meets the statute's other conditions. This guide read the California, Colorado, Minnesota, and Washington texts for the sunscreen question. It did not re-read the other seven for an over-the-counter exemption.

**Washington.** Revised Code of Washington section 70A.560.020 says that beginning January 1, 2025, no person may manufacture, knowingly sell, offer for sale, distribute for sale, or distribute for use in the state a cosmetic product that contains intentionally added PFAS. Subsection (7)(a) says cosmetic products do not include prescription drugs approved by the FDA. Subsection (7)(b) says the restricted chemicals are restricted in cosmetics even when the product also contains drug ingredients regulated by the FDA, and that the ingredients regulated as drugs are not themselves subject to the restriction. An in-state retailer that already had stock may sell it through January 1, 2026. The Washington Department of Ecology's Toxic-Free Cosmetics Act page uses sunscreen as the example: for cosmetics such as sunscreens that include an FDA-regulated drug ingredient, the other ingredients must still meet the law. The same page says over-the-counter medications such as vitamins and mineral tablets are not cosmetics, and that the law does not apply to chemicals in packaging or to FDA-regulated drug ingredients. Zinc oxide is the drug ingredient. A PAP or PTFE in the rest of the formula is the kind of other ingredient that sentence is about. Ecology also says its definition of "intentionally added" takes effect January 1, 2027.

**Minnesota.** Minnesota Statutes section 116.943, subdivision 5, says that beginning January 1, 2025, a person may not sell or distribute in the state a listed product if it contains intentionally added PFAS. Cosmetics are item 4. The cosmetic definition excludes soap and covers articles intended to be applied for cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness, or altering appearance, including a component. It does not name sunscreen. Subdivision 8(b) says subdivisions 4 and 5 do not apply to a product that is a medical device or a drug regulated by the FDA. A sunscreen marketed to help prevent sunburn is a drug under the FDA page cited above, and the deemed final order is how that drug is regulated. The exemption's words cover that product. They do not cover a cosmetic that makes no drug claim. The electronic-component exception in subdivision 5 is a different sentence, and it is not a sunscreen rule.

**California.** Health and Safety Code section 108981.5 says that beginning January 1, 2025, no person or entity shall manufacture, sell, deliver, hold, or offer for sale any cosmetic product that contains intentionally added PFAS. Section 108982 defines a cosmetic product as an article for retail sale or professional use intended to be applied to the body for cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness, or altering the appearance. PFAS means a class of fluorinated organic chemicals containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom. Intentionally added means PFAS a manufacturer added for a functional or technical effect, or PFAS that are intentional breakdown products of an added chemical. The definition does not mention sunscreen. It does not exclude over-the-counter drugs, and it does not contain Washington's sentence separating drug ingredients from the rest of the formula. This guide does not turn that silence into a ruling that every sunscreen is covered or that every sunscreen is exempt.

**Colorado.** Section 25-15-604(3) says that on and after January 1, 2025, a person shall not sell or distribute cosmetics that contain intentionally added PFAS chemicals. Section 25-15-603 defines a cosmetic to include a skin moisturizer, perfume, lipstick, nail polish, eye or facial makeup, shampoo, conditioner, permanent wave, hair dye, and deodorant. It excludes a product that requires a prescription. Until January 1, 2027, it also excludes hydrofluoroolefins used as propellants in cosmetics. It does not use the words sunscreen or over-the-counter. A separate definition, "product," excludes drugs approved or authorized by the FDA. The sale ban says "products" and then names cosmetics. The Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment's consumer-products page says PFAS may be found in makeup and personal care products such as foundation, moisturizer, mascara, sunscreen, and lipstick. It tells shoppers to be careful with labels that say wear-resistant, long-lasting, or waterproof, and with the ingredient PTFE. It also says to look for "fluoro," "PTFE," or "nonstick" on ingredient lists. That page does not say the sale ban covers or exempts an over-the-counter sunscreen drug. This guide stops at those sentences.

## How do you read a sunscreen ingredient label?

Start with the Drug Facts panel, then match any fluoro name to the words the Colorado researchers and the FDA report actually use. Match a company claim to the bottle, not to the category.

### Label checks, in order

1. Read "Active ingredients" and "Inactive ingredients." The actives are the ultraviolet filters, with a percentage. Zinc oxide and titanium dioxide, each up to 25 percent, are the mineral filters on the current order. The inactives are everything else the label has to name. A mineral active does not answer the inactive list.

2. In both lists, look for PTFE, polytetrafluoroethylene, polyfluoroalkyl phosphate, PAP, and the stems perfluoro and polyfluoro. McCullough's rule is broader: any ingredient word containing fluoro. The Colorado health department prints the same "fluoro" and "PTFE" prompt, and it adds wear-resistant, long-lasting, and waterproof as label words to be careful with. "Waterproof" is also a claim the FDA order calls misbranding. "Water resistant (40 minutes)" or "water resistant (80 minutes)" is the tested claim. McCullough said those water-resistant formulas tend to carry more fluorinated ingredients. Read the inactive list anyway.

3. Do not stop at "oxybenzone-free" or "octinoxate-free." Those are ultraviolet filters. They are not PTFE, and they are not PAP. A reef-safe sentence on a listing is about the filters the listing names. It is not a fluorine test.

4. A company page that says "we never use PFAS" would be the company's claim. None of the three listings below says PFAS-free. What this guide checked is the ingredient names on the Amazon listing, and on a Drug Facts label or a brand page for that same product. No fluorinated name appears on those lists. That is a label check. It is not a total-fluorine test of the tube.

5. Keep the sun-protection directions. Apply liberally or generously 15 minutes before you go out. Reapply at least every two hours, and follow the 40-minute or 80-minute line if the label has one. Cover skin with clothing, a hat, and shade when you can. For a baby under 6 months, the Drug Facts line is to ask a doctor. Lampe's separate comment was about children under 7: he would use a kid-specific product and try to avoid the PFAS compounds, because a smaller body gets a larger dose from the same exposure. Those are two different age lines. Neither one says to skip sunscreen.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should you skip sunscreen to avoid PFAS?

No. Lampe told University of Colorado Anschutz that a sunscreen containing fluoro compounds is probably better than no sunscreen. The FDA's sunscreen questions page says broad spectrum SPF 15 or higher is one part of sun protection, with clothing, a hat, sunglasses, and shade, and that its proposal on twelve chemical filters is not a finding that those filters are unsafe. Read the inactive list. Do not leave skin bare in order to dodge a fluoro name.

### Does "water resistant" mean the sunscreen contains PFAS?

Not by itself. McCullough said water-resistant formulas tend to have more fluorinated ingredients. The FDA order allows "water resistant" only with a tested time of 40 or 80 minutes, and it calls "waterproof" misbranding. The three listings in this guide claim 80 minutes and print inactive ingredients with no fluoro name. A tendency is not a result for those three, and a missing name is not a lab test.

### Did the 2022 volunteer study find PFOA in ordinary sunscreen?

It found labeled PFOA that the researchers had mixed in. Abraham and Monien put 110 micrograms of 13C4-PFOA into 30 grams of sunscreen, left it on one man's skin, and measured a peak of 132 ng/L in plasma at day 22. Their best estimate of absorption was 1.6 percent of that dose. The paper's summary of an older study is the part about sunscreens that already listed fluorinated ingredients. Those are different facts.

### Does the FDA's 2025 report say how many sunscreens contain PFAS?

No. The report does not mention sunscreen. It found 51 PFAS listed as intentional ingredients in 1,744 cosmetic formulations, 0.41 percent of products registered as of August 30, 2024, and PTFE in 490 of those formulations. A sunscreen sold to prevent sunburn is an over-the-counter drug. The report's percentage is not a sunscreen percentage.

### Are zinc oxide and titanium dioxide the only legal sunscreen filters?

No. The deemed final order still lists 16 active ingredients, including zinc oxide and titanium dioxide up to 25 percent. The proposed order would leave those two as the GRASE filters and would call twelve others short on data. The FDA says that proposal is not in effect and does not pull products off the shelf. A mineral filter is a choice a shopper can make. It is not the only filter the current order allows.

### Does Washington ban PFAS in sunscreen?

The Department of Ecology says the other ingredients in a sunscreen still have to meet the Toxic-Free Cosmetics Act. RCW 70A.560.020, beginning January 1, 2025, restricts intentionally added PFAS in cosmetics, says prescription drugs are not cosmetic products, and says an FDA-regulated drug ingredient in a cosmetic is not what the restriction covers. Ecology's example of the product that remains covered is a sunscreen. The drug filter is the part the statute sets aside.

### Does Minnesota's 2025 cosmetics ban apply to sunscreen drugs?

The statute says the prohibition does not apply to a drug regulated by the FDA. Section 116.943, subdivision 5, restricts intentionally added PFAS in cosmetics beginning January 1, 2025. Subdivision 8(b) says that prohibition does not apply to a product that is an FDA-regulated drug. A sunscreen marketed to help prevent sunburn is that kind of product. A cosmetic with no drug claim is not.

### Do California or Colorado say sunscreen is exempt?

The sections this guide read do not say that. California's cosmetic-product definition does not mention sunscreen or an over-the-counter drug. Colorado's cosmetic definition excludes a prescription product, and its product definition excludes an FDA-approved or authorized drug, and the health department lists sunscreen as a personal care product where PFAS may be found. Neither the statute nor that page says the sale ban covers, or exempts, every over-the-counter sunscreen.

### Do the Waxhead and Badger listings say the formulas are PFAS-free?

No. The Waxhead stick, ASIN B06W2NVQYM, the Badger SPF 40 tin, ASIN B08KH5DCVL, and the Badger SPF 50 cream, ASIN B0D3CJY9F8, do not say PFAS-free. Their Amazon ingredient sentences and the DailyMed labels checked for this guide name zinc oxide and non-fluorinated oils, waxes, and, for Waxhead, vanilla. An EWG transcription of a Waxhead label also lists water and ethanol. None of those names is PTFE or PAP.

## Bottom line

Sunscreen is a product to keep using. The PFAS question is which formula, not whether to put it on. A 2022 interview from University of Colorado Anschutz named PAP, polytetrafluoroethylene, and any "fluoro" ingredient, and it said water-resistant formulas tend to carry more of those chemicals. A spiked-dose study in one man showed that PFOA mixed into sunscreen can enter blood. A 2024 mouse model said spreading the same amount over more skin lets more PFAS through. The FDA's 2025 cosmetics inventory never discusses sunscreen, and the current sunscreen order still allows sixteen ultraviolet filters, including zinc oxide and titanium dioxide up to 25 percent. Washington is the state, among the four read here, that says the non-drug ingredients in a sunscreen still have to meet a PFAS restriction. If you want to avoid a listed fluorinated ingredient, read the inactive list for PTFE, PAP, and the word fluoro, ignore "waterproof," and keep the two-hour reapply line. The listings in this guide that pass that label check are a Waxhead zinc oxide stick and two Badger zinc oxide sunscreens, a tin and a lotion.
