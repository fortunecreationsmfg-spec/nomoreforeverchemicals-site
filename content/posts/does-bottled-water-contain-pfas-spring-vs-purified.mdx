**Quick answer:** Some bottled water contains PFAS, and the published U.S. tests mostly found low totals. Chow, Ojeda, Jacangelo, and Schwab at Johns Hopkins screened 101 uniquely labeled bottled waters sold in the United States for 32 PFAS. PFAS were above the method detection limits in 39 of 101 products. Where PFAS were detected, the sum was 0.17 to 18.87 nanograms per liter, with a median of 0.98 ng/L. The paper says 97% of samples were below 5 ng/L. EPA uses nanograms per liter and parts per trillion as the same unit for these limits, so those figures are 0.17 to 18.87 ppt as a sum of the PFAS the lab measured. That sum is not a PFOA result and not a PFOS result. Purified waters had significantly less PFAS than spring waters. The authors tied that gap to reverse osmosis, used in 25 of 35 purified products and in 1 of 45 spring products. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration's 2023–2024 retail test found detectable PFAS in 10 of 197 bottled waters, and none of those detects would have exceeded EPA's tap-water maximum contaminant levels. FDA's PFAS page says the agency is writing a bottled-water standard and has not finished one. For water you drink every day, read the local Consumer Confidence Report, and if you filter, look for NSF/ANSI 53 or NSF/ANSI 58 for PFAS reduction. Carry that water in a glass or stainless bottle. A bottle is a container. It does not remove PFAS from the water you pour in.

How to read a filter certificate is in the [water filters guide](/post/do-water-filters-remove-pfas-best-pfas-water-filters). The ZeroWater-specific writeup is [Does ZeroWater Filter Remove PFAS?](/post/nomoreforeverchemicals-com-newsletter).

## Does bottled water contain PFAS?

In the two large U.S. surveys below, yes for a minority of bottles, at totals that were usually under 5 ng/L in the Johns Hopkins set and under EPA's tap-water maximum contaminant levels in the FDA's 2023–2024 set. A label that says spring, purified, or mineral is a naming rule. It is not a PFAS test of that bottle.

The Johns Hopkins paper is Chow S.J., Ojeda N., Jacangelo J.G., and Schwab K.J., "Detection of ultrashort-chain and other per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) in U.S. bottled water," *Water Research*, volume 201, article 117292 (2021). The PubMed record is [34118648](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34118648/). The lab bought 101 uniquely labeled products for sale in the United States and screened them for 32 target PFAS by solid-phase extraction and liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. Fifteen of the 32 analytes were detected. PFAS were above the method detection limits in 39 of 101 products. The detected sums were 0.17 to 18.87 ng/L, median 0.98 ng/L, and 97% of samples were below 5 ng/L. The abstract says no enforceable PFAS regulations existed for U.S. bottled water, and that some products approached levels of concern. [Consumer Reports' June 17, 2021 article](https://www.consumerreports.org/health/bottled-water/pfas-in-bottled-water-new-study-finds-a1111233122/) says the study did not identify the brands. This guide does not add brand names the paper withheld.

EPA's drinking-water page states the PFOA and PFOS maximum contaminant levels as 4.0 parts per trillion, and it says parts per trillion are also expressed as ng/L. A Johns Hopkins total of 18.87 ng/L is a sum of up to 32 chemicals in one product. It is not a measurement of PFOA alone, and it is not a measurement of PFOS alone. This guide does not treat that sum as a pass or a fail against the 4.0 ppt single-chemical limits.

## How do spring water and purified water differ?

Under FDA's bottled-water identity standard, spring water is a source description and purified water is a treatment description. The Johns Hopkins comparison found less PFAS in the purified group, largely because most of those products had been treated by reverse osmosis. The word purified on a label is still not a laboratory report for the bottle in your hand.

[21 CFR 165.110(a)(2)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-165/subpart-B/section-165.110) sets the names. Spring water is water from an underground formation that flows naturally to the surface. It is collected at the spring or through a bore hole tapping the formation that feeds the spring, and the location of the spring has to be identified. Purified water, which may also be called demineralized water, is water produced by distillation, deionization, reverse osmosis, or another suitable process that meets the U.S. Pharmacopeia definition of purified water incorporated in that section. The same paragraph allows the more specific names distilled water, deionized water, and reverse osmosis water when that is the process used. Mineral water is a third name: at least 250 parts per million total dissolved solids, from a protected underground source, with no minerals added.

The Johns Hopkins abstract says purified products contained significantly less PFAS than spring products. The reason it gives is reverse osmosis in 25 of 35 purified waters, against 1 of 45 spring waters. Products that had been reverse-osmosis treated had significantly lower sums, and significantly lower long-chain, short-chain, and PFPrA concentrations, than products that had not. Ultrashort-chain PFPrA, measured in bottled water for the first time in that paper, was 42% of the detected PFAS mass and was found almost exclusively in products labeled spring water. On a mass basis, perfluorocarboxylic acids were 83% of what was detected, and short-chain perfluoroalkyl acids with five or fewer CF2 groups were 67%.

Read the label in that order. "Spring" names where the water came from. "Purified" or "reverse osmosis" names a treatment. A purified product can be distilled or deionized rather than reverse osmosis, because the regulation lists all three. The study's advantage for purified water was concentrated in the reverse-osmosis subset. One spring brand and one purified brand are not the study. The study is 101 products, and it did not print their names.

When bottled water comes from a community water system and has not been treated to meet the purified-water or sterile-water definitions, the same regulation says the label has to state "from a community water system" or "from a municipal source." The [International Bottled Water Association's April 17, 2024 statement](https://bottledwater.org/nr/bottled-water-industry-applauds-epa-efforts-to-address-pfas-in-tap-water/) says purified bottled water made from a public supply is not untreated tap water in a bottle: at the plant it is treated, including by reverse osmosis, distillation, or deionization, to meet FDA's purified-water standard. That is the trade group's description of the process. It is not a PFAS measurement.

## What did Consumer Reports find in named brands?

Consumer Reports tested 47 bottled waters in work it published in 2020: 35 noncarbonated and 12 carbonated, two to four samples of each product, for arsenic, cadmium, lead, mercury, and 30 PFAS. The [article](https://www.consumerreports.org/water-quality/whats-really-in-your-bottled-water-a5361150329/) also ran in the November 2020 magazine. Most noncarbonated products had detectable PFAS. Two exceeded 1 part per trillion total PFAS: Deer Park Natural Spring Water at 1.21 ppt, and Tourmaline Spring Sacred Living Water at 4.64 ppt. CR marked three noncarbonated products with an asterisk for no detectable PFAS: Arrowhead Natural Spring Water, Boxed Water Is Better, and Good & Gather (Target) Purified Drinking Water. Starkey Spring Water, owned by Whole Foods, appears in that chart for arsenic at 9.53 ppb. That figure is the arsenic result, not a PFAS total.

Seven carbonated products were over 1 ppt total PFAS in the chart on that page. In the order CR pairs the names with the totals, they are Perrier Natural Sparkling Mineral Water at 1.1 ppt, La Croix Natural Sparkling Water at 1.16 ppt, Canada Dry Lemon Lime Sparkling Seltzer Water at 1.24 ppt, Poland Spring Zesty Lime Sparkling Water at 1.66 ppt, Bubly Blackberry Sparkling Water at 2.24 ppt, Polar Natural Seltzer Water at 6.41 ppt, and Topo Chico Natural Mineral Water at 9.76 ppt. The other five carbonated products on the chart were Sparkling Ice Black Raspberry (not detected), Spindrift Raspberry Lime (0.19 ppt), Sanpellegrino (0.31 ppt), Dasani Black Cherry (0.37 ppt), and Schweppes Lemon Lime (0.58 ppt).

Those totals are Consumer Reports' totals for the PFAS it measured. LaCroix and Polar challenged how CR calculated the totals. Tourmaline Spring told CR its PFAS were below the levels set by the IBWA and by states. Nestlé told CR that its most recent testing for Deer Park indicated undetectable PFAS, and that its recent testing did not detect PFAS in Poland Spring and Perrier. Topo Chico, made by Coca-Cola, had the highest total in the test and said it would keep improving ahead of stricter standards. A [February 12, 2021 Consumer Reports retest](https://www.consumerreports.org/water-quality/topo-chico-cuts-pfas-levels-by-more-than-half-a4286812129/) of three new Topo Chico samples found an average of 3.9 ppt after Coca-Cola said it had upgraded filtration. That retest is a 2021 average of three samples. It is not a test of a bottle bought in 2026.

CR's 1 ppt line was a comparison point in the article, alongside the EPA health advisory of the time, which the article describes as a combined 70 ppt for two PFAS. It was not a federal maximum contaminant level. The 4.0 ppt PFOA and PFOS limits did not exist yet. A 2020 total above 1 ppt is not, by itself, a violation of the 2024 tap-water rule.

The June 17, 2021 Consumer Reports article on the Johns Hopkins paper adds three points that are not in the abstract. It says 19 brands in the study contained more than 1 ppt of PFAS. It quotes IBWA spokesperson Jill Culora saying the vast majority of those results were well below the association's 5 ppt single-compound standard, and that three samples exceeded the association's 10 ppt combined limit. And it quotes co-author Steven Chow saying the levels were generally low, and that regulators should test source water so the elevated products stay off the shelf. The brand count and the three-sample remark are CR's report of the study and of Culora. They are not sentences in the PubMed abstract. CR's scientist Michael Hansen told the magazine that the practical shopping step, for someone worried about PFAS in bottled water, is a product processed with a treatment such as reverse osmosis.

One regulatory split matters for sparkling water. Section 165.110 says the bottled-water identity and the bottled-water quality standard do not cover products declared on the label as "carbonated water," "seltzer water," "soda water," "sparkling water," or "tonic water." CR's February 2021 article says many carbonated waters are regulated as soft drinks, while a carbonated product that claims a spring or mineral source, such as Topo Chico's mineral water, falls under the bottled-water rules. The citation for the exclusion is the regulation. The Topo Chico example is CR's.

## Does the FDA limit PFAS in bottled water?

Not with a finished standard of quality, on the FDA pages reviewed for this guide. FDA has tested bottled water for PFAS. It has also said it is writing allowable levels. Those are different steps.

The [April 14, 2025 constituent update](https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates/fda-shares-testing-results-pfas-bottled-water) reports 197 domestic and imported bottled waters collected at retail in 2023 and 2024, including purified, artesian, spring, and mineral waters, tested for 18 PFAS. Ten samples had detectable PFAS. None of those levels would have exceeded EPA's maximum contaminant levels for PFAS in public drinking water. Of the ten, eight were domestic purified or spring waters, with one to four PFAS each, and two were imported artesian waters, with one or two PFAS each. FDA says four of the PFAS detected were at levels below the EPA maximum contaminant levels, and two of the PFAS detected do not have an EPA maximum contaminant level. FDA's earlier 2016 targeted survey and its Total Diet Study samples had not detected PFAS in bottled water. The [analytical-results page](https://www.fda.gov/food/environmental-contaminants-food/analytical-results-testing-food-pfas-environmental-contamination) describes the 2016 set as 30 carbonated and noncarbonated samples, none with detectable PFAS, and says that sample size cannot support a general conclusion about all bottled water. The same page's 2023–2024 summary matches the constituent update: 10 of 197 detects, none above the EPA drinking-water maximum contaminant levels.

[FDA's PFAS page](https://www.fda.gov/food/environmental-contaminants-food/and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas), reviewed for this guide on November 11, 2026, lists bottled water as a priority for a future allowable level. It says FDA sets bottled-water levels when EPA sets drinking-water levels, unless an exception applies. It notes EPA's April 2024 rule and EPA's May 2026 proposed amendments, and it says FDA is reviewing those regulations for the bottled-water standard in 21 CFR 165.110. The page describes work in progress. It does not link a final PFAS standard of quality.

The legal hook is [21 U.S.C. 349(b)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title21-section349&num=0&edition=prelim). Not later than 180 days before the effective date of an EPA national primary drinking water regulation for a contaminant, the Secretary of Health and Human Services must issue a bottled-water standard of quality for that contaminant, or find that a standard is unnecessary because the contaminant is in public-water-system water but not in water used for bottled drinking water. A bottled-water maximum contaminant level must be no less stringent than EPA's. If FDA does not issue the regulation in that period, the national primary drinking water regulation is considered the bottled-water regulation. FDA's April 14, 2025 update states that same fallback in plain language. This guide does not treat the fallback as something that has already replaced a bottled-water rule. FDA's own page still describes the bottled-water regulation as work underway. The compliance date EPA states for the 2024 tap-water maximum contaminant levels is April 26, 2029, in the May 20, 2026 proposed-rule notice. That date is a public-water-system compliance date. It is a different sentence from "bottled water is already held to 4.0 ppt."

## What is the status of EPA's tap-water limits?

EPA's April 10, 2024 national primary drinking water regulation is still the rule described on [EPA's PFAS drinking-water page](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas). It applies to public water systems. It is not a bottled-water standard. On that page, the enforceable maximum contaminant levels are 4.0 ppt for PFOA, 4.0 ppt for PFOS, 10 ppt for PFHxS, 10 ppt for PFNA, and 10 ppt for HFPO-DA, also called GenX. Mixtures of two or more of PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and PFBS have a hazard-index limit of 1. The health-based goals for PFOA and PFOS are zero. Compliance uses a running annual average. Public water systems had three years to complete initial monitoring, by 2027, and five years, by 2029, to install treatment when the averages exceed a limit. The [Federal Register notice of May 20, 2026](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/05/20/2026-10086/extending-the-compliance-deadline-for-the-pfoa-and-pfos-maximum-contaminant-levels) states the current compliance date as April 26, 2029.

On May 18, 2026, EPA announced two proposed rules. The pages for both still called them proposals when this guide was written, after the July 20, 2026 comment deadlines. The [PFOA and PFOS extension proposal](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/proposed-pfoa-and-pfos-compliance-extension-rule) would keep the 4.0 ppt limits and let a system request two more years, to April 26, 2031. The Federal Register summary says the extension is for systems that submit a request. It is not an automatic change of the date. The [rescission proposal](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/proposed-pfas-rescission-rule) would remove the drinking-water regulations for PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and the hazard-index mixture of those three plus PFBS. EPA's main PFAS page still prints all six 2024 limits and still describes both May 2026 actions as proposals. This guide does not treat either proposal as a repeal of the 4.0 ppt PFOA and PFOS limits.

The number that matters for a kitchen is the one in the local report, not the national proposal docket. [EPA requires community water systems](https://www.epa.gov/ccr/ccr-information-consumers) to deliver a Consumer Confidence Report, also called an annual drinking water quality report, to their customers. Call the water supplier or use EPA's report search. A private well does not come with that report. [EPA's home-filter page](https://www.epa.gov/cleanups/reducing-pfas-your-drinking-water-home-filter), which this guide read for the certification language below, says to start there: if PFAS have not been measured, or if they are below federal limits, a filter may not be useful.

## What does IBWA require of its members?

The International Bottled Water Association is a trade group, not the FDA. Its member standard is a condition of membership. It is not a federal maximum contaminant level, and it does not cover every bottle on a shelf.

[IBWA's April 18, 2025 statement](https://bottledwater.org/nr/per-and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas-and-bottled-water-updateresults-of-recent-shelf-test-by-fda-show-no-products-exceeded-standards/) says FDA does not have a standard of quality for PFAS in bottled water. It says that since 2019, members have had to test bottled water annually for PFAS and meet an IBWA standard of quality. After EPA's April 2024 rule, the IBWA board revised that standard, effective January 1, 2025, to include the six PFAS in the EPA rule and to keep monitoring 12 additional PFAS that are in EPA Method 537.1. The statement says the detects in FDA's 197-sample survey were below IBWA's standard and would not have exceeded EPA's tap-water maximum contaminant levels. It also repeats FDA's earlier reply to a 2019 request for a federal standard: establishing one then "would not significantly enhance FDA's mission of public health protection," based on FDA testing of 30 bottled waters with no detectable PFAS.

The April 18, 2025 statement does not reprint a numeric member limit. Consumer Reports' 2020 article and its February 2021 Topo Chico article both report the member standard as it stood then: below 5 ppt for any single PFAS compound, and 10 ppt for more than one. Culora's comments in the June 2021 article use those same figures. This guide reports 5 ppt and 10 ppt as the standard CR and IBWA's spokesperson described in 2020 and 2021. It does not assume those two numbers are still the limit for every compound after the January 1, 2025 revision, because the 2025 statement describes the revision without restating them.

IBWA's April 17, 2024 statement says member bottlers make up about 73% of the U.S. bottled-water market by volume. A non-member bottle is outside that membership rule. The absence of a membership logo is not a PFAS result either.

## What should you use instead of a single-use bottle?

Use the tap if the Consumer Confidence Report, or a well test, supports it. If the report shows PFAS you want to lower, use a filter certified for that reduction and refill a glass or stainless bottle. If you are buying water in a bottle, the Johns Hopkins result favors a label that names purification, and especially reverse osmosis, over a spring label. Neither label is a certificate for one untested lot.

EPA's home-filter page says granular activated carbon, reverse osmosis, and ion exchange can reduce PFAS, and that not every filter does. The check it names is certification to NSF/ANSI 53 or NSF/ANSI 58 for PFAS reduction, confirmed in the directory of an accredited body. It lists five of those bodies: CSA Group, IAPMO R&T, NSF, UL, and the Water Quality Association. A sentence that only says "NSF certified" can be a chlorine, lead, or materials mark. EPA says the certifications, as of April 2024, focus on PFOA and PFOS and do not yet show that a filter removes PFAS down to the drinking-water limits. Replacing the cartridge on the manufacturer's schedule is part of the result. EPA says a late replacement can increase PFAS exposure.

## Frequently asked questions

### Did the Johns Hopkins bottled-water study name brands?

No. The paper reports 101 uniquely labeled products and does not identify them in the abstract. Consumer Reports' June 17, 2021 coverage says the study did not identify which brands were tested. Brand totals in this guide come from Consumer Reports' own 2020 test and its 2021 Topo Chico retest, which are separate from the Johns Hopkins paper.

### Is purified bottled water always reverse osmosis?

No. FDA's identity standard says purified water is produced by distillation, deionization, reverse osmosis, or another suitable process, and it may be labeled with the process that was used. In the Johns Hopkins set, 25 of 35 purified products used reverse osmosis, and those treated products had significantly lower PFAS than products without reverse osmosis. A purified label is the category. The process name on that bottle is the closer detail.

### Does a spring label mean the water was tested for PFAS?

No. Spring water, in 21 CFR 165.110, is water from an underground formation that flows naturally to the surface, collected at the spring or a connected bore hole. The Johns Hopkins paper found PFPrA almost exclusively in spring-labeled products, and it found more PFAS in spring water than in purified water. A spring name does not report a PFAS concentration.

### Are Consumer Reports' 2020 totals a current recall list?

No. They are CR's totals for 47 products it bought for a 2020 investigation, two to four samples each, for 30 PFAS. LaCroix and Polar challenged the way the totals were calculated. Nestlé told CR its own testing did not detect PFAS in Deer Park, Poland Spring, and Perrier. Topo Chico's chart value was 9.76 ppt, and CR's February 2021 retest of three newer samples averaged 3.9 ppt. A bottle on a shelf in 2026 needs its own test, or the bottler's current report.

### Do EPA's 4 ppt limits already apply to bottled water?

EPA's 4.0 ppt limits are maximum contaminant levels for PFOA and PFOS at public water systems. FDA regulates bottled water. FDA's pages reviewed for this guide say the agency is writing bottled-water levels and has not published a finished PFAS standard of quality. Section 349(b) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act says that if FDA misses its deadline, the EPA regulation is considered the bottled-water regulation. FDA's April 14, 2025 update describes that fallback. It does not say the fallback has already become the bottled-water rule.

### Did EPA drop the 4 ppt tap-water limits in 2026?

The EPA pages reviewed on November 11, 2026 do not say that. The April 10, 2024 limits remain the levels printed on EPA's PFAS page, including 4.0 ppt for PFOA and 4.0 ppt for PFOS. Two May 18, 2026 actions were still proposals after the July 20, 2026 comment period: one would let systems request a compliance date of April 26, 2031 instead of April 26, 2029, and the other would rescind the limits for PFHxS, PFNA, GenX, and the hazard-index mixture. The extension proposal says the 4.0 ppt limits would stay.

### Does NSF/ANSI 53 or 58 mean the water meets 4 ppt?

EPA's home-filter page says the certifications, as of April 2024, focus on PFOA and PFOS and do not yet show removal down to the maximum contaminant levels in the drinking-water rule. Look for 53 or 58 and for the words PFOA, PFOS, or PFAS on the certificate, then confirm the model in the certifier's directory. The water filters guide compares that certification line with the filters in this directory.

### Will a glass or stainless bottle remove PFAS?

No. The Aqulea listing is a borosilicate glass bottle. The Elemental listing is a metal tumbler with a ceramic lid. Neither listing says the bottle removes PFAS, and a container does not treat the water inside it. Filter the tap, or choose a bottled water whose treatment you have checked, and use the bottle to carry that water.

## Bottom line

Bottled water is not a PFAS-free category, and the U.S. surveys that named their methods mostly found low totals. Johns Hopkins detected PFAS in 39 of 101 products, with a detected median of 0.98 ng/L and 97% of samples below 5 ng/L, and it found significantly less PFAS in purified water than in spring water because reverse osmosis was common in the purified group and rare in the spring group. FDA's 2023–2024 retail test found detects in 10 of 197 bottles, none above EPA's tap-water maximum contaminant levels, and FDA has not finished a bottled-water PFAS standard. EPA's 4.0 ppt PFOA and PFOS limits are public-tap limits. They were still the printed rule in November 2026, with two May 2026 changes still proposed. For a daily drink, start with the Consumer Confidence Report, use a filter whose certificate names PFAS under NSF/ANSI 53 or 58 if the report gives you a reason to, and carry the water in glass or stainless.
