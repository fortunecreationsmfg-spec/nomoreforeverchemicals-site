**Quick answer:** Some makeup contains PFAS, and a blank ingredient list is not proof that a product is free of them. A 2021 paper in *Environmental Science & Technology Letters* screened 231 cosmetics bought in the United States and Canada for total fluorine. High fluorine, above 0.384 micrograms of fluorine per square centimeter, was in 52% of the products, including 63% of foundations, 47% of mascaras, and 55% of lip products. A summary published the same day by Indiana University, where co-author Marta Venier works, gives the split the paper places in its supplement: 82% of 11 waterproof mascaras and 62% of 42 liquid lipsticks. Only 8% of the 231 products listed any PFAS on the label. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration's December 29, 2025 report is a different count. Companies had listed 51 PFAS as intentional ingredients in 1,744 formulations, 0.41% of cosmetic products registered as of August 30, 2024. The FDA says toxicology data are incomplete for most of those ingredients and that dermal and oral absorption data are lacking. No federal rule bans PFAS in cosmetics. California, Minnesota, Colorado, and Washington restrict intentionally added PFAS in cosmetics as of January 1, 2025. Maryland's rule, the same day, names a shorter list and does not cover the whole class.

Below is what those tests and laws actually say, how to read an ingredient list, and three Amazon listings whose brands publish a no-PFAS statement. PFAS in period products is a separate guide, [period underwear and cups](/post/does-period-underwear-contain-pfas-safer-period-product-swaps). PTFE floss is covered in the [dental floss guide](/post/does-dental-floss-contain-pfas-pfas-free-floss-swaps).

## Does makeup contain PFAS?

Some of it does. A lot of it does not say so on the label. Those are two different facts.

PFAS are a large family of fluorinated chemicals. The 2021 cosmetics paper screened for fluorine, then looked for named PFAS in a subset. The FDA's 2025 report counted PFAS that companies listed as intentional ingredients. A fluorine screen of makeup bought from 2016 to 2020 and a 2024 ingredient-listing census will not produce the same percentage. This guide keeps them apart.

The paper's authors, led by Heather D. Whitehead, with Graham F. Peaslee as corresponding author, bought or received 231 products. U.S. products came from Ulta Beauty, Sephora, Target, and Bed Bath & Beyond in Indiana and Michigan between 2016 and 2020. Canadian products were bought online in 2020 from Sephora and Shoppers Drug Mart in Toronto. The set is not a random sample of every cosmetic on sale in 2026. A brand can change a formula.

## What did the 2021 cosmetics study measure?

The paper is Whitehead and colleagues, "Fluorinated Compounds in North American Cosmetics," published online June 15, 2021, in *Environmental Science & Technology Letters*. The lab method for the full set was particle-induced gamma-ray emission (PIGE) spectroscopy. PIGE measures total fluorine from inorganic and organic sources. It does not name the chemical.

The limit of detection was 0.127 μg F/cm2. The limit of quantification was 0.384 μg F/cm2. The paper sorted products into three bins: at or below the detection limit (32%), between detection and quantification (16%), and high fluorine, above 0.384 μg F/cm2 (52%). Foundations had the highest median. Mascaras had the widest range. The categories with the highest share of high-fluorine products were foundations (63%), eye products (58%), lip products (55%), and mascaras (47%). The eight categories were lip products, eye products, foundations, face products, mascaras, concealers, eyebrow products, and miscellaneous products.

The team then measured specific PFAS in 29 products, including 20 with high total fluorine, using liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry and gas chromatography mass spectrometry. Every one of the 29 contained at least four of the 53 PFAS on the target list. One product had 13. The sums ran from 22 to 10,500 nanograms per gram of product. The abstract also says those concentrations had "an average and a median of 264 and 1050 ng/g product weights, respectively." Sorting the 29 sums printed in Table 1 puts 264 ng/g in the middle. This guide uses the range, which the abstract and the table share, and does not treat 264 as the average.

The most common finds were 6:2 and 8:2 fluorotelomer compounds: alcohols, methacrylates, and phosphate esters. In the U.S. products, 6:2 fluorotelomer alcohol was in 100% of the targeted samples. In the Canadian products it was in 94%. The paper calls these compounds precursors to perfluoroalkyl carboxylic acids. The targeted sums and the PIGE fluorine readings were not significantly correlated. The authors list three reasons that can all be true: PFAS that were not on the target list, inorganic or polymeric fluorine, and the fact that the product was not mixed before testing, or a different shade was tested.

High fluorine was described in products advertised as "wear-resistant" to water and oils or "long-lasting," including foundations, liquid lipsticks, and waterproof mascaras. The paper says those marketing words line up with why industrial literature uses PFAS in cosmetics. It does not name the brands in the main text.

The university summary says high fluorine indicates the probable presence of PFAS, and that the 29 products selected for further testing each contained at least four PFAS. That matches the paper's targeted result. It is still a 2021 product survey. It is not a test of a mascara bought today.

## Did the labels name the fluorinated ingredients?

Usually, no. Only 8% of the 231 products screened for fluorine listed any PFAS as ingredients. Only 3% of the 29 products in the targeted test listed any PFAS. The paper says U.S. law at the time had exemptions for ingredients considered proprietary, and that polymer, silane, and siloxane names in the International Nomenclature of Cosmetic Ingredients can hide a fluorinated version under a general name such as methicone or acrylate.

Two fluorine sources on labels are not the same thing as a perfluoro name. The paper lists synthetic fluorphlogopite and disteardimonium hectorite as inorganic minerals and clays. It also says mica and talc can carry inorganic fluoride, and that patents describe mica and talc treated with polyfluoroalkyl phosphate esters to make makeup last. A word with "fluoro" in it is a prompt to look harder. It is not, by itself, a PFAS identification. "Perfluoro" and "polyfluoro" are the stems Credo Beauty tells shoppers to treat as the class. PTFE, the fluoropolymer, often appears under that name or as polytetrafluoroethylene.

The FDA's current cosmetics page says a cosmetic sold at retail must list ingredients in descending order of predominance. It names PFAS that show up in listings submitted to the agency: polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), perfluorononyl dimethicone, trifluoroacetyl tripeptide-2, tetradecyl aminobutyroylvalylaminobutyric urea trifluoroacetate, perfluorohexylethyl triethoxysilane, methyl perfluorobutyl ether, and methyl perfluoroisobutyl ether. A hit on that list is a disclosed PFAS. A miss is what the 2021 paper found most of the time.

## What did the FDA's 2025 cosmetics report find?

On December 29, 2025, the FDA published "Report on the Use of PFAS in Cosmetic Products and Associated Risks." Section 3506 of the Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act of 2022 required the assessment and the public report. The press release the same day said toxicological data for most of these PFAS are incomplete or unavailable.

The report's scope is PFAS intentionally added as ingredients. It does not cover PFAS that show up only as contaminants, impurities, or breakdown products. As of August 30, 2024, listing data showed 51 PFAS in 1,744 formulations. That was 0.41% of products registered that month. The top five categories were 56% of those PFAS-containing products: eye shadows (20.5%), leave-on face and neck products (15.9%), eyeliners (8.4%), face powders (6.6%), and foundations in traditional applications (4.5%). Mascara is not in that top five. That is a count of disclosed intentional ingredients, not a fluorine screen, and the report says small businesses are exempt from listing. The 2021 study is why a low listing percentage should not be read as "the shelf is clean."

PTFE was the most frequently listed PFAS, in 490 products, 28.1% of the PFAS-containing formulations. The report cites a Cosmetic Ingredient Review assessment for use levels: PTFE up to 13% in mascara, up to 12% in eye shadow, and up to 2.4% in rinse-off hair bleach. Those are concentrations in products that use PTFE, not a share of all mascaras. Voluntary registration data, which stopped taking submissions on March 27, 2023, showed PTFE in 249 products in 2019 and 79 by March 2023. The FDA says that history cannot be rebuilt from the new mandatory listings, because those listings do not go back far enough.

The FDA reviewed the 25 most frequently used PFAS, which it says account for about 96% of PFAS use in the listings. For 19 of the 25, safety could not be determined because critical toxicology data were missing. Five were judged a low safety concern under the intended conditions of use: PTFE, perfluorodecalin, HC Yellow No. 13, perfluorohexane, and tetrafluoropropene. The Cosmetic Ingredient Review, as described in the FDA report, concluded that PTFE is safe in cosmetics in the present practices of use and concentration. One ingredient, perfluorohexylethyl triethoxysilane, was flagged as a potential safety concern at the highest use level in body lotion, with significant remaining uncertainty. The report says that assessment assumed an absorption rate because measured dermal and oral absorption data were not available. The FDA's cosmetics page states the same gap in plain language: limited data on use level, lack of dermal and oral absorption data, and a lack of dermal toxicity data.

The report also says there is no single federal definition of PFAS. For this report the FDA used the OECD definition: a fluorinated substance with at least one fully fluorinated methyl or methylene carbon. It says neither PFOA nor PFOS is intentionally added to cosmetics as an ingredient. It says they can still be present unintentionally. And it says intentionally added PFAS are not currently prohibited by federal cosmetic law and do not, by presence alone, make a product adulterated or misbranded. The FDA's footnote, dated as of May 15, 2025, says eleven states had passed class-wide bans on intentionally added PFAS in cosmetics, with effective dates from 2025 through 2028: California, Colorado, Connecticut, Maine, Minnesota, New Hampshire, New Mexico, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont, and Washington. This guide checked the statutes below. It did not re-read all eleven.

## Does a fluorine result mean PFAS enters the body?

These studies do not measure that. The 2021 paper measured fluorine and named PFAS in products. It did not measure PFAS in the blood of people who wore the makeup, and it did not measure a dose. Its exposure language is about routes that could matter: "PFAS could be ingested when lipstick containing PFAS is inadvertently ingested, and some PFAS in mascara could be absorbed through tear ducts." Could is the verb in that sentence.

The FDA report says dermal contact is the expected main route, because most cosmetics are applied to skin. Inhalation can matter for sprays and powders. Incidental ingestion can matter for lipstick. The same report says the data to turn those routes into a dose are missing for most of the ingredients it reviewed. A high fluorine reading, a nanogram-per-gram lab result, and an unmeasured absorption step are three different things. State sale rules, below, are not blood tests either.

## Which state laws restrict PFAS in cosmetics?

The rules here are sale restrictions in the named states. They are not a federal recall, and they do not govern a store in another state. "Intentionally added" is the usual trigger. A trace leftover from an impurity is a different question, and only some of these laws speak to it.

**California.** Assembly Bill 2771, Chapter 804, was approved September 29, 2022. Health and Safety Code section 108981.5 says that beginning January 1, 2025, no person or entity shall manufacture, sell, deliver, hold, or offer for sale in commerce any cosmetic product that contains intentionally added PFAS. Section 108982 defines PFAS as a class of fluorinated organic chemicals containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom. "Intentionally added" means PFAS a manufacturer added for a functional or technical effect, or PFAS that are intentional breakdown products of an added chemical. An older section, 108980, separately restricts a list of intentionally added ingredients that includes thirteen named PFAS and their salts, also beginning January 1, 2025. The class rule in 108981.5 is the broader one. PTFE meets the class definition. It is not one of the thirteen names in 108980.

**Minnesota.** Minnesota Statutes section 116.943, Amara's Law, says that beginning January 1, 2025, a person may not sell, offer for sale, or distribute for sale in the state a listed product if it contains intentionally added PFAS. Cosmetics are item 4 on that list. The statute defines a cosmetic as an article, other than soap, intended to be applied to the body for cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness, or altering appearance, including a component of such an article. "Intentionally added" means PFAS deliberately added so that PFAS stays in the finished product, or in a component, to do a job. The same section's dental-floss item is covered in the [floss guide](/post/does-dental-floss-contain-pfas-pfas-free-floss-swaps). The prohibition does not apply when the only intentionally added PFAS is in an electronic or internal component. Resale of a used product is exempt. The section is not a recall of makeup already at home.

**Colorado.** House Bill 22-1345 added section 25-15-604. Subsection (3) says that on and after January 1, 2025, a person shall not sell, offer for sale, distribute for sale, or distribute for use cosmetics that contain intentionally added PFAS chemicals, along with indoor textile furnishings and indoor upholstered furniture. The bill defines a cosmetic to include lipstick, nail polish, and eye or facial makeup, and to exclude a product that requires a prescription. "Intentionally added PFAS chemicals" means PFAS a manufacturer added that have a functional or technical effect, including intentional breakdown products of an added chemical. Hydrofluoroolefins used as propellants were carved out of the cosmetic definition, with that carve-out repealed January 1, 2027.

**Washington.** Revised Code of Washington section 70A.560.020 says that beginning January 1, 2025, no person may manufacture, knowingly sell, offer for sale, distribute for sale, or distribute for use in the state a cosmetic product that contains intentionally added PFAS. The chapter uses the definition in section 70A.350.010: a class of fluorinated organic chemicals containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom. An in-state retailer that already had stock when the restriction took effect may sell that stock through January 1, 2026. Prescription drugs are outside the cosmetic definition for this section.

**Maryland.** Maryland's rule is narrower. Health-General section 21-259.2, from House Bill 643 of 2021, Chapter 490, prohibits knowingly manufacturing, selling, delivering, holding, or offering for sale a cosmetic that contains specified intentionally added ingredients. The PFAS portion is a list of thirteen entries and their salts, including PFOS, PFOA, and PFNA. The General Assembly's bill page lists the effective date as January 1, 2025. A person is not in violation if the product was made through a process intended to comply and contains only a technically unavoidable trace from an impurity, manufacturing, storage, or packaging. PTFE is not on that named list. The FDA's eleven-state footnote, which is about bans on intentionally added PFAS as a class, does not include Maryland.

## How do you pick a makeup product without added PFAS?

Read the ingredient list, then read what the company is willing to say in its own words. Match the claim to the product, not to the category.

**Treat PTFE and any "perfluoro" or "polyfluoro" name as a disclosed PFAS.** The FDA's list above is the practical checklist. Credo Beauty's Dirty List, updated July 2022, tells shoppers to avoid ingredients with "perfluor" or "polyfluor" in the name, and says brand partners agree not to formulate with Dirty List ingredients on purpose. Credo's journal says the retailer added the whole PFAS class to that list in 2019 and also restricts PFAS in packaging. Credo is a store standard. It is not a lab report for every brand it sells.

**Do not stop at "PFOA-free" or "PFOS-free."** The FDA report says those two chemicals are not the PFAS companies list as intentional cosmetic ingredients. PTFE can still be the ingredient. The FDA's own safety table calls PTFE a low concern under intended cosmetic use and still counts it as a PFAS.

**Waterproof and long-wear are the marketing words the 2021 screen associated with high fluorine.** That was a pattern in products bought years ago, not a finding that every waterproof mascara on sale now has PFAS. A tubing mascara is a different formula claim: the listing says the film forms tubes and comes off with warm water. Tubing is not a fluorine test. Water-resistant, which some tubing listings also say, is closer to the marketing the paper flagged. Read both sentences.

**A company page that says "we never use PFAS" is the company's claim.** The 2021 paper is why that sentence is not a substitute for the ingredient list, and why the ingredient list is not a substitute for a test. None of the listings below was identified as one of the 231 products.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does waterproof mascara contain PFAS?

In the 2021 screen, waterproof mascara was the subset with the highest rate of high fluorine. Indiana University's summary of that study says 82% of 11 waterproof mascaras were above the high-fluorine cutoff, compared with 47% of all 32 mascaras. High fluorine is a marker, not the name of a chemical. The targeted test found named PFAS in all 29 products it measured, and the paper does not say those 29 were the waterproof mascaras.

### Did the 2021 study find PFAS in most makeup on the market?

It found high fluorine in 52% of the 231 products it tested, which were foundations, lip products, mascaras, and other makeup bought from 2016 to 2020. That is not a census of every cosmetic. The FDA's 2024 listing data, published in the 2025 report, found intentional PFAS ingredients in 0.41% of registered products. The two percentages use different methods. The 2021 paper also found that most high-fluorine products did not list a PFAS.

### Are the average and median in the 2021 abstract 264 and 1,050?

The abstract says concentrations ranged from 22 to 10,500 ng/g, "with an average and a median of 264 and 1050 ng/g product weights, respectively." The 29 sums in Table 1 run from 22 to 10,500, and 264 is the middle value when those sums are sorted. This guide relies on the range.

### Does "fluoro" on an ingredient list mean the product contains PFAS?

Not by that syllable alone. The 2021 paper treats synthetic fluorphlogopite as an inorganic fluorine source. PTFE, and names containing perfluoro or polyfluoro, are the disclosures the FDA and Credo point shoppers to. Credo's Dirty List says to avoid "perfluor" and "polyfluor" in the ingredient name.

### Did the FDA ban PFAS in makeup in 2025?

No. The December 29, 2025 report says there is no federal regulation that specifically prohibits intentionally added PFAS in cosmetics, and that presence alone does not make a product adulterated or misbranded. The report's safety review could not reach a conclusion for 19 of the 25 most-used PFAS. It rated PTFE a low concern under intended use and flagged perfluorohexylethyl triethoxysilane as a potential concern in a body-lotion scenario, with large data gaps.

### Does PTFE in mascara mean the FDA considers that mascara unsafe?

The FDA report says PTFE was listed in 490 formulations and cites use levels up to 13% in mascara. It also says the Cosmetic Ingredient Review concluded PTFE is safe in cosmetics at current use levels, and the FDA's summary table places PTFE in the low-concern group for intended use. PTFE is still a PFAS under the class definitions in the California, Minnesota, Colorado, and Washington statutes checked here. Maryland's named list does not include PTFE.

### Do the California, Minnesota, Colorado, and Washington rules apply in every state?

No. Each one restricts manufacture or sale in that state. California Health and Safety Code section 108981.5, Minnesota Statutes section 116.943, Colorado section 25-15-604(3), and Washington RCW 70A.560.020 all apply to intentionally added PFAS in cosmetics beginning January 1, 2025. Washington lets an in-state retailer sell stock it already had until January 1, 2026. None of these statutes is a recall of a product you already own.

### Is Maryland's cosmetics rule the same as a class-wide PFAS ban?

No. Section 21-259.2 restricts a list of intentionally added ingredients. The PFAS entries include PFOS, PFOA, PFNA, and named salts. The bill page gives an effective date of January 1, 2025. A technically unavoidable trace has an exception. The FDA's list of states with class-wide intentionally added PFAS bans does not include Maryland.

### Does the Thrive mascara listing say the formula is PFAS-free?

The listing for ASIN B0F566TFJN does not say PFAS-free. It says the Brynn shade is a tubing mascara, that it is water-resistant, and that it removes with warm water. Thrive's help center says PFAS, PFOA, PFOS, and PTFE are prohibited and are never used in the formulation of any Thrive product. That help page is the company's claim.

### Does the 100% PURE foundation or lipstick listing say PFAS-free?

No. The Cocoa foundation, ASIN B07B6DSR78, and the Clover lipstick, ASIN B07B6DJMX4, do not say PFAS-free. Their printed ingredient lists do not include PTFE or a perfluoro or polyfluoro name. 100% PURE's about page says the company never uses PFAS. The lipstick title also says long-lasting, which is a marketing word the 2021 paper associated with high fluorine in a different product type, liquid lipstick.

## Bottom line

Makeup is one of the product types where a 2021 fluorine screen found a high-fluorine marker in waterproof mascara, liquid lipstick, and foundation more often than in the set as a whole, and where the labels usually did not say PFAS. The FDA's 2025 inventory of intentional ingredients is much smaller, 0.41% of registered products, and the agency says it still cannot settle absorption or safety for most of those ingredients. If you want to avoid an added PFAS, skip a label that says PTFE or a perfluoro or polyfluoro name, treat "PFOA-free" as too narrow, and prefer a brand that says on its own site that it does not use PFAS. The listings in this guide that meet that last test are a Thrive tubing mascara and two 100% PURE products, a foundation and a lipstick.
