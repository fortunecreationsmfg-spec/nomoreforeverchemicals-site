**Quick answer:** Some children's car seats have been made with PFAS on the fabric, some with chemical flame retardants in the fabric or foam, and some with neither. Those are different additives. The Ecology Center's December 3, 2018 study of 18 seats, described by the Center for Environmental Health, found hazardous flame-retardant additives in 15 and likely PFAS on the fabric of 9. Its February 28, 2022 report found flame retardants in major components of 12 of 22 U.S. seats and likely PFAS, by total organic fluorine, in 4 of 19. Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard No. 302 tells a material how fast it may burn. It does not name a flame-retardant chemical. FMVSS 213, and FMVSS 213b, require each material in a child restraint system to meet that burn test. The Ecology Center's FAQ says chemical flame retardants are not required, and that a PFAS stain treatment is a design choice a washable cover can avoid. California has prohibited regulated PFAS in new juvenile products, including a child restraint system, since July 1, 2023. A current "PFAS-free" or "no added flame retardant" line is the manufacturer's sentence for the fabric it names. It is not a 2018 lab result, and it is not a reason to skip the seat. NHTSA says car crashes are a leading cause of death for children ages 1 to 13. Use a car seat that fits the child and the vehicle, and install it correctly, on every ride.

Below is what those tests, the flammability rule, the state statutes, and the current brand pages actually say. Stain finishes on furniture are a separate guide, [Scotchgard and stain-resistant fabric](/post/is-scotchgard-pfas-free-stain-resistant-furniture-carpet). Waterproof coatings on outerwear are in the [rain jacket guide](/post/do-rain-jackets-contain-pfas-pfas-free-rain-jackets).

## Have tests found PFAS in car seats?

Some of the seats that have been tested likely did. A seat on sale in 2026 is not one of those samples unless the report names it.

PFAS, per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, are a class of fluorinated chemicals. California, Minnesota, Colorado, Maine, and Vermont each define the class, in the statutes below, as fluorinated organic chemicals containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom. On a car seat, the use these sources describe is a water- and stain-resistant treatment on fabric. That is a different job from a flame retardant, which is added so a material will pass a burn test. A seat can have one, both, or neither. A wool cover and a washable polyester cover are two design answers. They are not the same chemical claim.

The record this guide can check is the Ecology Center's 2018 and 2022 car-seat testing, the 2019 list of flame-retardant-free models, the federal flammability text, the state sale rules, and the words on the brand pages reviewed in November 2026. It is not a census of every seat in a store.

Use the seat you have. The Ecology Center prints that instruction on the 2018 report, the 2018 FAQ, the 2019 write-up, and the 2022 report: a car seat is a required safety device, and a chemical finding is not a reason to leave a child unrestrained. NHTSA's car-seat page says the same thing in crash terms. Car crashes are a leading cause of death for children ages 1 to 13. The page says car seats saved 325 children under 5 in one year, and that 39 percent of children killed in car crashes in 2024 were unrestrained. It says to select a seat for the child's age and size, choose one that fits the vehicle, and use it every time. It also says a child's safety could be in jeopardy if the seat is not installed correctly. Chemical questions come after that.

## What did the 2018 car-seat tests find?

They found flame retardants in most of the 18 seats, and a fluorine signal the Ecology Center read as likely PFAS in half of them. Three seats in that set did not have added chemical flame retardants.

The report is the Ecology Center's Healthy Stuff program, *Hidden Hazards: Flame Retardants & PFAS in Children's Car Seats*, released December 3, 2018. The Center for Environmental Health's same-day release describes the set: 18 infant and convertible seats, purchased in 2018, manufactured in 2017 or later. Components were cut apart and analyzed by chromatography and mass spectrometry at Indiana University, total fluorine at the University of Notre Dame, and X-ray fluorescence and infrared spectroscopy at the Ecology Center. CEH's release says 80 percent (15) of the seats contained hazardous flame-retardant chemical additives, and 50 percent (9) likely contained persistent PFAS on the fabric. Fifteen of 18 is 83 percent if rounded to the nearest percent, so this guide uses the counts, 15 and 9, and treats "likely" as CEH's word for the fluorine result. The release says the chemicals found are most likely PFAS added for stain resistance. That is a screening judgment. It is not a list of named PFAS compounds for each seat.

The peer-reviewed companion is Yan Wu, Gillian Z. Miller, Jeff Gearhart, Kevin Romanak, Viorica Lopez-Avila, and Marta Venier, "Children's Car Seats Contain Legacy and Novel Flame Retardants," *Environmental Science & Technology Letters* 2019, volume 6, issue 1, pages 14–20, published online December 3, 2018. It measured brominated and phosphorus-based flame retardants in foam and fabric from 18 newly marketed seats. It did not measure PFAS. Two cyclic phosphonates, which the paper calls PMMMPs, had a median concentration of 73.6 micrograms per gram and accounted on average for 52 percent of the total flame-retardant concentration. The authors read that level as intentional addition during manufacturing. Decabromodiphenyl ethane, DBDPE, had a median of 128 micrograms per gram and was the only halogenated flame retardant the paper measured at levels suggesting intentional use. Polybrominated diphenyl ethers, PBDEs, were in 75 percent of the samples at a median total of 0.24 micrograms per gram. The paper says those trace levels suggest background contamination rather than intentional use.

The Ecology Center's FAQ for that study says three seats did not use chemical flame retardants and still passed the flammability test: the UPPAbaby MESA Jordan, which the FAQ says the lab confirmed, the Nuna Pipa Lite in Fog, and Clek's Mammoth wool fabric on the Fllo and Foonf. The 2018 ratings page lists the Clek Fllo Mammoth, the Nuna Pipa Lite Fog, and the UPPAbaby MESA Jordan as "Low Concern." The same page lists a different Nuna Pipa, in Graphite, as "High Concern," and a different UPPAbaby MESA, in Taylor, as "Moderate Concern." A brand name was not a result. The model and the fabric were.

The FAQ also says the lab result is valid only for the specific product tested, the model and the fabric. A seat that looks similar is not that sample. The seats were bought in 2018. This guide does not treat a 2026 listing with the same name as a retest.

## What did the 2019 and 2022 reports add?

By December 2019 the Ecology Center knew of 16 flame-retardant-free seats from five companies. By February 2022 it counted more than 40 models from eight companies marketed that way, and the share of tested seats with a likely PFAS fabric was lower than in 2018. The prices and the company list are 2019 and 2022 facts. They are not a 2026 catalog.

The December 2019 page says the lab tested at least one seat from each company making a flame-retardant-free claim, and in some cases sent samples to an external certified lab. The five companies were Britax, Clek, Maxi-Cosi, Nuna, and UPPAbaby. The basis column matters. "HS Tested Whole Seat" means the lab tested components of that seat. "HS Tested Cover" means the cover only. "Manufacturer Claim" means the lab did not test that seat. "Manufacturer Claim +" means the lab had tested the same fabric from the same company on another seat, plus the company's information. The page says Nuna's Pipa infant seat tested in 2018 contained flame retardants, and that Nuna later updated its site to say the Pipa was flame resistant with no added fire-retardant chemicals. A later company sentence and an earlier lab result are both on that page. This guide does not erase either one.

The 2019 write-up is the source for the washable-cover point. It says two companies featured removable, washable covers to help avoid stain-repellent coatings such as PFAS: Maxi-Cosi's PureCosi Magellan, whose cover came off with hook-and-loop, and Britax's SafeWash line. The same note says companies were meeting the flammability standard with wool blends, which are naturally fire resistant, and with denser weaves that hold less air. Melissa Cooper Sargent's sentence in that write-up is the safety line in the lab's own words: parents should always properly install and use a car seat, regardless of its chemical composition.

The February 28, 2022 report, *Toxic Inequities*, tested more than 600 components of 25 car seats, 22 from the United States and three from the European Union, plus four strollers bundled with matching seats. Infant and convertible seats from 11 brands were tested for flame retardants and PFAS. The report says more than half of U.S. children's car seats still contained flame retardants or PFAS. The counted results are narrower than that sentence. Of the tested U.S. seats, 12 of 22, or 55 percent, contained flame-retardant chemicals in major components. All 12 had phosphorus-based flame retardants in the upholstery. Seven of 22 had high bromine in a major component, which the report reads as consistent with brominated flame retardants. For PFAS, 4 of 19 tested car seats, 21 percent, had water- and stain-resistant fabrics the lab called likely to contain PFAS, based on total organic fluorine. Two of the four strollers likely contained PFAS, as did their matching car seats. None of the four strollers contained bromine or phosphorus-based flame retardants, even when the matching car seat did. The report's reading is that companies were adding those flame retardants to meet the U.S. car-interior standard, and were not adding them to the stroller.

The methods page says total organic fluorine was the PFAS indicator: combustion and an ion-selective electrode for total fluorine, a second measurement for inorganic fluoride, and the difference. Bromine above 1,000 ppm and phosphorus above 500 ppm were the flags for a possible flame retardant, followed by infrared spectroscopy. Six seats missed the fluorine shipment and were judged with a water-drop test and company information. The report's own PFAS page says a droplet that beads is not proof of PFAS. It gives a Clek "C-zero" fabric as a non-PFAS treatment that still beaded, and wool as naturally water-repellent to varying degrees.

The 2022 marketplace count is "over 40 car seat models, from eight companies, marketed as FR-free," up from eight seats from four companies in 2018. The report says Britax and Maxi-Cosi offered flame-retardant-free and PFAS-free seats for $200, that Maxi-Cosi had released a flame-retardant-free and PFAS-free convertible for $130, and that no flame-retardant-free and PFAS-free infant or convertible seat was sold in the U.S. for under $100. It also says Graco, Evenflo, and Baby Trend did not offer a flame-retardant-free seat and were the lowest-ranked seats in that study because of flame retardants and PFAS. Those sentences are the February 2022 report. This guide did not repeat the Graco, Evenflo, or Baby Trend check in 2026, so it does not state what those brands sell now.

European seats in the 2022 set did not contain phosphorus-based flame retardants even when a similar U.S. seat of the same brand did. The report attributes that to a different flammability standard. Some of those European seats still had elevated bromine. A seat bought in the European Union is not evidence about the U.S. version of the same name.

## What is FMVSS 302, and does it require chemical flame retardants?

It requires a burn rate. It does not require a chemical.

Standard No. 302, 49 CFR 571.302, specifies burn resistance for materials used in the occupant compartments of passenger cars, multipurpose passenger vehicles, trucks, and buses. Its purpose is to reduce deaths and injuries from vehicle fires, especially fires that start in the interior from sources such as matches or cigarettes. The requirement, S4.3, is that material described in the standard shall not burn, or transmit a flame front across its surface, at a rate of more than 102 millimeters per minute. If the material stops burning before it has burned for 60 seconds from the start of timing, and has not burned more than 51 millimeters from the point where timing started, it meets that burn-rate requirement. The covered components include seat cushions, seat backs, and seat belts, and any portion of a material within 13 millimeters of the occupant-compartment air space. The section does not name bromine, phosphorus, chlorine, or any flame-retardant chemical.

Child restraints are pulled into that test by the child-restraint standards. FMVSS 213, 49 CFR 571.213, S5.7 says each material used in a child restraint system shall conform to the requirements of S4 of FMVSS 302. FMVSS 213b, which becomes mandatory for child restraints beginning December 5, 2026, uses the same S5.7 sentence. A built-in restraint has to meet it in both the in-use and stowed positions. The chemical is the manufacturer's method. The regulation is the burn result.

The Ecology Center's 2018 FAQ says this directly: "Chemical flame retardants aren't required." They are, the FAQ says, a relatively inexpensive way to meet the standard. The same FAQ says the test was written for fires that start inside a vehicle from matches or cigarettes, and that a range of materials can meet FMVSS 302 without chemical flame retardants. The 2019 write-up says federal regulators have not been able to show a meaningful safety benefit from requiring children's car seats to comply with the vehicle-interior standard, and that companies often, but not always, meet it with chemical flame retardants. That is the Ecology Center's judgment about the standard. It is not a finding that a car seat without flame retardants fails in a crash. Crash protection is the child-restraint standard's dynamic tests and the seat's installation. The burn test is a separate paragraph.

The FAQ's answer on stain treatments is just as short. "Are waterproofing/stainproofing PFAS-based treatments required? No." They are a design choice. The FAQ says stain- and water-resistant treatments exist without PFAS, and that washable covers should be an industry standard. The 2022 report repeats the request: stop using PFAS, and make the cover easy to remove and wash. A washable cover avoids one reason to apply a stain treatment. It does not, by itself, prove the fabric was never treated. The claim still has to be read on the product.

The Center for Environmental Health's August 14, 2020 guide makes the same split. It says makers can meet the flammability standard without chemical flame retardants, that wool is one naturally flame-resistant option, and that washable covers are a reason a PFAS stain coating is not needed. It also says that if you are not shopping for a new seat, keep using the one you have, because motor-vehicle harm is the reason the seat exists. The 2020 product picks on that page are a 2020 list. This guide uses the manufacturers' own pages, below, for what a line claims now.

## What did the 2024 study find in cars?

It found TCIPP in the cabin air of nearly every car it sampled, and in some of the vehicle seat foam. It did not test child car seats.

The paper is Rebecca M. Hoehn, Lydia G. Jahl, Nicholas J. Herkert, Kate Hoffman, Anna Soehl, Miriam L. Diamond, Arlene Blum, and Heather M. Stapleton, "Flame Retardant Exposure in Vehicles Is Influenced by Use in Seat Foam and Temperature," *Environmental Science & Technology* 2024, volume 58, issue 20, pages 8825–8834, published online May 7, 2024. One hundred one people in the United States who owned a vehicle of model year 2015 or newer hung a silicone passive sampler on the rearview mirror for seven days. Fifty-one of the 101 collected a foam sample from a vehicle seat. A subset of 54 vehicles was sampled again in summer. The abstract says tris(1-chloro-isopropyl) phosphate, TCIPP, had a 99 percent detection frequency and was measured from 0.2 to 11,600 nanograms per gram of sampler. Table 2 splits that by season: 99 percent of the winter samplers and 98 percent of the summer samplers. The results section also names the chemical tris(1-chloro-2-propyl) phosphate and says the reported TCIPP is the sum of three major isomers. The short version of the abstract says organophosphate ester flame retardants were detected in the cabin air of all 101 vehicles. That "all 101" sentence is the class. The 99 percent figure is TCIPP.

TCIPP was the dominant flame retardant in the vehicle seat foam. In the results, flame retardants were detected in 33 of the foam samples analyzed, and TCIPP was the most common, in 23 foam samples. When TCIPP was in the paired foam, the median sampler concentration was about four times higher in winter and about nine times higher in summer than in vehicles whose foam sample did not contain it. Summer sampler concentrations of TCIPP were higher than winter. The paper says seat foam is a source and not the only source, because vehicles without TCIPP in the foam sample still had TCIPP on the sampler. The authors tie the chemicals to flammability standards such as FMVSS 302, and they say the standard does not prescribe which flame retardants could or should be used.

Consumer Reports' article on flame-retardant-free car seats describes this study and says the researchers found that about 99 percent of the cars contained TCIPP. The article's job in this guide is that summary, plus the point that flame-retardant-free child seats have become easier to find. The numbers this guide relies on are the paper's: 101 vehicles, model year 2015 or newer, 99 percent TCIPP detection in the winter cabin-air samplers, and TCIPP in 23 of the foam samples. A child car seat is a separate product, regulated under FMVSS 213, and it was not the foam in this study. A car with TCIPP in the vehicle seat does not tell you what is in the child restraint clicked into it. It does tell you that the cabin air in these late-model vehicles commonly carried a flame retardant, and that heat raised the amount the sampler collected.

The paper does not estimate a child's dose from riding in a child car seat. It estimates TCIPP air concentrations in the vehicle cabin from the silicone data, with a median of 56 nanograms per cubic meter in winter and 180 in summer, using a generic indoor sampling rate the authors say will vary with temperature, humidity, and airflow. Those are cabin-air estimates for the vehicles in the study. They are not a blood level, and they are not a result for a named child seat.

## Which state laws cover PFAS in car seats?

Several states restrict PFAS in juvenile products, and a child restraint is named in some of those definitions. The rules govern sale in that state. They are not a recall of a seat already in a car, and a rule in one state does not govern a store in another.

**California.** Assembly Bill 652 added Health and Safety Code sections 108945 through 108947, Chapter 500 of the Statutes of 2021. The chapter took effect January 1, 2022. The sale rule starts later. On and after July 1, 2023, a person, including a manufacturer, shall not sell or distribute in commerce in the state any new, not previously owned, juvenile product that contains regulated PFAS. A juvenile product includes a child restraint system for use in motor vehicles and aircraft, and also a booster seat, an infant carrier, and an infant seat, among other products for infants and children under 12. The definition excludes a children's electronic product, a medical device, an adult mattress, and an internal component that would not come into direct contact with a child's skin or mouth during reasonably foreseeable use and abuse.

Regulated PFAS means either of two things. The first is PFAS the manufacturer intentionally added for a functional or technical effect, including PFAS components of an added chemical and PFAS that are intentional breakdown products of an added chemical. The second is PFAS present at or above 100 parts per million, measured as total organic fluorine. A manufacturer shall use the least toxic alternative when replacing PFAS in a juvenile product under this chapter. A used seat is outside the "new, not previously owned" line. A seat sold in another state is outside the "in this state" line.

**Colorado.** On and after January 1, 2024, C.R.S. section 25-15-604 says a person shall not sell, offer for sale, distribute for sale, or distribute for use in the state a juvenile product that contains intentionally added PFAS chemicals. Section 25-15-603 defines a juvenile product to include booster seats, car seats, and other child restraint systems. Intentionally added means PFAS the manufacturer added for a functional or technical effect, including intentional breakdown products of an added chemical. Section 25-5-1302 defines PFAS chemicals as a class of fluorinated organic chemicals containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom. The juvenile-product definition excludes an internal component that would not contact a child's skin or mouth during reasonably foreseeable use and abuse. Colorado's juvenile-product rule, unlike California's, is an intentionally-added rule. It does not print a 100-ppm total-organic-fluorine threshold for this product category.

**Minnesota.** Beginning January 1, 2025, Minnesota Statutes section 116.943, subdivision 5, says a person may not sell, offer for sale, or distribute for sale in the state a juvenile product if it contains intentionally added PFAS. The juvenile-product definition includes a child restraint system for use in motor vehicles and aircraft, along with a booster seat, an infant carrier, and an infant seat. The same subdivision says the prohibition does not apply to a product that contains intentionally added PFAS only in electronic components or internal components. PFAS has the same class definition: at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom. A separate reporting duty, for manufacturers of products with intentionally added PFAS, has its own date. The sale prohibition for juvenile products is the January 1, 2025 line.

**Maine.** Effective January 1, 2026, 38 M.R.S. section 1614 says a person may not sell, offer for sale, or distribute for sale in the state a juvenile product containing intentionally added PFAS. The definition includes a child restraint system for use in motor vehicles and aircraft. Intentionally added means PFAS added to a product or a component to provide a specific characteristic, appearance, or quality, or to perform a specific function, and it includes degradation by-products. The prohibition does not apply to a juvenile product sold in used condition. It also does not apply where the department has determined by rule that the use is currently unavoidable, for the period that exemption lasts, or to a retailer who has not received the statute's notice that the sale is prohibited. A new child restraint with intentionally added PFAS is in the January 1, 2026 prohibition. A seat already owned is not a sale.

**Vermont.** Since January 1, 2026, 9 V.S.A. section 2494f says a manufacturer shall not manufacture, sell, offer for sale, distribute for sale, or distribute for use in the state a juvenile product to which PFAS have been intentionally added in any amount. Until July 1, 2027, "intentionally added" means the addition of a chemical that serves an intended function in the product component. A different, broader definition of intentionally added PFAS takes effect July 1, 2027. Section 2494e defines a juvenile product as a product designed or marketed for infants and children under 12. The examples include a booster seat, an infant carrier, and an infant seat. The section does not use the words "car seat" or "child restraint system." The prohibition does not apply to a product a consumer has already used for its intended purpose. PFAS again means a class of fluorinated organic chemicals containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom.

None of these statutes is a flame-retardant law. A seat can be legal under a PFAS sale rule and still contain a chemical flame retardant. A seat can be marketed as flame-retardant-free and still need a separate sentence about PFAS. Read the statute for the state where the seat is sold, and read the label for the chemical the question is actually about.

## Which car seat lines publish a PFAS-free or flame-retardant-free claim?

Only the sentence on the maker's page, for the fabric it names. A 2018 "low concern" rating is a result for the seat bought then. GREENGUARD Gold, where a brand states it, is an emissions test for volatile organic compounds. Chicco's page describes it that way. It is not a PFAS test, and it is not a flame-retardant test.

1. Use the seat you have, installed the way the manual and the vehicle manual describe. NHTSA's inspection stations and the registration card are the safety steps. A chemical question does not replace them.
2. Split flame retardants from PFAS. FMVSS 302 is the burn test. A PFAS stain treatment is optional, and several states now restrict intentionally added PFAS in new juvenile products.
3. Read the fabric, not only the brand. Clek's PFAS-free checkmarks are on Mammoth. Britax's no-added-FR claim is select fashions, including SafeWash, and StayClean is a different claim. UPPAbaby's Mesa V3 sentence is the fabric.
4. "No added flame retardant" does not mean PFAS-free unless the page says so. Nuna's page says the first. It does not say the second. Chicco says both, and says them about different scopes: ClearTex for flame retardants, car seats and strollers for added PFAS.
5. A 2018 or 2022 test result stays attached to the seat that was purchased. A 2026 company page is a 2026 sentence. Neither one is a crash-test rating.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do car seats contain PFAS?

Some tested seats likely did. In the Ecology Center's 2018 set, 9 of 18 seats likely had PFAS on the fabric. In the 2022 set, 4 of 19 did, by total organic fluorine. A seat that has not been tested is not those results. California has prohibited regulated PFAS in new juvenile products, including child restraint systems, since July 1, 2023. A maker's PFAS sentence still has to name the product.

### Are chemical flame retardants required in car seats?

No. FMVSS 302 and the child-restraint standards require materials to meet a burn rate of no more than 102 millimeters per minute, with a short-burn exception. They do not name a chemical. The Ecology Center's FAQ says chemical flame retardants are one inexpensive way to pass, and that seats have passed without them. Nuna, Clek's Mammoth page, Britax's SafeWash page, Maxi-Cosi's PureCosi page, UPPAbaby's Mesa V3 page, and Chicco's ClearTex page each say their named fabrics meet the standard without added flame retardants.

### Does a washable cover mean the car seat is PFAS-free?

No. The Ecology Center recommends washable covers so a stain-repellent coating is less necessary. Washable describes how you clean the cover. PFAS-free is a separate sentence. Britax says SafeWash covers have no added flame-retardant chemicals and can be machine washed. That page does not say PFAS-free. Clek's Mammoth page does say PFAS-free, and it also says the wool cover is spot-cleaned rather than machine-washed.

### Did the 2024 study find TCIPP in child car seats?

It found TCIPP in cars. In 101 vehicles of model year 2015 or newer, TCIPP was detected on 99 percent of the winter cabin-air samplers, and it was the most common flame retardant in the vehicle seat-foam samples, present in 23 of them. The foam was from the vehicle seat, not from a child restraint. Consumer Reports summarized the same study as about 99 percent of the cars. The study is a reason to understand flame retardants in vehicle interiors. It is not a brand list of child seats.

### Does California ban PFAS in car seats?

It bans regulated PFAS in a new juvenile product sold or distributed in the state, as of July 1, 2023. A child restraint system for motor vehicles and aircraft is in the definition. Regulated PFAS means intentionally added PFAS, or PFAS at or above 100 parts per million as total organic fluorine. The law does not apply to a seat that is not new, and it does not apply outside California. It also does not restrict flame retardants. Colorado, Minnesota, Maine, and Vermont have their own intentionally-added rules, with their own dates and definitions.

### Does Clek say its car seats are PFAS-free?

Clek says that about Mammoth. The Mammoth page says the wool and TENCEL blend is free of added flame retardants, PFAS-free, and fluorine-free, on the Liing, Foonf, Fllo, and Oobr. The April 13, 2026 support article says other named fabrics were free of added known flame retardants in the company's screening, and that C-Zero Plus is free of halogenated flame retardants. That article does not call C-Zero Plus PFAS-free.

### Does Nuna say its car seats are free of flame retardants and PFAS?

Nuna says the whole U.S. car-seat line is free of added fire-retardant chemicals, from fabric to foam, and that those materials meet FMVSS 302. That is the FR-free page. The page does not say the seats are PFAS-free. A 2018 test of a Nuna Pipa in Graphite found flame retardants. Nuna's later sentence is the company's update. It does not rewrite the 2018 sample.

### Does UPPAbaby say the Mesa is PFAS-free?

The Mesa V3 page says the fabric is free from flame-retardant chemicals, that the cover is removable and washable, and that the seat is GREENGUARD Gold certified. The support answer says all V3 fashions are fire-retardant free through SafeTech fabrics. The pages reviewed do not say PFAS-free. GREENGUARD Gold, as Chicco and UPPAbaby describe it, is a volatile-organic-compound emissions certification. The 2018 low-concern rating was a MESA Jordan, not a Mesa V3.

### What is the difference between Britax SafeWash and StayClean?

SafeWash, and Britax's other naturally flame-retardant covers, are the no-added-flame-retardant fabrics. Britax says they pass FMVSS 302 by the weave, and that SafeWash can be machine washed and dried. StayClean, also called Nanotex, is the stain-resistant fabric. Britax says it meets FMVSS 302 by adding flame retardants, and that the stain protection is free of PFOS and PFOA. Free of PFOS and PFOA is not a PFAS-free claim for the class.

### Does Chicco say ClearTex is free of flame retardants and PFAS?

ClearTex, yes for added flame retardants: the page says the fabrics, foam, and labels meet FMVSS 302 without flame-retardant chemical treatments. The PFAS sentence is wider. Chicco's chemical-policy page says all of its car seats and strollers do not contain added PFAS. Chicco also says a Cornell Center for Materials Research analysis of ClearTex components did not find added fire-retardant or stain-repellent chemicals. That Cornell account is the one Chicco publishes. Compliance with FMVSS 302, which Chicco states for all of its car seats, is the burn test. It is not, by itself, the ClearTex no-added-chemical sentence.

### Should you stop using a car seat because of PFAS or flame retardants?

No. NHTSA says to use the right seat every time, because car crashes are a leading cause of death for children ages 1 to 13, and because an incorrect installation can defeat the seat. The Ecology Center says to properly install and use a car seat regardless of chemical results. If you are buying another seat, you can choose a fabric whose maker states no added flame retardants, a PFAS sentence, or both, and you can prefer a cover that comes off and washes. The seat you already own is still the seat that restrains the child.

## Bottom line

Tested car seats have contained chemical flame retardants, likely PFAS, or both. The 2018 counts are 15 of 18 seats with hazardous flame-retardant additives and 9 of 18 with likely PFAS. The 2022 counts are 12 of 22 U.S. seats with flame retardants in major components and 4 of 19 with likely PFAS. FMVSS 302 sets the burn rate and does not require a flame-retardant chemical, and FMVSS 213 and 213b apply that burn rate to every material in a child restraint. California's juvenile-product PFAS rule has covered new child restraint systems since July 1, 2023, and Colorado, Minnesota, Maine, and Vermont have their own intentionally-added rules. Clek's Mammoth page is the one in this set that says both flame-retardant-free and PFAS-free. Nuna and the Mesa V3 page say flame-retardant-free fabric and do not say PFAS-free. Chicco says ClearTex has no added fire retardants and that its car seats and strollers do not contain added PFAS. None of that changes the safety instruction. Put the child in a properly installed car seat on every ride. Other household swaps are in the [non-toxic products directory](/non-toxic-products).
