**Quick answer:** It is still reasonable to eat fish, and it is not reasonable to treat every fillet as the same. A 2023 paper in *Environmental Research* analyzed more than 500 composite fillets of locally caught freshwater fish from EPA surveys in 2013–2015. The median of total targeted PFAS was 9,500 nanograms per kilogram in rivers and streams and 11,800 ng/kg in the Great Lakes. PFOS averaged 74% of that total. The median in those freshwater fillets was 278 times the median in commercially relevant fish the FDA tested in 2019–2022. The authors' slides for the same paper put median PFOS at 8,415 ng/kg and modeled one 8-ounce serving at that level as equal to a month of drinking water containing 48 ppt PFOS, assuming cooking removes none of it. Grocery seafood is lower in that comparison, and it is not a clean category. FDA detected at least one PFAS in 60 of 81 retail seafood samples in a 2022 survey. A Dartmouth-led study published in *Exposure and Health* on April 12, 2024 found PFAS in every seafood sample it bought at a New Hampshire market, with the highest concentrations in shrimp and lobster, not in the tuna or salmon in that set. Massachusetts' Department of Public Health says that if a fish contains PFAS, cutting, cleaning, and cooking will not remove it. FDA's advice is still to eat seafood, and to check the state, territorial, or tribal advisory before you eat fish you caught.

What PFAS are, as a class, is in the [complete guide to forever chemicals](/post/what-are-pfas-chemicals-the-complete-guide-to-forever-chemicals). PFAS in drinking water, a separate route from the fillet, is in the [bottled water guide](/post/does-bottled-water-contain-pfas-spring-vs-purified) and the [water filters guide](/post/do-water-filters-remove-pfas-best-pfas-water-filters).

## Does fish contain PFAS?

In the surveys below, yes. The amount depends on where the fish lived, which species it is, and whether you caught it or bought it. There is no national PFAS grade for "fish" as a food.

PFAS, per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, are the class EPA added to its fish-monitoring recommendations in July 2024. EPA's news release on that update says the agency's National Aquatic Resource Surveys, and other studies, have found PFAS in freshwater fish and shellfish at levels that may affect human health. The same release says those national surveys do not, by themselves, tell a person how many meals to eat from one lake. That job belongs to the state, Tribe, or territory, using fish from that water.

FDA's questions and answers on PFAS in food say exposure to certain PFAS has been linked to higher cholesterol, higher blood pressure and pre-eclampsia in pregnant women, developmental effects, a lower immune response, changes in liver function, and increases in certain cancers. The page says most of that research has focused on PFOA and PFOS, and that the health effects of most other PFAS are unknown or limited. Massachusetts DPH's fact sheet on PFAS in recreationally caught fish uses a similar list and adds a limit of its own: those health effects also have other causes, and a meal of fish cannot be matched to a later illness in one person. This guide uses those pages for the health context. It does not add a dose for one dinner.

## How do freshwater fish and store-bought fish compare?

The largest published gap is between locally caught freshwater fillets in EPA's 2013–2015 surveys and the commercial fish FDA tested in 2019–2022. "Store-bought" and "ocean" are not the same thing. A grocery case can hold marine fish, farmed salmon, and freshwater species such as tilapia. The paper's comparison is commercial fish versus those EPA freshwater fillets.

The paper is "Locally caught freshwater fish across the United States are likely a significant source of exposure to PFOS and other perfluorinated compounds," *Environmental Research*, volume 220, article 115165 (March 1, 2023). The PubMed record is [36584847](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36584847/). The DOI is [10.1016/j.envres.2022.115165](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envres.2022.115165). The lab did not catch the fish. It analyzed more than 500 composite fillet samples EPA had already collected across the United States from 2013 to 2015, under the National Rivers and Streams Assessment and the Great Lakes Human Health Fish Fillet Tissue Study.

The abstract reports three results that set the scale. The median level of total targeted PFAS in fillets from rivers and streams was 9,500 ng/kg. The median in the Great Lakes was 11,800 ng/kg. PFOS was the largest contributor, averaging 74% of the total. The median levels of total detected PFAS in freshwater fish across the United States were 278 times the levels in commercially relevant fish FDA tested in 2019–2022. A single serving of freshwater fish per year, at the median level in those EPA programs, was modeled as a significant increase in PFOS in blood serum. The abstract also says this exposure falls hardest on communities that depend on fishing for food and for traditional cultural practices.

The authors' [National Fish Forum slides](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2023-02/Andrews-Locally-Caught-Freshwater.pdf), posted by EPA, put numbers on the PFOS portion and on the drinking-water comparison. Median PFOS in the freshwater fillets from the 2013–2015 EPA testing was 8,415 ng/kg. The 90th percentile was 49,976 ng/kg. An 8-ounce serving at the median, 8,415 ng/kg PFOS, was drawn as equal to one month of drinking water at 48 parts per trillion PFOS. The same serving at the 90th percentile was drawn as equal to a month at 286 ppt. The slides' comparison figure for FDA-tested fish is an average of 20 ng/kg PFOS, drawn as equal to a month of water at 0.1 ppt. [EWG's January 2023 news release](https://www.ewg.org/news-insights/news-release/2023/01/ewg-study-eating-one-freshwater-fish-equals-month-drinking) on the study describes the same median calculation as one freshwater fish serving in a year equating to a month of water at 48 ppt PFOS. The release rounds the commercial gap to 280 times. This guide uses 278 times, which is the figure in the paper's abstract.

Those drinking-water equivalents are a model, not a blood test of people who ate one fish. The slides list the assumptions: steady-state consumption, any freshwater fish as exposure above the U.S. median, no PFOS removed by cooking, and 100% of the PFOS in the tissue absorbed. EPA's [April 10, 2024 drinking-water rule](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas) later set an enforceable maximum contaminant level of 4.0 ppt for PFOS in public tap water, with a health-based goal of zero. On the EPA page reviewed for this guide, that 4.0 ppt PFOS limit was still the printed MCL, and two May 18, 2026 actions were still described as proposals. Forty-eight parts per trillion is above that later tap-water MCL. The 2023 slides were not a comparison to the 2024 rule, and a tap-water MCL is not a fish-meal limit. The status of the 2026 proposals is covered in the [bottled water guide](/post/does-bottled-water-contain-pfas-spring-vs-purified).

The commercial side of the 278-times gap is not a certificate that ocean fish, or a supermarket fillet, has no PFAS. It is a median-to-median comparison against FDA's commercial testing in those years. The FDA results are in the next sections. They include detects in salmon, tuna, shrimp, and other retail seafood, at levels FDA judged differently from the freshwater medians.

## What do EPA's fish surveys and 2024 advisory recommendations say?

EPA recommends which contaminants a fish-advisory program should test for. It does not publish a national meal chart for PFAS the way FDA and EPA publish one for mercury.

The [July 2024 fact sheet](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-06/contaminants-monitor-fish-factsheet-july2024.pdf), EPA 823-F-24-011, updates the contaminants states, Tribes, and territories should consider monitoring in fish and shellfish. It makes two lists. The first, contaminants to monitor for advisories, is for chemicals that already have a federal measure of oral toxicity, such as a reference dose or a cancer slope factor. Programs are told to use that list when they monitor and when they set consumption limits. Five PFAS are on it: PFDA, PFHxS, PFNA, PFOA, and PFOS. The second list, contaminants to monitor to watch, is for chemicals found in edible tissue at levels that could matter, but without a federal oral-toxicity value yet. Seven PFAS are on it: PFDS, PFDoA, PFHpS, PFOSA, PFTeDA, PFTrDA, and PFUnDA, also abbreviated PFUdA or PFUnA. That is twelve PFAS. The fact sheet says a chemical's presence on either list does not mean it is in every water in the country. It means the chemical has accumulated in fish or shellfish somewhere at a level that could harm health if it were released locally.

[EPA's news release](https://www.epa.gov/newsreleases/epa-releases-new-science-based-recommendations-help-more-states-tribes-and-territories), last updated July 11, 2024, says the agency issued those recommendations on July 11. It says this is the first time PFAS were added to the contaminant list for locally caught freshwater fish. It names states that already had some PFAS fish advisories: Alabama, Connecticut, Indiana, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, North Carolina, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Washington, and Wisconsin. The release's instruction for a person who eats locally caught freshwater fish or shellfish is to ask the state, Tribe, or territory how much of that fish is a safe amount. EPA's list of [state, territorial, and tribal fish-program contacts](https://www.epa.gov/choose-fish-and-shellfish-wisely/state-territorial-tribal-and-epa-fish-program-contacts) is the directory for that question. This guide does not copy the names or phone numbers on that page.

The same release is the National Aquatic Resource Survey sentence. The most recent survey, which monitors fish tissue from lakes and streams, and other studies, found PFAS in freshwater fish and shellfish at levels that may affect human health. The release says those studies show that PFAS are in fish, and that they do not give enough local detail for a public-health decision. A [questions-and-answers document](https://www.asdwa.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/QA-Monitoring-PFAS-in-Fish-07_11_24.pdf) on the July 2024 recommendations, dated the day after the announcement, is more specific about the survey counts. It says the 2018–2019 National Rivers and Streams Assessment reported PFOS in 91% of the fish samples, and that this was a significant decrease from the 2013–2014 assessment. It does not print the 2013–2014 percentage. It says Great Lakes studies EPA has conducted since 2010 found PFOS in every sample. It also says EPA does not issue fish advisories under the Clean Water Act and was not developing a national consumption advisory for the new contaminants. Local programs do that, from a species in a waterbody. This guide is using that document for the 91% figure and the Great Lakes sentence. The percentage is not repeated on the news release.

A later EPA step is about water, not a meal card. The agency's [January 2025 Fish and Shellfish Program newsletter](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2025-01/fish-program-news-jan-2025.pdf) says that on December 19, 2024 EPA announced draft recommended human-health criteria for PFOA, PFOS, and PFBS in waterbodies. Once final, states and authorized Tribes could use them in water-quality standards that protect people who drink the water and eat the fish and shellfish. The newsletter says the criteria are not regulatory requirements. Comments were open through February 24, 2025. The July 2024 questions and answers, written before that announcement, say EPA does not expect states and Tribes to turn national water-quality criteria directly into local fish advisories. A fish advisory is a number of meals. A water-quality criterion is a concentration in the water. This guide did not find a final version of those December 2024 criteria. The binding local instruction is still the advisory for the water where the fish was caught.

## What did the 2024 Dartmouth study find in shrimp, lobster, tuna, and salmon?

The market study is the one that speaks to tuna, salmon, shrimp, and lobster people buy, rather than to a bass from a river. It is a New Hampshire case study, not a national ranking of every brand.

Dartmouth's [April 12, 2024 account](https://home.dartmouth.edu/news/2024/04/pfas-exposure-high-seafood-diets-may-be-underestimated) describes a paper in *Exposure and Health*: "Patterns of Seafood Consumption Among New Hampshire Residents Suggest Potential Exposure to Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances," DOI [10.1007/s12403-024-00640-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12403-024-00640-w). The researchers measured 26 PFAS in the marine species people there eat most: cod, haddock, lobster, salmon, scallop, shrimp, and tuna. The seafood was bought fresh at a market on the New Hampshire coast and came from more than one region. Shrimp and lobster had the highest concentrations. Averages for certain PFAS compounds were as high as 1.74 nanograms per gram of flesh in shrimp and 3.30 ng/g in lobster. Individual PFAS in the other fish and seafood in the set were generally under 1 ng/g. A nanogram per gram is 1,000 nanograms per kilogram, so 1.74 ng/g is 1,740 ng/kg and 3.30 ng/g is 3,300 ng/kg. Those are averages for certain compounds in market seafood. They are not the median sum of targeted PFAS in the freshwater fillets, which the 2023 paper reports as 9,500 ng/kg in rivers and streams. The two studies did not test the same fish, and this guide does not divide one number by the other.

The news account says some shellfish may build up PFAS because they live and feed on the seafloor, close to coastal sources, and that larger marine animals may take PFAS in by eating smaller ones. It also says the university's recommendation is not to stop eating seafood. Seafood is a source of lean protein and omega fatty acids. The same account says people who eat moderate amounts should be able to have those benefits without excessive PFAS risk, and that the tradeoff matters most for pregnant people and children.

An [NIEHS Superfund Research Program brief](https://tools.niehs.nih.gov/srp/researchbriefs/view.cfm?Brief_ID=354) on the same paper, released June 5, 2024, adds the detection pattern. PFAS were found in every seafood sample. Shrimp and lobster each had seven PFAS compounds, the most of any species in the test. The high compounds were PFOS in shrimp and PFUnDA in lobster. Hazard quotients, the brief's comparison of estimated intake with health-guidance values, were not high for average consumers of shrimp and lobster. Among people who eat a lot of seafood, PFOS in shrimp and PFUnDA in lobster had high hazard quotients for both adults and children. The brief does not print a gram-per-day cutoff for "high." This guide does not invent one.

The eating survey behind the paper covered 1,829 New Hampshire residents. Dartmouth's account says men ate just over one ounce of seafood a day and women just under one ounce, both higher than the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey figures for the Northeast and more than 1.5 times the national average. Children ages 2 to 11 ate about 0.2 ounces a day, the high end of the national range for children. About 95% of the adults said they had eaten seafood in the past year, 94% of that group had eaten fish or shellfish in the past month, and more than two-thirds of those respondents had eaten seafood in the past week. Among adults who ate seafood at least monthly, more than 70% ate shrimp, more than 70% ate haddock, and more than 70% ate salmon. Just over 54% ate lobster. For children, the most common species were salmon, canned tuna, shrimp, and haddock. The NIEHS brief's order for the whole survey is shrimp, salmon, and haddock, then canned tuna, lobster, and scallops. More than half of the people who had eaten seafood in the week before the survey lived on the coast or near the Massachusetts line. More than 60% of people in households with income under $45,000 a year ate seafood at least weekly, and people with higher incomes ate it less often.

Tuna and salmon were in the lower group of that market test. They were not reported as free of PFAS. The news account's "generally less than one nanogram per gram" is an individual-compound description for the other species in this purchase, including tuna and salmon. It is not a test of every can of tuna or every farmed salmon fillet.

## What has FDA found in seafood from the store?

FDA has found PFAS more often in seafood than in the rest of the foods it tested, and it has not told the public to stop eating fish. The one retail product it flagged as a health concern in the 2022 seafood survey was certain canned clams, not tuna or salmon.

[FDA's questions and answers on PFAS in food](https://www.fda.gov/food/process-contaminants-food/questions-and-answers-pfas-food) say the agency has tested more than 1,900 food samples from the Total Diet Study and from targeted assignments. In the Total Diet Study foods, no PFAS were detected above the method detection limit in 1,290 of 1,352 fresh and processed foods, which the page describes as over 95%. Of the 62 Total Diet Study samples with a detect, 46 were seafood. That is 69% of the 67 seafood samples in that study. In the 2022 targeted seafood survey, one or more PFAS were detected in 74% of samples, 60 of 81, across clams, cod, crab, pollock, salmon, shrimp, tilapia, and tuna. The page says the seafood data are limited, and that seafood may be at higher risk of environmental PFAS contamination than other foods.

The [July 6, 2022 constituent update](https://www.fda.gov/food/fda-updates-food/fda-shares-results-pfas-testing-seafood), with a July 15 note, is the health reading of those 81 samples. Most were imported. FDA evaluated the detected PFAS that have toxicological reference values. Estimated exposure to PFOA from the canned clam samples, which were from China, was likely a health concern. For the two clam samples with the highest PFOA, FDA said there would be a potential health concern for someone eating more than about 10 ounces of those clams a month, and that young children should limit those clams to 2 ounces a month. The other PFAS evaluated in the clams, and the PFAS evaluated in all the other seafood samples, were not likely a health concern. Two distributors took voluntary recall action on the canned clam products included in the test. FDA told people who regularly eat canned clams from China, or feed them to children, that they may want to cut back until more was known. A later passage on the questions-and-answers page says a 2023–2024 follow-up of 12 samples of processed clams, from the two processors tied to the highest 2022 PFOA results, found at least one PFAS in all 12 samples and PFOA in all 12. The same page says filter-feeding bivalves, including clams, oysters, mussels, and scallops, may build up certain contaminants more than other seafood. It says additional sampling of more than 130 molluscan shellfish ran from September 2024 through September 2025. The section this guide read does not print the concentrations from that later round.

FDA's answer to "should I stop eating seafood" is no. The page says to keep eating a variety of healthy foods, including seafood, because of nutrients for a child's brain and immune system and because of heart-health benefits for adults. For fish or shellfish you caught, it says to check the state consumption advisory. The same page says FDA is reviewing seafood, bottled water, and infant formula as places where an action level might later be appropriate. It does not announce a finished PFAS limit for fish in the grocery case. An action level, as that page defines it, is not a permission slip for contamination below the number.

## Does cooking or cleaning remove PFAS from fish?

No. Massachusetts DPH's fact sheet on [PFAS in recreationally caught fish](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/per-and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas-in-recreationally-caught-fish) answers the question in one line: if a fish contains PFAS, there is no way to remove it, and it cannot be cut, cleaned, or cooked out. The 2023 exposure model assumed the same thing for PFOS. That assumption is not a kitchen experiment. The department's sentence is the instruction.

The department's separate page on [eating fish safely in Massachusetts](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/eating-fish-safely-in-massachusetts) talks about chemicals in fish more broadly. It says chemicals cannot be removed by cleaning or cooking, and it also says skipping the fat and the fatty organs can lower exposure because some chemicals concentrate there. The PFAS fact sheet does not say that trimming the fat removes PFAS. This guide does not recommend trimming as a PFAS step. The same general page says to eat fewer large predatory fish and bottom-feeding fish, with largemouth bass and carp as examples, and more panfish, such as pumpkinseed, because the larger and bottom-feeding fish are more likely to carry high levels of chemicals. It also says trout are left out of the state's fish advisories except when the advisory is for PFAS. Those are Massachusetts rules for Massachusetts waters. They are not a PFAS measurement of a salmon fillet in another state.

Boiling the fish in filtered water does not change that. A filter treats the water. Massachusetts says the PFAS already in the flesh stays in the flesh. Filtering the water you drink, and the water you cook with, is a response to the drinking-water route, which EPA regulates separately. It is not a way to decontaminate the fish. How to read a filter certificate is in the [water filters guide](/post/do-water-filters-remove-pfas-best-pfas-water-filters).

## Which fish do FDA and EPA still say to eat?

The federal meal chart that names tuna and salmon is a mercury chart. FDA and EPA revised [Advice about Eating Fish](https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish) in October 2021. The page says the chart is based on mercury levels. It is written for people who are or might become pregnant, people who are breastfeeding, and children ages 1 to 11, and it says it supports the Dietary Guidelines for Americans. "Fish" on that page includes shellfish.

On the chart, salmon is a Best Choice. Canned light tuna, including skipjack, is a Best Choice. Albacore, also called white tuna, canned, fresh, or frozen, is a Good Choice. Yellowfin tuna is a Good Choice. Bigeye tuna is on the Choices to Avoid list, with shark, swordfish, king mackerel, marlin, orange roughy, and tilefish from the Gulf of Mexico, because those are the highest-mercury fish on the chart. Shrimp, American and spiny lobster, cod, haddock, and scallops are also Best Choices. That is a mercury sorting. In the Dartmouth market test, shrimp and lobster were the high-PFAS species, and tuna and salmon were in the lower group. A Best Choice for mercury is not a low-PFAS result.

The amounts on the FDA and EPA page, for pregnancy and breastfeeding, are 2 to 3 servings a week from the Best Choices list, or 1 serving a week from the Good Choices list. One serving in that section is 4 ounces. The Dietary Guidelines line on the same page is 8 to 12 ounces a week, during pregnancy or breastfeeding, of a variety of seafood lower in mercury. For a 2,000-calorie diet, the Dietary Guidelines recommendation printed there is at least 8 ounces of seafood a week, and less for children. Children are told to eat 2 servings a week from the Best Choices list. A child's serving is about 1 ounce at ages 1 to 3, 2 ounces at ages 4 to 7, 3 ounces at ages 8 to 10, and 4 ounces at age 11. The page says fish provide DHA and EPA, and that strong evidence shows eating fish, as part of a healthy pattern, may benefit the heart.

For fish caught by family or friends, the same page says to check the fish and shellfish advisories for how often those fish can be eaten. If there is no advisory, eat only one serving of that fish and no other fish that week. It says larger carp, catfish, trout, and perch are more likely to have advisories because of mercury or other contaminants. "Choose a smaller fish" fits that sentence, and it fits Massachusetts' advice to prefer panfish over large predators and bottom feeders. It does not tell you which grocery species had less PFAS in the Dartmouth purchase. Varying species is the step both FDA's PFAS page and the mercury advice actually share.

## What should you do before the next fish meal?

Use the advisory for the water in front of you, keep seafood in the diet, and do not expect the pan to solve PFAS.

1. Before you eat fish you caught, or fish someone else caught locally, check the state, territorial, or tribal advisory for that water and that species. EPA's July 2024 release tells people who eat locally caught freshwater fish to do this. FDA says the same for recreational catch. If there is no advisory, FDA and EPA's mercury advice says one serving that week and no other fish. [EPA's contact list](https://www.epa.gov/choose-fish-and-shellfish-wisely/state-territorial-tribal-and-epa-fish-program-contacts) is the directory. Posted signs at the water count too. Massachusetts says to follow the posted PFAS advisory where there is one.
2. Keep eating fish. FDA's PFAS page says not to drop seafood. The Dietary Guidelines and the FDA/EPA chart still recommend it, in the amounts above, from the lower-mercury choices. Dartmouth's account of the 2024 market study says the recommendation is not to stop.
3. Vary the species. A week that is only shrimp, or only one local bass, is a different exposure from a week that moves around the Best Choices list. The mercury list and the PFAS studies do not rank species in the same order, which is a reason to vary rather than a reason to crown one fish.
4. For a local catch, prefer the smaller fish the advisory allows. FDA and EPA flag larger carp, catfish, trout, and perch as more likely to have an advisory. Massachusetts tells its anglers to eat fewer large predators and bottom feeders, and more panfish, when the concern is chemicals in local fish. Read the PFAS line on that advisory separately. Trout can be outside a mercury advisory and inside a PFAS advisory.
5. Do not count on cooking, trimming, or a water filter to remove PFAS from the fillet. Massachusetts says cutting, cleaning, and cooking will not do it. A home filter is for the drinking water, including water you use for cooking, when a test or a Consumer Confidence Report gives you a reason to filter. The certificate to look for is the one in the [water filters guide](/post/do-water-filters-remove-pfas-best-pfas-water-filters): NSF/ANSI 53 or 58 for PFAS reduction.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it still safe to eat fish?

Health agencies still tell people to eat it. FDA's PFAS page says not to stop eating seafood. FDA and EPA's October 2021 advice, and the Dietary Guidelines language on that page, still recommend regular seafood from the lower-mercury choices. The PFAS record is a reason to check the advisory before you eat a local catch, and a reason not to treat grocery fish as PFAS-free. It is not a reason these agencies give for dropping fish.

### Does freshwater fish have more PFAS than ocean fish or store-bought fish?

In the 2023 *Environmental Research* comparison, the median total detected PFAS in U.S. freshwater fillets from EPA's 2013–2015 surveys was 278 times the median in commercially relevant fish FDA tested in 2019–2022. Rivers and streams were 9,500 ng/kg at the median, and the Great Lakes were 11,800 ng/kg. Store-bought is not a synonym for ocean. FDA's 2022 retail survey included tilapia along with tuna, salmon, shrimp, and other seafood, and it detected PFAS in 60 of 81 samples. The health conclusion FDA published for that survey was that the non-clam results were not likely a health concern, while PFOA in certain canned clams from China was.

### Are tuna and salmon low in PFAS?

In the Dartmouth market purchase, individual PFAS in the species other than shrimp and lobster, a set that includes tuna and salmon, were generally under 1 ng/g. The study detected PFAS in every sample, so those fish were not reported as PFAS-free. On the FDA and EPA mercury chart, salmon and canned light tuna, including skipjack, are Best Choices, albacore and yellowfin are Good Choices, and bigeye tuna is on the avoid list. That chart is mercury.

### Do shrimp and lobster contain PFAS?

In the Dartmouth study, yes. They had the highest concentrations in that purchase, with averages as high as 1.74 ng/g in shrimp and 3.30 ng/g in lobster for certain PFAS compounds, and seven PFAS compounds in each. The NIEHS brief says average consumers of those two were not at high risk on the hazard quotients, and that people who eat a lot of seafood had high quotients for PFOS in shrimp and PFUnDA in lobster. Shrimp and lobster are Best Choices on the mercury chart. That is a different contaminant.

### Does cooking fish remove PFAS?

No. Massachusetts DPH says PFAS in a fish cannot be cut, cleaned, or cooked out. Trimming the fat is advice aimed at chemicals that sit in fat. The PFAS fact sheet does not say trimming removes PFAS.

### Which fish are safest?

There is no single safest species in these sources. For a fish from a local river or lake, the safest choice is the one the state, Tribe, or territory says you can eat, in the amount it prints, and a smaller panfish is the direction Massachusetts gives for chemicals in local fish. For fish from a store, FDA still recommends a variety of lower-mercury seafood, including salmon and canned light tuna, and it has told people not to stop. The Dartmouth purchase found less PFAS in tuna and salmon than in shrimp and lobster, at one New Hampshire market. A national "safest fish" label would need a newer, broader test than the ones this guide could verify.

## Bottom line

Fish is still on the menu, and PFAS in fish is real. EPA's freshwater fillets from 2013–2015 had a median of 9,500 ng/kg total targeted PFAS in rivers and streams and 11,800 ng/kg in the Great Lakes, with PFOS about three-quarters of the total, and that freshwater median was 278 times the commercial fish in FDA's 2019–2022 testing. One 8-ounce serving at the median PFOS of 8,415 ng/kg was modeled as a month of water at 48 ppt PFOS, if cooking removes none. Cooking removes none, according to Massachusetts DPH. EPA's July 2024 recommendations tell advisory programs to monitor twelve PFAS and tell anglers to use the local advisory. The 2018–2019 river-and-stream survey still found PFOS in 91% of fish samples. Grocery seafood is a lower and uneven category: FDA found PFAS in 60 of 81 retail samples in 2022, flagged certain canned clams, and said the other seafood results were not likely a health concern. The 2024 Dartmouth market study found the highest PFAS in shrimp and lobster, with tuna and salmon in the lower group of that purchase, and it said not to give up seafood. Follow the FDA and EPA mercury amounts, vary the species, check the advisory before a local fish dinner, and do not expect a pan or a water filter to clean the fillet. The filter, if you need one for the tap, is a drinking-water decision covered in the [water filters guide](/post/do-water-filters-remove-pfas-best-pfas-water-filters).
