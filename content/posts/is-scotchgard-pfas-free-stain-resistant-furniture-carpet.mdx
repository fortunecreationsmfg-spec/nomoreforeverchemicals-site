**Quick answer:** The original Scotchgard stain and water repellents used PFOS, and PFOS is a PFAS. EPA's April 25, 2006 release says those PFOS-related compounds were the active ingredients for decades, that 3M stopped manufacturing PFOS in the United States in 2000, and that it phased the chemistries out globally by the end of 2002. A 2005 report from the Danish Environmental Protection Agency says that in June 2003, 3M replaced the PFOS compound in Scotchgard products with PFBS, perfluorobutane sulfonate. PFBS is a shorter-chain chemical, and it is still a PFAS under the class definition California and Minnesota use: a fluorinated organic chemical containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom. On a 3M page last updated January 28, 2025, and still carrying the sentence in a May 8, 2025 capture, the company says it has eliminated the use of PFAS in Scotchgard products that help protect soft surfaces in homes. The December 20, 2022 announcement was broader. 3M said it would exit all PFAS manufacturing, and work to discontinue PFAS use across the portfolio, by the end of 2025. The 2025 annual report says the manufacturing exit is complete, and that some products made before the end of 2025 were still being sold through customer transitions. That soft-surface sentence does not describe an old can in a cabinet, a couch treated years ago, or a stain finish applied by a furniture or carpet mill that is not 3M.

Below is what those company pages, the California background document, and the state statutes actually say, and which swaps the listings support. PFAS on a waterproof shell is a separate guide, [rain jackets](/post/do-rain-jackets-contain-pfas-pfas-free-rain-jackets). Other rooms are in the [room-by-room guide](/post/your-room-by-room-guide-to-removing-forever-chemicals-from-your-home).

## Is Scotchgard PFAS-free?

The answer depends on which product, and which year.

3M's consumer FAQ for Scotchgard, reviewed for this guide, does not say whether the cans contain PFAS. It says that once the product is dry, the item is safe for children and pets, and that the products comply with applicable laws and are safe for their intended uses. It also gives coverage numbers. One can of Fabric Water Shield protects one average-sized sofa, two chairs, or five jackets. For upholstery, the FAQ says to reapply every six months or after a professional or water-extraction cleaning. Those sentences are instructions and a company safety statement. They are not an ingredient list, and they are not a dust measurement.

The PFAS sentence is on a different 3M page. The page titled "PFAS & Their Uses," last updated January 28, 2025, says 3M was the first company to announce an exit from certain PFAS compounds in the early 2000s, and then: "For example, we have eliminated the use of PFAS in our Scotchgard products that help protect soft surfaces in homes." A capture of that page from May 8, 2025 still shows the sentence. When this guide tried to open the live pfas.3m.com address in October 2026, the server returned an access denial. The current stewardship page at 3m.com, as returned to a search of that URL, describes the manufacturing exit and the work to discontinue use. It does not, in the passage returned, repeat the Scotchgard sentence. This guide cites the sentence from the January 28, 2025 page, not from a guess about a can on a shelf today.

A safety data sheet is a different document from that sentence. The U.S. sheet for Scotchgard Fabric Water Shield 4106 PF, document group 41-0602-7, version 5.05, issue date July 16, 2026, lists hydrotreated light petroleum distillates at 60 to 70 percent, liquefied sweetened petroleum gases at 20 to 30 percent, a proprietary silicone mixture at 2 to 7 percent, a proprietary resin under 5 percent, and a proprietary crosslinker under 1 percent. The sheet says the specific chemical identity and the exact percentage have been withheld as a trade secret. A silicone line on that sheet is what 3M printed. A trade-secret line is not a fluorine test. This guide does not read the characters "4106 PF" in the product name as a PFAS result.

## What did the original Scotchgard use?

PFOS. The records that say so are an EPA enforcement release and 3M's own May 16, 2000 announcement.

On May 16, 2000, after negotiations with EPA, 3M announced it would voluntarily phase out and find substitutes for perfluorooctanyl sulfonate chemistry used in a range of products, including some Scotchgard lines. EPA's release that day says 3M data indicated the chemicals are very persistent, tend to accumulate in human and animal tissues, and could pose a risk over the long term. EPA said it supported a phase-out and substitutes by year's end for the production of the involved products, and that 3M was then the only U.S. manufacturer of PFOS. The same day's 3M announcement, filed with the SEC, says the company was phasing out the perfluorooctanyl chemistry used to produce certain repellents and surfactant products. The affected lines were about 2 percent of nearly $16 billion in annual sales. 3M named many Scotchgard products among them, including soil, oil, and water repellents, and said it planned to substantially phase out production by the end of the year.

EPA's April 25, 2006 settlement release is the later official summary of that chemistry. It says PFOS-related compounds were the active ingredients used for decades in the original formulation of 3M's Scotchgard stain and water repellents. It says 3M voluntarily stopped manufacturing PFOS in the United States in 2000 and phased out all of these chemistries on a global basis by the end of 2002. 3M's 2025 annual report uses the same window for the U.S. work: the company announced in 2000 that it would phase out production of PFOA and PFOS globally, and most of the phase-out activities in the United States were completed by the end of 2002. The phase-out, the report says, included materials used to produce certain repellents and surfactant products.

PFOS is one chemical in the class. EPA's page on health and environmental risks says there are thousands of PFAS, with different effects, and that household products and dust include stain- and water-repellent used on carpets, upholstery, clothing, and other fabrics. Dropping PFOS left the rest of the class as a separate question.

## What replaced PFOS in Scotchgard?

A shorter-chain PFAS, according to the Danish Environmental Protection Agency's 2005 survey, and then, on 3M's later account, a formula the company describes as free of PFAS for soft-surface home products.

The Danish report, Environmental Project No. 1013, says that in June 2003, 3M replaced the PFOS compound in Scotchgard products with PFBS, perfluorobutane sulfonate. It describes Scotchgard products as cleaners and stain protectors for carpets, leather, furniture, automotives, hard surfaces, and other apparel. It says that after the PFOS phase-out, 3M first sold an aerosol based on non-perfluoro chemistry, that the can worked on water and not on grease, and that 3M then used the shorter four-carbon perfluoro compound. The report cites the Fluoride Action Network's 2004 account for that sequence. This guide treats those sentences as the Danish agency's 2005 statement, not as a 2026 ingredient panel.

PFBS meets the statutory class definition this guide is using. California Health and Safety Code section 108970 and Minnesota Statutes section 116.943 both define PFAS as a class of fluorinated organic chemicals containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom. A four-carbon perfluoroalkyl sulfonate is in that class. A move from an eight-carbon sulfonate to a four-carbon sulfonate is a change of chain length inside the class.

The later company claim is the soft-surface sentence above. It is a 3M statement about Scotchgard products that protect soft surfaces in homes. It is not a statement about every product that has ever carried the Scotchgard name, and it is not a statement about a mill that buys a finish from someone else.

## What did 3M announce in December 2022?

It announced two dates, both the end of 2025, and its 2025 annual report says the manufacturing half of that announcement is finished.

The December 20, 2022 release, from St. Paul, says 3M will exit PFAS manufacturing and work to discontinue the use of PFAS across its product portfolio by the end of 2025. The manufacturing exit is specific. 3M said it would discontinue manufacturing all fluoropolymers, fluorinated fluids, and PFAS-based additive products, help customers through an orderly transition, and fulfill current contracts during that period. The use exit is worded as work: "Work to discontinue use of PFAS across our product portfolio by the end of 2025." The release says manufactured PFAS then had annual net sales of approximately $1.3 billion, with estimated EBITDA margins of approximately 16 percent. It says the use discontinuation represented a small portion of overall revenue. It estimates related pre-tax charges of approximately $1.3 billion to $2.3 billion. The release also says 3M's products are safe for their intended uses. That is the company's sentence in the announcement.

The 2025 annual report, the filing at accession 0000066740-26-000155, says 3M completed its exit of PFAS manufacturing at the end of 2025. It says the company has made substantial progress eliminating PFAS use in its own products. It then describes the limit. For PFAS-containing materials 3M does not manufacture, the company says it is still evaluating whether third-party products without PFAS are available and feasible. It names examples that may continue past 2025, including lithium-ion batteries, printed circuit boards, and certain seals and gaskets. It says that in some cases, sales and use of products manufactured before the end of 2025, and sales through customer transitions, regulatory approvals, or re-qualification of substitutes, were not completed by the end of 2025, and that transitional efforts are ongoing.

Read those paragraphs in order. The manufacturing exit is a completed statement. The portfolio-wide use exit is substantial progress plus a list of transitions that were still open. The Scotchgard soft-surface sentence is an example 3M gave in January 2025, before that manufacturing date. A can filled earlier, or a sofa finished by another company, sits in the gap the annual report describes for products made before the exit.

## Can an older can or a pre-treated sofa still contain PFAS?

Yes. The soft-surface sentence is about 3M's Scotchgard products for that use. It does not inventory warehouses, and it does not speak for other mills.

The annual report says sales of products manufactured before the end of 2025 were not all finished when the manufacturing exit was. A can bought years earlier was filled under whatever formula was current then. The 2006 EPA release and the 2005 Danish report are why an old Scotchgard can is a PFAS question even when a 2025 company page says the current soft-surface products are not. The consumer FAQ tells people to reapply every six months. A household that has been doing that for years may have layers from more than one formula.

Furniture and carpet are a second source. Mills apply finishes during manufacture. California's aftermarket-treatment rule, below, expressly does not cover products sold exclusively for use during manufacturing. A sofa that arrived with a factory "stain protection" package can carry a finish 3M does not make. 3M's January 2025 sentence does not claim to describe that package. Asking the retailer whether the optional spray or the factory treatment is free of PFAS is a question about that item. A hangtag that says only "stain resistant" has not answered it.

## How do treated carpets and upholstery get into house dust?

DTSC's 2016 background document describes the path as ordinary use. Walking and vacuuming wear the treatment off the carpet, and the PFAS leave in indoor air and dust.

The document is "Perfluoroalkyl and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances (PFASs) in Carpets, Rugs, Indoor Upholstered Furniture, and their Care and Treatment Products," dated November 15, 2016. DTSC identified carpets, rugs, indoor upholstered furniture, and their care and treatment products as the largest potential sources of significant and widespread PFAS exposure among the product categories on its work plan. It says that during normal use, treated carpets, rugs, indoor upholstered furniture, and other consumer products release PFAS into indoor air and dust. It estimates that 50 percent of the PFAS treatment may be lost from carpets because of walking and vacuuming over a typical nine-year product lifespan. People ingest or inhale that dust, and they can also be exposed when they use after-market carpet and upholstery cleaning and treatment products. The document says young children may have higher exposure because they ingest more dust and transfer more from hand to mouth. It also says wastewater plants do not effectively remove PFAS, and that landfill leachate from disposed treated products is another release.

That 50 percent figure is DTSC's estimate, in a 2016 background paper, for PFAS-treated carpet of the kind the paper is describing. It is not a measurement of an untreated wool rug, and it is not a measurement of a 2026 can whose maker says the formula no longer uses PFAS.

ATSDR's prevention page, reviewed for this guide, lists the same household route in plainer language. People can be exposed by accidentally swallowing or breathing contaminated soil or dust, and by accidentally swallowing residue or dust from consumer products that contain PFAS, "such as stain-resistant carpeting and water-repellent clothing." The page says research has suggested that exposure to PFOA and PFOS from today's consumer products is generally lower than exposure from PFAS-contaminated drinking water. It also says young children may be more likely to take PFAS in because they chew on toys and fabrics and put their hands in their mouths. EPA's health page puts stain- and water-repellent on carpets, upholstery, and other fabrics in the same household-dust category. Neither page publishes a dose for one sofa.

EPA's asthma page is about dust in general, and it is the cleaning instruction this guide is willing to repeat. It says to vacuum carpets and furniture every week, to dust often with a damp cloth, and to use a vacuum with a HEPA filter on carpet and fabric-covered furniture to reduce dust build-up. A HEPA vacuum, on that page, is a tool for the dust. DTSC's estimate is why the dust from a treated carpet is part of this guide. Vacuuming does not pull a bonded finish back out of the fiber. The 2016 document lists vacuuming as one of the ways the treatment leaves the carpet.

## What does California require for carpets, couches, and fabric sprays?

Three different rules, with three different jobs. None of them is a recall of a sofa you already own.

**Carpets and rugs.** On July 1, 2021, DTSC began regulating carpets and rugs that contain PFAS as a Priority Product under the Safer Consumer Products program. That listing requires manufacturers who sell those products in California to notify DTSC and then to remove the chemical, remove the product, replace the chemical, or prepare an alternatives analysis. It is not the flat sales ban used for textile articles. On October 29, 2024, DTSC released a compliance report. The department says the regulation led to industry-wide abandonment of PFAS in these products, that no manufacturer has notified DTSC of continued or proposed use, and that DTSC's own tests of carpets and rugs sold in California found widespread compliance. The same page says that in 2023 DTSC tested carpets and rugs sold in California and found that PFAS are no longer used in the carpets and rugs sold in the state. It estimates that the transition has prevented nearly a quarter million pounds of PFAS from entering California homes and workplaces each year. Those sentences are about carpets and rugs sold in California. They are not a national census of upholstered furniture, and they are not a test of a rug already in a house.

**Aftermarket sprays and cleaners.** Effective April 1, 2022, DTSC listed treatments containing any PFAS for use on converted textiles or leathers as a Priority Product. "Converted" means the textile or leather has already been made into a consumer product such as a carpet, upholstery, clothing, or shoes. The listing covers products marketed to remove dirt or stains, or to repel stains, dirt, oil, or water, including cleaners, protectants, spot removers, and water repellents. It does not include products marketed exclusively for use while the carpet, rug, clothing, shoe, or furniture is being manufactured. Manufacturers whose treatments contain PFAS and are sold in California had to file a Priority Product Notification by May 31, 2022, and then, by September 28, 2022, a removal, replacement, or preliminary alternatives-analysis option. Manufacturers whose treatments do not contain PFAS did not have to file. DTSC says replacing one PFAS with another member of the class could be a regrettable substitution, which is why the listing covers the class.

**Upholstery and other household textiles.** Assembly Bill 1817 added Health and Safety Code sections 108970 and 108971. The chapter took effect January 1, 2023. The sale rule starts later. Beginning January 1, 2025, no person shall manufacture, distribute, sell, or offer for sale in the state any new, not previously used, textile article that contains regulated PFAS. Textile articles include furnishings and upholstery. They do not include carpets and rugs, and they do not include treatments containing PFAS for use on converted textiles or leathers, because those two are the Safer Consumer Products listings above. A new sofa's upholstery is in the 2025 textile rule. The factory or aftermarket carpet treatment is in the Priority Product program. A couch you already own is a previously used article, and the sale rule is written for new goods.

Regulated PFAS in that textile chapter means either of two things. The first is PFAS a manufacturer intentionally added for a functional or technical effect, including PFAS components of an added chemical and intentional breakdown products that have a functional effect. The second is PFAS at or above a total-organic-fluorine threshold: 100 parts per million beginning January 1, 2025, and 50 parts per million beginning January 1, 2027. A distributor or retailer that is not the manufacturer is not in violation if it relied in good faith on the manufacturer's certificate of compliance.

## Which other state rules cover carpets, fabric treatments, or upholstered furniture?

The dates below are sale rules for the named state. A statute in one state does not govern a store in another. Where the text exempts used goods, this guide says so. Minnesota restricts carpets, rugs, fabric treatments, textile furnishings, and upholstered furniture with intentionally added PFAS as of January 1, 2025. Colorado restricts carpets, rugs, and fabric treatments as of January 1, 2024, and indoor upholstered furniture as of January 1, 2025. Maine restricts carpets, rugs, and fabric treatments as of January 1, 2023. Vermont has restricted residential rugs and carpets since July 1, 2023. New York's carpet rule starts December 31, 2026.

**Minnesota.** Amara's Law is Minnesota Statutes section 116.943. Beginning January 1, 2025, a person may not sell, offer for sale, or distribute for sale in the state a product that contains intentionally added PFAS if the product is a carpet or rug, a cleaning product, cookware, a cosmetic, dental floss, a fabric treatment, a juvenile product, a menstruation product, a textile furnishing, ski wax, or upholstered furniture. The statute defines a fabric treatment as a substance applied to fabric to give it a characteristic such as stain resistance or water resistance. The Pollution Control Agency's page on the 2025 prohibitions says that definition is the substance applied to the fabric, and that pre-treated fabric which is itself a prohibited product, or a component of one, is included. Examples the agency gives for carpets or rugs include car floor mats, recreational-vehicle carpeting, outdoor rugs, and synthetic turf. The prohibition does not apply when the only intentionally added PFAS are in an electronic component or an internal component. Subdivision 8 says the section does not apply to the sale or resale of a used product. Beginning January 1, 2032, the statute extends the prohibition to any product with intentionally added PFAS unless the agency has determined the use is currently unavoidable, and it says the agency may not make that determination for a product already listed in the 2025 categories.

**Colorado.** Section 25-15-604 of the Colorado Revised Statutes, in the text current through Fall 2025, sets three dates. On and after January 1, 2024, a person shall not sell, offer for sale, distribute for sale, or distribute for use in the state carpets or rugs, or fabric treatments, if the product contains intentionally added PFAS chemicals. The same subsection also lists food packaging, juvenile products, and oil and gas products. On and after January 1, 2025, the prohibition reaches cosmetics, indoor textile furnishings, and indoor upholstered furniture. On and after January 1, 2027, it reaches outdoor textile furnishings and outdoor upholstered furniture. House Bill 22-1345, the 2022 act, defined "product" to exclude a used product offered for sale or resale.

**Maine.** Title 38, section 1614, sets the earliest carpet date in this group. Effective January 1, 2023, a person may not sell, offer for sale, or distribute for sale in the state a carpet or rug that contains intentionally added PFAS. The same date applies to a fabric treatment, defined as a substance applied to fabric to give it a characteristic such as stain resistance or water resistance. The fabric-treatment prohibition also applies when the treatment itself has no intentionally added PFAS but is sold in a fluorinated container or a container that otherwise contains intentionally added PFAS. A carpet or rug, in this section, is a consumer product of natural or synthetic fabric intended as a floor covering inside a commercial or residential building. It includes a carpeted doormat. It does not include a carpet or rug intended solely for outdoor use, or one intended solely for use inside a vehicle. Used carpets, rugs, and fabric treatments are exempt. Effective January 1, 2026, the section also prohibits upholstered furniture and textile articles that contain intentionally added PFAS. The textile-article definition includes furnishings and upholstery, and it excludes carpets, rugs, and treatments, which already have the 2023 date. Used goods in the 2026 list are exempt. A separate paragraph, effective January 1, 2032, reaches other products with intentionally added PFAS unless the department has determined the use is currently unavoidable.

**Vermont.** From July 1, 2023 until its repeal on January 1, 2026, section 1682 of Title 18 said a manufacturer, supplier, or distributor shall not manufacture, sell, offer for sale, distribute for sale, or distribute for use in the state a residential rug or carpet to which PFAS have been intentionally added in any amount. That section did not apply to the sale or resale of used products. The replacement is 9 V.S.A. section 2494f, added effective January 1, 2026. A manufacturer shall not manufacture, sell, offer for sale, distribute for sale, or distribute for use in the state residential rugs and carpets, or aftermarket stain and water-resistant treatments, to which PFAS have been intentionally added in any amount. The same section prohibits textiles or textile articles to which regulated PFAS have been intentionally added. In the definitions effective until July 1, 2027, regulated PFAS means intentionally added PFAS with a functional or technical effect, or the presence of PFAS at or above 100 parts per million total organic fluorine. Beginning July 1, 2027, that threshold is 50 parts per million. The textile-article definition excludes rugs or carpets, which are covered by their own line. Previously used products are outside the prohibition.

**New York.** The Department of Environmental Conservation's carpet page states the rule in one sentence: effective December 31, 2026, no carpet sold or offered for sale in New York State shall contain or be treated with PFAS substances for any purpose. The law is Article 27, Title 33 of the Environmental Conservation Law, the carpet extended-producer-responsibility statute, which took effect December 28, 2024. Carpet, on DEC's page, includes broadloom carpet, modular carpet tiles, artificial turf, and pads or underlayment used with carpet. The law does not cover handmade rugs, area rugs, or mats. This guide did not find a furniture ban or a fabric-spray ban in that carpet page. An area rug and a sofa are outside the sentence DEC printed for December 31, 2026.

## What should you buy instead of a stain-resistant finish?

Skip the optional factory add-on, or ask whether that add-on is free of PFAS. Prefer a fiber that does not need a topical fluorinated spray, and a cover you can wash. If you still want a spray, use one whose maker uses a PFAS or fluorochemical sentence for that bottle, and match the bottle to the sheet.

Wool, cotton, and an untreated fabric are the straightforward swaps. A solution-dyed synthetic is a different construction: the color is put into the fiber when the fiber is made, which is why some outdoor fabrics resist fading without a topical coating. Solution-dyed is a description of the color. It is not, by itself, a statement that nobody later sprayed the fabric. Ask which one you are buying. A "stain resistant" line on a hangtag, with no chemical name, has not answered the question.

Shield Industries' own page for ForceField AQ, the water-based carpet and upholstery protector, lists the chemical family as an alkyl copolymer, a pH of 7 to 9, and sizes that include 22 oz. That page does not use the word PFAS. A different ForceField document, the 2020 solvent-based Fabric Protector sheet, lists the chemical family as a fluorocarbon polymer blend. Those are two products. Match the bottle in your hand to the water-based listing before you treat the Amazon sentence as the formula.

### The wool rug

The [Lorena Canals Woolable rug](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B082SY614V?tag=nomoreforev05-20), ASIN B082SY614V, is the Winter Calm size listed as 5 feet 7 inches by 7 feet 11 inches. The title says the pile is 100 percent wool and the base is recycled cotton. A bullet says to wash it separately on a gentle short cycle at 30 degrees Celsius with a neutral detergent, and another bullet says the rug is handmade of natural wool with non-toxic dyes. Wool is the fiber DTSC's background document is not describing when it estimates treatment loss from a PFAS-treated carpet. The Amazon page still does not say this rug was sold without a topical finish, and "non-toxic dyes" is not a PFAS result.

1. Decline the optional stain-protection package, or ask the seller to name the treatment and to say whether it contains PFAS.
2. Prefer wool, cotton, or a fabric that was not given a topical stain finish. If a synthetic is solution-dyed, ask whether a finish was added after the fiber was made.
3. Use a washable slipcover when the sofa underneath would otherwise get a spray.
4. If you buy a protector, read the sentence on that bottle. A 3M page updated January 28, 2025 says soft-surface Scotchgard products no longer use PFAS. An older can is the PFOS and PFBS history above. The ForceField water-based listing is the one whose text says zero fluorochemicals.
5. Vacuum carpets and upholstery weekly with a HEPA vacuum, and damp-dust hard surfaces. That is EPA's dust instruction. It reduces the dust DTSC and ATSDR describe. It does not decontaminate a treated fiber.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is current Scotchgard free of PFAS?

3M said so for one group of products. A company page last updated January 28, 2025 says 3M has eliminated PFAS from Scotchgard products that help protect soft surfaces in homes. The consumer FAQ does not repeat that sentence. A July 16, 2026 safety data sheet for Fabric Water Shield lists a proprietary silicone mixture and withholds other identities as trade secrets. An old can is a different product from that sentence.

### Did the original Scotchgard contain PFOS?

Yes. EPA's April 25, 2006 release says PFOS-related compounds were the active ingredients in the original Scotchgard stain and water repellents for decades. 3M's May 16, 2000 announcement said it would phase out the perfluorooctanyl chemistry used in many Scotchgard repellents. EPA says U.S. PFOS manufacturing stopped in 2000 and the global phase-out of those chemistries was done by the end of 2002.

### Is the PFBS that replaced PFOS still a PFAS?

Yes. The Danish Environmental Protection Agency's 2005 report says 3M replaced the PFOS in Scotchgard products with PFBS in June 2003. PFBS is perfluorobutane sulfonate, a four-carbon fluorinated sulfonate. California and Minnesota define PFAS as fluorinated organic chemicals containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom. A shorter chain is still inside that definition.

### When did 3M say it would stop manufacturing PFAS?

On December 20, 2022. The release says 3M would exit all PFAS manufacturing, including fluoropolymers, fluorinated fluids, and PFAS-based additives, by the end of 2025, and would work to discontinue PFAS use across the portfolio by the same date. Manufactured PFAS were about $1.3 billion in annual net sales. The 2025 annual report says the manufacturing exit was completed at the end of 2025.

### Can a stain-resistant couch or an old spray can still contain PFAS?

Yes. The annual report says some products manufactured before the end of 2025 were still moving through sales and customer transitions. A factory finish from a company other than 3M is outside the Scotchgard sentence. California's aftermarket-treatment rule does not cover products used only during manufacturing, which is where a mill-applied finish sits.

### Do treated carpets shed PFAS into house dust?

DTSC's November 15, 2016 background document says they do. Treated carpets, rugs, and indoor upholstered furniture release PFAS into indoor air and dust during normal use. The document estimates that 50 percent of the treatment may be lost from carpets to walking and vacuuming over a typical nine-year life. ATSDR lists swallowing dust from stain-resistant carpet as an exposure route and says that route is generally lower, for PFOA and PFOS, than contaminated drinking water.

### Does California ban PFAS in carpets and in sofas?

The tools are different. Since July 1, 2021, carpets and rugs that contain PFAS have been a Safer Consumer Products Priority Product. Since April 1, 2022, aftermarket PFAS treatments for converted textiles have been one too. Beginning January 1, 2025, AB 1817 restricts regulated PFAS in new textile articles, including upholstery, at an intentional-use test or at 100 ppm total organic fluorine, dropping to 50 ppm in 2027. The textile chapter excludes carpets, rugs, and those treatments. DTSC's 2024 report says 2023 tests found PFAS no longer used in carpets and rugs sold in California.

### What does Minnesota's Amara's Law cover in 2025?

Beginning January 1, 2025, Minnesota prohibits sale of carpets or rugs, fabric treatments, textile furnishings, and upholstered furniture, among other categories, if they contain intentionally added PFAS. Used products are exempt. A fabric treatment is the substance applied to the fabric. The agency says pre-treated fabric that is part of a prohibited product is included. A 2032 prohibition reaches other products unless the use is found currently unavoidable, and that finding is not available for the 2025 categories.

### What do Colorado, Maine, Vermont, and New York require?

Colorado restricts intentionally added PFAS in carpets, rugs, and fabric treatments as of January 1, 2024, and in indoor textile furnishings and indoor upholstered furniture as of January 1, 2025. Outdoor furnishings and outdoor upholstered furniture follow on January 1, 2027. Maine restricts carpets, rugs, and fabric treatments as of January 1, 2023, and upholstered furniture and textile articles as of January 1, 2026. Vermont has restricted residential rugs and carpets with intentionally added PFAS since July 1, 2023, and from January 1, 2026 the statute also covers aftermarket stain and water-resistant treatments. New York, on December 31, 2026, says no carpet sold in the state shall contain or be treated with PFAS. Handmade rugs, area rugs, and mats are outside that carpet definition.

### Does a HEPA vacuum remove PFAS from carpet?

It is a dust control, not a decontamination. EPA says to vacuum weekly with a HEPA filter and to damp-dust, to reduce dust build-up. The Shark NV356E listing cites a 99.9 percent dust-and-allergen figure for particles of 0.3 microns and larger. DTSC lists vacuuming as one way a PFAS treatment leaves a carpet and enters dust. Catching that dust is the reason to use a sealed HEPA machine. The listing does not say the vacuum pulls PFAS out of the fiber.

## Bottom line

The original Scotchgard repellent was a PFOS product, and the formula the Danish Environmental Protection Agency described for June 2003 was a PFBS product. Both are PFAS. 3M's December 20, 2022 announcement set the end of 2025 for leaving PFAS manufacturing and for working to discontinue PFAS use, and the 2025 annual report says the manufacturing exit is complete while some earlier production was still in transition. The company's January 28, 2025 page says the Scotchgard products that protect soft surfaces in homes no longer use PFAS. That sentence leaves older cans, and stain finishes from other manufacturers, in the question. California regulates PFAS carpets and aftermarket textile treatments as Priority Products and, from January 1, 2025, restricts regulated PFAS in new upholstery. Minnesota, Colorado, Maine, Vermont, and New York restrict some combination of carpets, fabric treatments, and upholstered furniture, on the dates above, and New York's December 31, 2026 rule is a carpet rule that leaves area rugs and sofas out. The practical steps these sources support are to skip an unnamed stain-protection add-on, choose wool, cotton, or an untreated fabric, wash a slipcover instead of respraying the sofa, buy a protector whose own text names the chemistry, and vacuum the dust with a HEPA machine.
