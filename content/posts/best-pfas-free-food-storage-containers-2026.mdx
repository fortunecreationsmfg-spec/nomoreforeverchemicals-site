## PFAS in packaging paper, takeout containers, and lids

The published PFAS problem next to food is concentrated in three places: grease-resistant paper and paperboard, the takeout container made from that paper, and the lid or coating you add on top of an inert body.

On February 28, 2024, the FDA announced that grease-proofing materials containing PFAS are no longer being sold for use in food packaging in the United States. The agency named fast-food wrappers, microwave popcorn bags, take-out paperboard containers, and pet food bags, and called that packaging the major source of dietary exposure to PFAS from food packaging. The same announcement says the FDA has authorized certain PFAS for limited use in cookware, food packaging, and food-processing equipment, and that exposure to some types of PFAS has been linked to serious health effects. A sales stop on grease-proofing substances is not a recall of every box already in a restaurant, and it is not a fluorine test of a reusable container. The later step, on the food-contact notifications, is written up in the [takeout explainer](/post/does-takeout-food-packaging-contain-pfas-safer-pizza-box-swaps).

Consumer Reports published its packaging investigation on March 24, 2022. CR tested multiple samples of 118 food-packaging products from restaurant and grocery chains for total organic fluorine, which the press release calls the simplest way to assess a material's total PFAS content. CR found measurable total organic fluorine in more than half of the products. Thirty-seven were above 20 parts per million, and 22 were above 100 ppm. The products were paper bags for fries, hamburger wrappers, molded-fiber salad bowls, and paper plates. The two highest averages CR reported were paper bags for sides from Nathan's Famous, at 876 ppm and 618 ppm. CR's own advice on that release: do not assume an environmentally friendly claim means PFAS-free, move food out of the packaging, and do not reheat the meal in that packaging. Those samples are not the glass and steel in the table above. They are the reason the table starts with glass and steel.

State rules aimed at the same paper. California Health and Safety Code section 109000, added by AB 1200, says that beginning January 1, 2023, no person shall distribute, sell, or offer for sale in the state any food packaging that contains regulated PFAS. In that section, food packaging is a nondurable package or food-service ware made in substantial part of paper, paperboard, or other materials originally derived from plant fibers. Regulated PFAS means either PFAS the manufacturer intentionally added for a functional or technical effect, or PFAS present at or above 100 ppm measured as total organic fluorine. Washington's Department of Ecology says certain paper or other plant-fiber food packaging with intentionally added PFAS may not be manufactured, sold, or distributed in the state, under RCW 70A.222.070. Ecology's list as of February 1, 2023 names wraps, plates, food boats, and pizza boxes. As of May 1, 2024 it also names bags and sleeves, bowls, flat serviceware, open-top containers, and closed containers. Both laws are about that plant-fiber packaging. They are not a rule that a reusable glass bowl, or the plastic lid on it, has been tested.

Iowa State University Extension and Outreach published "Safe Food Storage Containers" on AnswerLine on October 10, 2023. The page says food-grade is a regulatory term for materials suitable to touch food, that the FDA decides which materials are allowed, and that a food-grade material is food-safe only when it is used as intended. On glass, stainless steel, and ceramic, the page says these materials are non-reactive and non-toxic, easily sanitized, and sturdy, and that they "do not release chemicals or toxins into food." It then says they are inert and do not react with natural chemicals or dyes in food. It also lists the practical costs: weight, breakability for glass and ceramic, bulk, and a container that is harder to carry than a bag. That is extension guidance about the material class. It is not a 2026 certificate for one brand's lid.

The same Iowa State page treats silicone as its own case. It says, citing the FDA, that food-grade silicone is safe and will not react with other materials or release hazardous compounds or fumes when heated, and it says food-grade silicone is made without petroleum-based chemicals, BPA, BPS, PVC, latex, lead, phthalates, or fillers. The next paragraph is the limit: studies on the long-term health effects are limited because the products are fairly new, and silicone is recycled only at special centers. This guide is not going to turn that paragraph into a PFAS test of one bag.

## Reading a food-storage listing

Use the same split this guide used on the eight picks.

**Name the body.** Glass, stainless steel, and ceramic are the materials Iowa State calls inert. A listing that says tempered, borosilicate, or 18/8 is telling you which of those it is. A listing that only says "non-toxic" has not named a material.

**Then name the lid.** Plastic, polypropylene, silicone, beeswax, and a metal lid with a sealing compound are different parts. If the only chemical word on the lid is BPA, that is all the listing has claimed.

**Keep packaging claims on the packaging.** The FDA's 2024 announcement, the California and Washington rules, and the 2022 Consumer Reports tests are about paper, paperboard, and molded fiber. Moving last night's pizza into one of the glass containers above is the step those sources support. Heating it in the box is the step they tell you to skip.

**Use the container as intended.** Iowa State says a food-grade material is food-safe when it is used the way it was meant to be used. That is the oven number on the U-Konserve page, the dry-food line on the LunchBots listing, the cool-food line on Bee's Wrap, and the lid-off line on the glass sets.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is glass food storage PFAS-free?

Iowa State Extension says glass is inert and does not release chemicals or toxins into food when it is used as intended. The Pyrex, Glasslock, Razab, and Ball listings name a glass body. None of those listings, on the pages checked for this guide, say "PFAS-free." The lid on the Pyrex and Razab sets is plastic. Glasslock's US page says the lid is polypropylene with a silicone gasket. Ball's canning lid has a softened sealing compound. The glass and the lid are two claims.

### Are stainless steel food containers PFAS-free?

Iowa State Extension puts stainless steel with glass and ceramic: non-reactive, and inert in the page's words. LunchBots' listing says 18/8 stainless steel is what touches the food in the Medium Trio, and that the lids are not watertight. U-Konserve states an 18/8 body and a platinum silicone lid, and states tests for BPA, BPS, and phthalates. Neither brand page checked here says PFAS.

### Do container lids contain PFAS?

This guide cannot answer that for every lid. The pages checked name the lid material and then stop. Pyrex's listing says BPA-free plastic. Glasslock's page says polypropylene and a silicone gasket, and BPA-free. U-Konserve and Stasher state platinum silicone and name BPA, BPS, phthalates, and, for Stasher, lead and latex. Ball states a softened sealing compound on a BPA-free metal canning lid. None of those pages use the word PFAS. A lid is the part to read because it is not the inert body.

### Did the FDA ban PFAS in every food container?

No. The February 28, 2024 announcement says grease-proofing materials containing PFAS are no longer being sold for U.S. food packaging, and it names fast-food wrappers, microwave popcorn bags, take-out paperboard, and pet food bags. The same announcement says the FDA has authorized certain PFAS for limited use in cookware, food packaging, and food-processing equipment. California and Washington restrict PFAS in plant-fiber food packaging. Those rules do not say a reusable glass bowl has been laboratory tested.

### What did Consumer Reports find in food packaging?

On March 24, 2022, Consumer Reports reported measurable total organic fluorine in more than half of 118 food-packaging products from restaurants and grocery stores. Thirty-seven products were above 20 ppm, and 22 were above 100 ppm. The products were items such as fry bags, burger wrappers, molded-fiber bowls, and paper plates. CR said to move food out of that packaging and not to reheat it there. The test was not a test of the glass, steel, wrap, or silicone bag in this guide.

### Is Bee's Wrap PFAS-free?

The brand FAQ does not say that. It says original Bee's Wrap is GOTS-certified organic cotton, responsibly sourced beeswax, organic plant oil, and tree resin, and that it is for cooled food, not raw meat or fish. Those named materials are why it is on this list. Compostable, on that page, is an end-of-life sentence.

### Are Stasher bags PFAS-free?

Stasher's FAQ does not say that. It says the bags are platinum, food-grade silicone, free of lead, latex, BPA, BPS, and phthalates, with an oven limit of 425°F and the seal partly open. Silicone is a silicon-and-oxygen material, which the [silicone guide](/post/is-silicone-bakeware-pfas-free-safer-baking-swaps) distinguishes from PTFE. Iowa State says long-term health studies of silicone storage are limited. This guide did not test the bag.

### Should leftovers stay in the takeout box?

Move them. Consumer Reports said PFAS are more likely to migrate the longer food sits in the packaging, and said not to reheat in that packaging. The FDA's 2024 announcement is about the grease-proofing that was used on that kind of paper and paperboard. A glass container from the list above is the swap. Take the plastic lid off before the microwave or the oven if the listing says the lid is not rated for that heat.

## Bottom line

Pick the body first. Glass and stainless steel are the materials Iowa State Extension calls inert, and the eight listings above name what the body is. Then read the lid, because that is the plastic, silicone, wax, or sealing compound, and the brand pages checked for this guide do not call those parts PFAS-free. The PFAS evidence cited here sits on grease-proofed paper packaging: the FDA's February 28, 2024 sales stop, California's and Washington's plant-fiber packaging rules, and Consumer Reports' 2022 organic-fluorine tests. The containers are in the [non-toxic products directory](/non-toxic-products).
