## PFAS, nonstick coatings, and a baking pan

The published PFAS problem next to a baking pan sits in two different places. One is a fluoropolymer coating, usually PTFE, applied so food will release. The other is grease-proofing on paper packaging, which is a different object from a metal sheet.

The [U.S. EPA's PFAS Explained page](https://www.epa.gov/pfas/pfas-explained) says PFAS are widely used, long-lasting chemicals, components of which break down very slowly over time. It says many PFAS are found in the blood of people and animals all over the world, and that scientific studies have shown that exposure to some PFAS in the environment may be linked to harmful health effects in humans and animals. It also says there are thousands of PFAS chemicals in many different products, which makes the class hard to study. That page is not a test of a sheet pan.

The [FDA's page on authorized PFAS uses in food contact](https://www.fda.gov/food/process-contaminants-food/authorized-uses-pfas-food-contact-applications) puts the uses in four groups: nonstick coatings, sealing gaskets for food-processing equipment, manufacturing aids, and grease-proofing agents for paper packaging. For nonstick on pots and pans, the FDA says the PFAS molecules are polymerized and applied at very high temperatures so the coating is tightly bound, that the process drives off virtually all of the smaller molecules that could migrate, and that studies show negligible amounts of PFAS in this coating can migrate to food. A footnote says polymerized or large-molecule PFAS are not absorbed by the human body when ingested. The page says current data indicate that only the paper and paperboard grease-proofers would result in dietary exposure that may raise a potential safety concern. It says that in February 2024 the FDA announced those grease-proofing substances were no longer being sold into the U.S. market, and that in January 2025 the FDA determined that the 35 related food-contact notifications are no longer effective. The nonstick row names pots and pans. It does not name the eight products in the table, and a finding of negligible migration is not a sentence that a home pan has been tested.

Health Canada's household page is the one that talks about bakeware as bakeware. It says many nonstick coatings are made with PFAS, that PTFE is the most commonly used PFAS in those coatings, and that PFOA was formerly used in making nonstick coatings and has been phased out. It says nonstick cookware or bakeware may produce irritating or poisonous fumes if heated to high temperatures, and it gives an empty pan left on a burner as the example. The use lines say not to preheat an empty nonstick pan, and not to use nonstick cookware or bakeware above 260°C (500°F). Those lines are instructions for a nonstick coating. They are not a description of bare steel or bare glass.

On the materials that are not that coating, the same Health Canada page says ceramic, enamel, and glass can be heated to fairly high temperatures and are easily cleaned, and that pigments used to make, glaze, or decorate them can contain lead or cadmium. It says stainless steel is iron with other metals, commonly chromium and nickel, and that cast iron is primarily iron. It says not to cook or store acidic foods such as stewed rhubarb or stewed tomatoes in uncoated cast iron for long periods. On aluminum, it says not to cook or store food for long periods, and not to use aluminum for foods high in acid or salt, because acid, salt, and a worn surface can transfer aluminum or lead more readily. It also says some aluminum cookware manufactured outside North America has been found to transfer lead into food. Those are class warnings. They are not a 2026 certificate for one brand.

Iowa State University Extension's October 10, 2023 AnswerLine page, [Safe Food Storage Containers](https://blogs.extension.iastate.edu/answerline/2023/10/10/safe-food-storage-containers/), is about storage, not about a sheet pan. On glass, stainless steel, and ceramic, it says the materials are non-reactive and "do not release chemicals or toxins into food," and that they are inert. This guide is using that paragraph as the extension's description of the material class. It is not a test of a lid, a glaze, or a coating someone later adds.

Minnesota and Colorado restrict the sale of cookware that contains intentionally added PFAS, and their cookware definitions include baking sheets and baking molds. The statute language is in the [silicone bakeware guide](/post/is-silicone-bakeware-pfas-free-safer-baking-swaps). A sales rule is not a fluorine test of a pan already in a kitchen.

## Silicone bakeware is a different measurement

A flexible silicone mold is not, by the word silicone, a PTFE pan. Health Canada describes silicone as a synthetic rubber of bonded silicon and oxygen, and it says not to use silicone bakeware above 220°C (428°F). The chemical the 2025 paper measured is not fluorine.

The paper is Zhu, Wawrzynczak, Niu, and colleagues, "Silicone bakeware as a source of human exposure to cyclic siloxanes via inhalation and baked food consumption," *Journal of Hazardous Materials*, volume 497, article 139591. The PubMed record is [40876430](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40876430/). The abstract says the bakeware was purchased on the Canadian market. Total cyclic siloxanes, D4 through D16, in 25 products ranged from 680 micrograms per gram to 4,300 micrograms per gram. Migration was measured with an oil-and-sand simulant during 60-minute bakes at 177°C. The average sum of D4 through D16 in the baked simulant was 105 micrograms per gram. A one-hour average of that sum in indoor air reached 646 micrograms per cubic meter and declined rapidly after baking. On a per-body-weight basis, the abstract says young children had the highest exposure from the baked simulant and from room air. The closing sentence calls silicone bakeware a source of cyclic siloxane exposure. The abstract does not say the levels are safe, and it does not say they are unsafe. It also does not mention PFAS.

The full limits, including the 2023 assessment of five named siloxanes, are in the [silicone guide](/post/is-silicone-bakeware-pfas-free-safer-baking-swaps). A baking mat can still be fiberglass coated with PTFE. Read the mat for that word before treating it as silicone. This roundup does not add a silicone mold to the table.

## Reading a bakeware listing

Use the same split this guide used on the eight picks.

**Name the surface.** Uncoated metal, glass, cast iron and oil, and a ceramic body are materials you can point at. A listing that only says "non-toxic" or "nonstick" has not named one.

**Then ask whether a coating is there.** PTFE is a PFAS. "PFOA-free" does not answer that. A ceramic coating needs the brand's own no-PFAS or no-PTFE sentence. USA Pan's Americoat pan is a different product from the bare sheet linked above.

**Keep the heat line with the material.** Health Canada's 500°F cap is for nonstick coatings. The 428°F cap is for silicone. A stainless sheet with no coating described is not that nonstick instruction. A ceramic-coated sheet that prints 550°F is.

**Line the bare pans.** The USA Pan and Nordic Ware pages tell you to grease the pan or use parchment or a baking mat. The liner's own record is in the [parchment guide](/post/is-parchment-paper-pfas-free-safer-food-storage-and-baking-swaps). [If You Care parchment](/products/if-you-care-parchment-paper) is the roll in this directory. Unbleached and chlorine-free are not a PFAS test.

**Move the leftovers.** A glass or steel baker is the baking vessel. Storage is the next object. Glass and stainless containers are compared in the [food-storage roundup](/post/best-pfas-free-food-storage-containers-2026). Take a plastic lid off before the microwave or the oven if the listing says the lid is not rated for that heat.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is uncoated metal bakeware PFAS-free?

The USA Pan Amazon description says the bare half sheet is made without PFAS. The brand page for that bare pan names aluminized steel and does not use the word PFAS. Nordic Ware's listing says pure uncoated aluminum, free of chemicals and coatings, and does not say PFAS. All-Clad's pages name a polished stainless interior bonded with aluminum and do not say PFAS. Uncoated means the listing does not describe a fluoropolymer coating. It is not, by itself, a laboratory certificate.

### Is Pyrex bakeware borosilicate glass?

Not on the listing this guide linked. ASIN B00FN3M480 says glass, made in the USA, and does not say borosilicate. The Corning Museum of Glass says French-licensed Pyrex is tempered borosilicate and that U.S. Pyrex tableware today is, by most sources, primarily tempered soda-lime. Pyrex's Easy Grab page says tempered glass for a different 3-quart dish. None of those pages is a PFAS test.

### Is cast iron bakeware PFAS-free?

Lodge's muffin-pan page says the 6-well pan is made without PFAS and seasoned with vegetable oil. The Dutch oven listing in this directory says the pot is made with only iron and oil and describes it as PFAS-free. Those are Lodge's claims. Health Canada still says not to store acidic food in uncoated cast iron for long periods. This guide did not test the iron.

### Is 100% ceramic the same as a ceramic coating?

No. Xtrema's page says the baker is ceramic through the body, with no metal core and no synthetic nonstick coating, and states that it is made without PFAS, PTFE, or PFOA. Caraway's page says the sheet is a ceramic coating on an aluminized steel core, and states that the coating is made without PTFE, PFOA, or PFAS. One is a ceramic body. The other is a coating on metal. Both claims are the brands'. Amazon showed the Xtrema listing as unavailable on November 27, 2026.

### Does a ceramic coating that says PFAS-free override the 500°F nonstick limit?

Health Canada's household page says not to use nonstick cookware or bakeware above 260°C (500°F), and not to preheat an empty nonstick pan. Caraway states that its ceramic coating is made without PFAS and prints 550°F. This guide reports both sentences. It does not treat the brand's oven number as a change to the Health Canada instruction, and it did not review Caraway's laboratory report.

### Did the 2025 silicone study find PFAS in bakeware?

It did not test for PFAS. The abstract reports cyclic siloxanes, D4 through D16, in 25 silicone bakeware products, an average of 105 micrograms per gram in a baked oil-and-sand simulant, and a one-hour indoor-air level of 646 micrograms per cubic meter at 177°C. It says young children had the highest exposure per body weight. The [silicone guide](/post/is-silicone-bakeware-pfas-free-safer-baking-swaps) separates that silicon-oxygen material from PTFE.

### Did the FDA ban PFAS in bakeware?

The FDA page on authorized food-contact uses still describes polymerized nonstick on pots and pans as a use with negligible migration. The sales stop it describes is the grease-proofing used on paper and paperboard packaging, announced in February 2024, with the related notifications determined no longer effective in January 2025. That paper story is the [parchment guide](/post/is-parchment-paper-pfas-free-safer-food-storage-and-baking-swaps). It is not a recall of a baking sheet.

### Should acidic food sit in an aluminum or cast-iron pan?

Health Canada says not to cook or store foods high in acid or salt in aluminum for long periods, and not to store acidic food in uncoated cast iron for long periods. Nordic Ware's listing says tomato products and vinegar can darken or pit the aluminum. A normal bake is a different job from holding a tomato sauce in the pan overnight.

## Bottom line

Name the surface. Uncoated aluminized steel, uncoated aluminum, stainless steel, glass, cast iron and oil, and a 100% ceramic body are the materials the listings above actually name. A ceramic coating is a separate claim, and Caraway is here because the brand states the coating is made without PTFE, PFOA, and PFAS. PTFE nonstick is the PFAS Health Canada names, with a 500°F cap and a warning not to preheat an empty pan. The FDA's negligible-migration finding is about polymerized nonstick on pots and pans, not a test of these eight pieces, and the agency's packaging phase-out is about paper.
