**Quick answer:** Toilet paper can contain PFAS at low levels, and the testing does not show that companies add them so the sheet will resist water. A University of Florida paper, published online March 1, 2023, in *Environmental Science & Technology Letters*, measured 34 PFAS in 21 toilet paper samples from Africa, North America, South and Central America, and Western Europe. Six PFAS were detected. 6:2 fluorotelomer phosphate diester, called 6:2 diPAP, was the most prevalent, at a mean of 3.2 nanograms per gram, and it accounted for about 91% of the PFAS mass. The authors estimated that toilet paper use could contribute 6.4 to 80 micrograms of 6:2 diPAP per person per year to wastewater. In their country table, that load explains 3.7% of the 6:2 diPAP in U.S. sludge, 35% in Sweden, and 89% in France. Recycled and nonrecycled samples did not differ. The American Forest & Paper Association told WSVN that PFAS, including 6:2 diPAP, are not used to make toilet paper in the United States. Lead author Jake Thompson told *The Guardian* he was not rushing to change his toilet paper. A separate 2022 screen of 17 brands, reported by Mamavation and Environmental Health News, found fluorine at 10 to 35 parts per million in four brands and read those levels as unintentional. Neither study measured how much PFAS, if any, passes through skin during wiping.

Below is what those tests and the industry statement actually say, and which swaps the listings support. PFAS in drinking water are covered in the [water filter guide](/post/do-water-filters-remove-pfas-best-pfas-water-filters). Grease-proofed food paper is a different product, covered in the [parchment guide](/post/is-parchment-paper-pfas-free-safer-food-storage-and-baking-swaps).

## Does toilet paper contain PFAS?

Some of the rolls that have been tested do, at low concentrations of named PFAS or at low organic fluorine. A blank package is not a lab result, and a bamboo or recycled label is not one either.

The Florida paper looked for named PFAS and reported them in nanograms per gram. The 2022 consumer screen looked for organic fluorine, a marker, in parts per million. A nanogram of 6:2 diPAP and a part-per-million of fluorine are different measurements. This guide keeps them apart. The Florida paper did not name brands. The fluorine screen did.

## What did the University of Florida study measure?

The paper is Jake T. Thompson, Boting Chen, John A. Bowden, and Timothy G. Townsend, "Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances in Toilet Paper and the Impact on Wastewater Systems," *Environmental Science & Technology Letters* 2023, volume 10, issue 3, pages 234–239. Townsend is the corresponding author. The work was funded by the Hinkley Center for Solid and Hazardous Waste Management. The authors declare no competing financial interest.

Volunteers collected toilet paper from November 2021 to August 2022 in four regions: Africa, North America, South and Central America, and Western Europe. The lab extracted the paper and analyzed it for 34 PFAS by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. The same list was run on sewage sludge from eight wastewater plants in Florida, collected from June 2021 to August 2021.

Of the 34 PFAS, six were detected in the toilet paper: perfluorohexanoic acid (PFHxA), perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), perfluorodecanoic acid (PFDA), 6:2 diPAP, 6:2/8:2 diPAP, and 8:2 diPAP. The largest was 6:2 diPAP. It represented 91% of the total PFAS mass on average, plus or minus 8%. The figure caption splits the detected mass this way: 91% 6:2 diPAP, 3.7% 6:2/8:2 diPAP (the caption writes that mixed diester as 8:2/6:2), 1.3% 8:2 diPAP, 3.3% PFHxA, 0.6% PFOA, and 0.4% PFDA.

The mean 6:2 diPAP concentration across the 21 samples was 3.2 nanograms per gram. An analysis of variance found that 6:2 diPAP did not differ among regions. A t-test found that it did not differ between samples identified as recycled and samples that were not. Both comparisons were above the paper's significance cutoff (p > 0.05). *The Guardian* described the set as 21 major brands and said the paper did not name them. Thompson told WSVN there was a trace amount in almost all of the samples tested. This guide uses his "almost all," and the paper's count of six detected PFAS, rather than a claim that every roll on a store shelf in 2026 matches the 2021–2022 samples.

In the eight Florida sludges, the three diPAPs together contributed 54% of the sum of 34 PFAS, plus or minus 15%, on average. 6:2 diPAP in that sludge ranged from 27.2 to 750 nanograms per gram, with an average of 141. The paper says diPAPs were also the most abundant PFAS in earlier North American sludge studies, and a smaller share of the PFAS mass in studies from Sweden, France, and China.

## How much 6:2 diPAP could toilet paper send to wastewater?

The paper's estimate is 6.4 to 80 micrograms per person per year. That is the abstract's range, and it is an estimate for wastewater, not a dose for a person wiping.

The authors took the 3.2 ng/g mean and applied published per-person toilet paper use. They cite a range of 15 to 25 kilograms per person per year in places such as Japan, the United States, Canada, and Western Europe, and 2 to 10 kilograms in Latin America, China, and Africa. The low end of that span, 2 kilograms, times 3.2 ng/g, is 6.4 micrograms. The high end, 25 kilograms, is 80 micrograms. WSVN reported that the average American uses 57 pounds of toilet paper a year, and that more than 19 billion pounds are flushed in the United States each year. The paper's own U.S. row uses 26 kilograms per person per year, which is about 57 pounds. This guide treats 57 pounds as WSVN's statement of that usage rate.

The authors then asked what fraction of the 6:2 diPAP already measured in sludge could be explained by toilet paper. Table 1 prints these shares: United States 3.7%, Canada 3.5%, China 6.9%, Australia 7.2%, Sweden 35%, and France 89%. The U.S. row uses a median sludge concentration of 82 ng/g, 26 kilograms of toilet paper per person per year, and 27 kilograms of sludge per person per year. EcoWatch summarized the same comparison as about 4% in the United States, up to 35% in Sweden, and up to 89% in France. The larger European shares come from much lower 6:2 diPAP concentrations in those sludge data sets, not from people in France using more toilet paper.

The paper's closing judgment is that toilet paper should be considered a potentially major source of PFAS entering wastewater treatment systems. The same section says the smaller North American percentage, despite higher toilet paper use, means other 6:2 diPAP sources deserve attention, and it names cosmetics, textiles, and food packaging. Those products are separate guides on this site: [makeup](/post/does-makeup-contain-pfas-pfas-free-makeup-swaps), [rain jackets](/post/do-rain-jackets-contain-pfas-pfas-free-rain-jackets), and [parchment and food packaging](/post/is-parchment-paper-pfas-free-safer-food-storage-and-baking-swaps). The authors also say diPAPs are precursor chemicals that can be transformed into terminal PFAS such as PFHxA, PFOA, or PFDA. That is a statement about environmental transformation, not a measurement of PFOA formed in a bathroom.

Sludge is often spread on land. The introduction says that reuse is why the sources of PFAS in wastewater matter. The paper does not estimate a dose from food grown on treated fields.

## Were the PFAS in toilet paper added on purpose?

The concentrations are low enough that intentional use is the open question, and the sources disagree about the answer.

The paper points to earlier work that found diPAPs in paper-mill waste streams, and says that pattern suggests diPAPs could be introduced during production. Its introduction also says PFAS have been documented as wetting agents in pulping. Those sentences are the authors' reading of prior literature. They are not a mill's ingredient disclosure for the 21 rolls.

Thompson told *The Guardian* that the levels are low enough to suggest the chemicals are used in manufacturing so pulp does not stick to machinery. He also said a trade group's denial might still be true if the PFAS were not added on purpose. His words, as the Guardian reported them: "evidence seems to suggest otherwise," though it may be true that PFAS are not intentionally added, and "Companies might not be aware that it's used because it might come from the manufacturer of the instruments they use." He told the Guardian he was not rushing to change his toilet paper and was not saying people should stop using it or use less of it. "The issue is that we're identifying another source of PFAS, and it highlights that the chemicals are ubiquitous." He told WSVN, "There's this trace amount that's present in almost all of the samples that we tested," and, "The fact that it's there to begin with is alarming." He also told WSVN that the study was not a call to stop using toilet paper.

The American Forest & Paper Association's statement to WSVN, which EcoWatch also reported, says the opposite of intentional use in U.S. mills:

"PFAS (including 6:2 diPAP) is not used in the manufacture of toilet paper, or in the production of other tissue products in the United States."

The statement says the Florida study looks at PFAS including PFOA, that PFOA is widespread in the environment, and that the toilet paper results were close to or below the limit of detection, consistent with environmental PFOA and not with the manufacturing process. The paper's figure does list PFOA, at 0.6% of the detected PFAS mass. The association's point is about that chemical's near-detection-limit level. It is a separate sentence from the 6:2 diPAP mean of 3.2 ng/g.

Read together, the record this guide can actually cite is: named PFAS were measured, 6:2 diPAP dominated, the industry association says those chemicals are not used to make U.S. toilet paper, and the lead author said the amounts are traces that may have arrived without a brand adding them for the sheet. EHN's account of the earlier fluorine screen reaches a similar judgment on dose: the levels were low, which indicates the chemicals were not added on purpose and more likely came from manufacturing or packaging.

## What did the 2022 fluorine screen of 17 brands find?

Mamavation, with Environmental Health News, sent 17 toilet papers to an EPA-certified lab. EHN's August 3, 2022 article says four brands had detectable fluorine from 10 to 35 parts per million: Charmin Ultra Soft, Seventh Generation 100% Recycled Bath Tissue, Tushy Bamboo Toilet Paper, and Who Gives a Crap Bamboo Toilet Paper. EHN says those levels indicate the chemicals were unlikely to have been added on purpose.

Mamavation's results page reports the same four as organic fluorine, which is total fluorine minus free fluoride when total fluorine is at or above the lab's 10 ppm detection level: Charmin Ultra Soft at 13 ppm, Seventh Generation 100% Recycled Bath Tissue at 35 ppm, Tushy Bamboo Toilet Paper at 10 ppm, and Who Gives a Crap Bamboo Toilet Paper at 11 ppm. The page says 4 of 17 products, 24%, had indications of PFAS and 76% did not. Two of the four detections were bamboo, one was conventional wood pulp, and one was recycled paper. Mamavation says organic fluorine is a marker for PFAS and that other fluorinated chemicals, such as some pesticide residues or pharmaceuticals, can also register. The page says a detection in this range is unlikely to be intentional and is more consistent with manufacturing or packaging contamination.

That screen is not the Florida paper. It does not name 6:2 diPAP, and it does not report nanograms per gram. A non-detect means organic fluorine below 10 ppm by that method. It does not mean a laboratory looked for every PFAS. Mamavation's page has added products since the 2022 set of 17. The 10-to-35 ppm range and the four brand names above are the original screen EHN reported. A later non-detect on that page is a later test, not a rewrite of the 2022 count.

Linda S. Birnbaum, former director of the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences, told Mamavation, in the quote EHN published: "PFAS cause effects in males and females of animals and people in nearly every organ and at every life stage. Because toilet paper is created to rub up against such vascular parts of the body, it's logical to be concerned with this exposure." EHN also says the health impacts of PFAS exposure through skin contact are still somewhat unclear. Her sentence is a reason to ask the question. It is not a measured dose from these rolls.

## Can PFAS in toilet paper be absorbed through skin?

No study cited here has measured that.

The Florida paper measured paper and sludge. It did not measure blood, skin, or a health outcome from wiping. EcoWatch says the researchers did not evaluate the health risks of using toilet paper that contains PFAS. *The Guardian* says the paper did not consider the health implications of wiping with contaminated toilet paper, that PFAS can be dermally absorbed in general, and that no research exists on how the chemicals might enter the body during wiping. David Andrews, a senior scientist at the Environmental Working Group, told the Guardian that this exposure is "definitely worth investigating."

Those sentences leave the skin question open. They do not establish a risk from one wipe, and they do not establish that the trace is harmless. Thompson's own reading, on the record in the Guardian, was that he was not changing his toilet paper over the finding.

## Which toilet paper and bidet swaps do the listings support?

Use less mystery, and read the words on the exact package. "Bamboo," "recycled," "unbleached," and "fragrance-free" each answer a different question. The Florida paper found no significant 6:2 diPAP difference for recycled content or region. The 2022 screen found organic fluorine in two bamboo brands and in a recycled brand, and non-detects in other bamboo and recycled brands. Unbleached means the sheet was not whitened. Fragrance-free means scent was not added. Neither word is a PFAS result.

A maker's PFAS sentence counts only for the product it names, and only as that maker's statement, unless a cited lab report is attached. None of the listings below was identified as one of the 21 samples in the Florida paper.

## Frequently asked questions

### Did every toilet paper in the 2023 study contain PFAS?

The paper says six PFAS were detected in the toilet paper samples and that 6:2 diPAP was the most prevalent. It does not print a roll-by-roll non-detect count in the main text. Thompson told WSVN there was a trace amount in almost all of the samples. *The Guardian* reported that all of the toilet paper checked contained the compounds. The samples were collected from November 2021 to August 2022, and the brands were not named.

### Is 6:2 diPAP the same chemical as PFOA?

No. 6:2 diPAP is a polyfluoroalkyl phosphate diester. PFOA is a perfluorooctanoic acid. In the Florida toilet paper, 6:2 diPAP was about 91% of the detected PFAS mass and PFOA was about 0.6%. The paper says diPAPs are precursors that can transform into terminal PFAS, including PFOA. The American Forest & Paper Association said the PFOA results were close to or below the detection limit and consistent with PFOA already in the environment.

### Does toilet paper account for most of the 6:2 diPAP in U.S. wastewater sludge?

Not in this study. Table 1 attributes 3.7% of 6:2 diPAP in U.S. sludge to toilet paper, and 3.5% in Canada. The same table attributes 35% in Sweden and 89% in France, where the sludge concentrations used in the estimate were much lower. The authors still say toilet paper should be considered a potentially major source of PFAS to wastewater treatment systems, and they point to cosmetics, textiles, and food packaging as other North American sources worth attention.

### Did Mamavation find fluorine in most toilet paper?

In the 2022 screen EHN reported, 4 of 17 brands had detectable fluorine, from 10 to 35 ppm, and 13 did not. Mamavation's page gives the four organic-fluorine readings as Charmin Ultra Soft 13 ppm, Seventh Generation 100% Recycled Bath Tissue 35 ppm, Tushy Bamboo Toilet Paper 10 ppm, and Who Gives a Crap Bamboo Toilet Paper 11 ppm. The detection level described on that page is 10 ppm. Later additions on the page are not part of that 17-brand count.

### Does unbleached or fragrance-free toilet paper mean it is free of PFAS?

No. Those words describe whitening and scent. The Caboo unbleached listing says both and does not say PFAS-free. A PFAS claim has to be a separate sentence, and even then it is the maker's statement unless the report is public. The Florida paper also found no significant difference in 6:2 diPAP between recycled and nonrecycled samples, so a recycled logo is not a PFAS result either.

### Does the Bamboo Story listing say the toilet paper is PFAS-free?

Yes. ASIN B09YS275CD, sold by Bamboo Story, says the formula is PFAS-free, and the brand FAQ says the products are independently tested to be free of PFAS. The listing also says unbleached and unscented, with no fragrances. The FAQ does not name the lab, the analytes, or a detection limit. This guide did not find a Mamavation result for the brand.

### Does Caboo say its toilet paper is PFAS-free?

On one Amazon listing, yes. ASIN B01LAJ8F0Q, sold by Caboo Paper Products, puts "PFAS Free" in the title, and the description says the paper is PFAS free. That listing says elemental chlorine free. It does not say unbleached. The unbleached pack, ASIN B0DWZNYRFG, does not say PFAS-free. Caboo's own website says the paper is whitened without elemental chlorine and does not say PFAS-free. Mamavation reported an organic-fluorine non-detect for a Caboo bamboo bath tissue, which is a marker result below 10 ppm, not a named-PFAS test of either ASIN.

### Does a bidet remove PFAS from toilet paper or from water?

It reduces how much toilet paper you need. The LUXE NEO 120 listing and manual describe a cold-water plastic attachment. They do not say the device filters PFAS, and they do not say the ABS body is free of PFAS. The wash water is the toilet supply. Filtering drinking water is a different job, covered in the [water filter guide](/post/do-water-filters-remove-pfas-best-pfas-water-filters).

### Should you stop using toilet paper because of this study?

The lead author said no. Thompson told *The Guardian* he was not rushing to change his toilet paper and was not saying people should stop or cut back. He told WSVN the study was not a call to stop. The practical reading of the sources in this guide is that the measured levels are low, that intentional use is disputed, and that anyone who wants a narrower product can pick an unbleached, fragrance-free roll and prefer a maker that states a PFAS result for that product.

## Bottom line

The Florida study found low levels of named PFAS in toilet paper, led by 6:2 diPAP at a mean of 3.2 ng/g, and estimated 6.4 to 80 micrograms per person per year going to wastewater. The U.S. sludge share in that estimate is 3.7%, not most of the chemical. The industry association says PFAS are not used to make U.S. toilet paper. The lead author said he was not changing his own rolls and that the contamination may not be intentional. A 2022 fluorine screen found 10 to 35 ppm in 4 of 17 brands and called that too low for intentional use. Skin uptake from wiping has not been measured. If you want a swap the listings will support, choose an unbleached, fragrance-free paper, treat a PFAS sentence as the maker's claim, and consider a bidet if you want to flush less paper.
