**Quick answer:** Retail baking parchment is a different product from the grease-proofed food packaging the FDA has been taking PFAS out of. On February 28, 2024, the FDA announced that grease-proofing materials containing PFAS are no longer being sold for use in U.S. food packaging. The agency named fast-food wrappers, microwave popcorn bags, take-out paperboard containers, and pet food bags. In January 2025 it determined that 35 related food-contact notifications are no longer effective, with a compliance date of June 30, 2025 for certain packaging already made. None of that is a brand-by-brand test of baking parchment. For baking, choose a paper whose listing tells you what the sheet is, such as unbleached and chlorine-free. For storage, move food into glass.

## Is parchment paper PFAS-free?

There is no FDA finding that says every roll of baking parchment is PFAS-free. There is also no FDA finding that puts retail baking parchment on the list of PFAS grease-proofers. The grease-proofing uses the FDA describes are fast-food wrappers, microwave popcorn bags, take-out paperboard containers, and pet food bags.

Two limits matter when you shop.

**A paper claim is not a PFAS test.** Unbleached, compostable, and chlorine-free describe how the sheet was made. They do not report a laboratory result for PFAS.

**PFAS can show up without being an added ingredient.** The FDA says PFAS may occur in food packaging as an impurity formed during processing, or as a contaminant, for example when manufacturing water already contains PFAS. Highly sensitive tests can find PFAS even when they were not intentionally used, including at levels the FDA says would not be a health concern. The FDA also says a total-fluorine test only measures fluorine. It cannot tell PFAS apart from other fluorine-containing substances.

Read the maker's own description. Treat "PFAS-free" as a real claim only when those words are on the listing.

## What did the FDA announce about grease-proofing PFAS?

On February 28, 2024, the FDA announced that grease-proofing materials containing PFAS are no longer being sold for use in food packaging in the United States. The agency said this removes the major source of dietary exposure to PFAS from packaging such as fast-food wrappers, microwave popcorn bags, take-out paperboard containers, and pet food bags.

PFAS authorized for food contact fall into four groups: nonstick coatings, sealing gaskets for food-processing equipment, manufacturing aids, and grease-proofing agents for paper food packaging. The FDA says current data indicate that only the paper and paperboard agents would result in dietary exposure that may raise a safety concern. On those papers, PFAS are attached as smaller side chains. The FDA says that under certain conditions a side chain can detach, so PFAS can migrate into food.

The February 2024 announcement completed a voluntary phase-out. In 2020, after a post-market safety assessment raised questions about 6:2 fluorotelomer alcohol (6:2 FTOH), the FDA obtained commitments from manufacturers to stop selling grease-proofing substances that contain that type of PFAS. Separately, manufacturers of the remaining authorized PFAS grease-proofers told the FDA in 2023 that they had stopped selling those products for business reasons. The FDA says it did not have safety concerns for that remaining group.

Long-chain grease-proofers, often called C8 compounds and including PFOA and PFOS, stopped being sold for U.S. food-contact use between 2011 and 2016. The FDA revoked the remaining long-chain authorizations in 2016. The FDA says that as of November 2016, long-chain PFAS were no longer used in food-contact applications sold in the United States.

On January 3, 2025, the FDA announced a Federal Register determination that 35 food-contact notifications for these PFAS grease-proofing uses are no longer effective. The reason is abandonment: the manufacturers or suppliers had ceased production, supply, or use. Those notifications are no longer effective as of January 6, 2025. The FDA set a compliance date of June 30, 2025 for certain paper food packaging that had been produced, supplied, or used before January 6, 2025, so existing stocks could be exhausted. The FDA has also developed a screening method to keep checking paper food packaging for these substances.

This guide is dated after that June 30, 2025 compliance date. The phase-out covers those authorized grease-proofing substances. It is not a test result for every wrapper, bag, or imported package on a shelf.

## Do microwave popcorn bags contain PFAS?

They were one of the uses the FDA names for PFAS grease-proofing. The coating kept oil from soaking through the bag. Those substances are no longer being sold for that U.S. food-contact use, and the related notifications are no longer effective.

Older FDA research shows why the bags were a focus. A 2008 paper from FDA researchers tested fluorochemical-treated paper and found that microwave popcorn contained 3.2 milligrams of fluorochemical per kilogram of popcorn after popping. Butter in that study contained 0.1 milligrams per kilogram after 40 days at 4°C. A 2005 paper from FDA researchers reported PFOA in microwave popcorn bag paper at amounts as high as 300 micrograms per kilogram, and milligram-per-kilogram amounts of perfluoro paper additives transferring into food oil. Those papers describe products tested then. They are not a 2026 store survey.

Pop kernels in a covered pan on the stove instead. ## Do fast-food wrappers and takeout boxes contain PFAS?

They were a main use of the same grease-proofing agents. A 2017 study in *Environmental Science & Technology Letters* tested 407 pieces of U.S. fast-food packaging collected in 2014 and 2015. A total-fluorine screen found detectable fluorine, above 16 nmol per square centimeter, in 46% of food-contact papers and 20% of food-contact paperboard. Within the papers, detection was 38% for sandwich and burger wrappers, 56% for dessert and bread wrappers, and 57% for Tex-Mex packaging. Paper cups and papers that did not touch food had no detections in that set. Compound-specific testing of a 20-sample subset found known PFAS and unidentified polyfluorinated compounds. That study describes packaging collected in 2014 and 2015, before the later phase-out.

Washington State restricts sale, not only manufacturer phase-outs. The Department of Ecology says that under RCW 70A.222.070, certain paper and other plant-fiber food packaging with intentionally added PFAS may not be manufactured, sold, or distributed in the state. Ecology's list covers wraps, plates, food boats, and pizza boxes as of February 1, 2023, and bags, sleeves, bowls, flat serviceware, open-top containers, and closed containers as of May 1, 2024. Manufacturers of those items must keep a certificate of compliance. Ecology has also written that its research indicates PFAS was already being phased out of paper food packaging and would largely be removed in the United States by 2025. That is Ecology's assessment of the market, not a test of home baking parchment.

When a meal arrives in a grease-resistant wrapper or box, move it onto a plate or into a glass container before you reheat it.

## What health effects do agencies associate with PFAS?

The U.S. EPA describes PFAS as widely used, long-lasting chemicals, components of which break down very slowly. EPA says many PFAS are found in the blood of people and animals around the world, and that scientific studies have shown exposure to some PFAS in the environment may be linked to harmful health effects in humans and animals.

ATSDR says epidemiological evidence suggests associations between higher exposure to specific PFAS and higher cholesterol, a lower antibody response to some vaccines, changes in liver enzymes, pregnancy-induced hypertension and preeclampsia, small decreases in birth weight, and kidney and testicular cancer for PFOA. ATSDR says the chance of an effect depends on the dose, how often and how long someone is exposed, and the person's own health. That is what those agencies report. It is not a measure of exposure from one sheet of parchment, and it is not medical advice.

## Glass, plastic, or something else for food storage?

Glass keeps leftovers off grease-proofed paper. A plastic lid, a beeswax wrap, a cotton bag, and a reusable produce bag each do a different job. Check the wording on the listing, and count a PFAS claim only when the listing itself makes one.

## How should you handle takeout and leftovers?

Move food into glass before you store or reheat it. Pop popcorn in a covered pan. Line the baking sheet with parchment whose listing you have read.

1. Transfer takeout from the wrapper or box into a glass container first.
2. Skip the microwave popcorn bag.
3. Use the unbleached If You Care roll when you want a parchment liner.
4. Use cotton bags for produce and beeswax wrap for a washable cover.
5. Treat "BPA-free" and "PFAS-free" as separate claims.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does unbleached or compostable parchment mean it is PFAS-free?

No. Those words describe bleaching and whether the sheet is certified compostable. The If You Care listing says unbleached, totally chlorine-free, FSC certified, and certified compostable. It does not say PFAS-free. The FDA also says a total-fluorine test cannot tell PFAS from other fluorine-containing substances.

### Did the FDA ban every PFAS that touches food?

No. The 2024 and 2025 actions cover PFAS grease-proofing agents on paper and paperboard food packaging. The FDA still describes other authorized uses, including nonstick cookware coatings, gaskets on food-processing equipment, and manufacturing aids, and it says those uses leave only negligible amounts that can migrate into food. Pan choices are in the [PFAS-free cookware comparison](/post/best-pfas-free-cookware-nonstick-pan-alternatives-compared).

### Are microwave popcorn bags still allowed to use PFAS grease-proofing?

The FDA says those grease-proofing substances are no longer being sold for U.S. food-contact use. The 35 related notifications are no longer effective as of January 6, 2025, and the compliance date for certain packaging already produced was June 30, 2025. The FDA is using a screening method to monitor paper packaging. That monitoring is not a test of every bag in every store.

### Is glass food storage PFAS-free?

The Razab listing describes borosilicate glass containers and BPA-free lids. It does not make a PFAS claim. Glass is not a grease-proofed paper. The lids are a separate plastic and silicone part, and the listing says they are not microwave or oven safe.

### Are beeswax wraps a PFAS-free replacement for plastic wrap?

The Bee's Wrap listing says the roll is made from beeswax, organic plant oils, and tree resin, and that it can be washed, reused, and composted at the end. It does not say PFAS-free. It works as a bowl cover and as a wrap for bread, cheese, or produce.

### Can a fluorine test prove baking paper is PFAS-free?

The FDA says total-fluorine methods only measure fluorine. They cannot tell PFAS from other fluorine-containing substances. A screening result is not the same thing as the FDA's decision on grease-proofing authorizations.

## Bottom line

Use parchment for an unbleached, chlorine-free liner that lets cookies and vegetables release from the pan. Use glass for leftovers. The FDA's grease-proofing phase-out covers fast-food wrappers, microwave popcorn bags, take-out paperboard, and pet food bags, and those authorizations are no longer effective.
