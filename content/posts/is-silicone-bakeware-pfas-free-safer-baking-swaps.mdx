**Quick answer:** Food-grade silicone bakeware is not PTFE. Health Canada describes silicone as a synthetic rubber made of bonded silicon and oxygen. A Health Canada science page describes silicones as polymers of siloxanes, and it names polydimethylsiloxane, PDMS, as an example of a silicone. PTFE, polytetrafluoroethylene, is the fluoropolymer Health Canada calls the most commonly used PFAS in nonstick coatings. Minnesota's statute defines PFAS as fluorinated organic chemicals containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom. That base-chemistry difference is not a lab test of one mold, and it does not cover a separate coating. Health Canada says not to use silicone bakeware above 220°C (428°F), because it will melt, and not to use nonstick cookware or bakeware above 260°C (500°F). A 2023 Canadian assessment of five siloxanes did not find harm to human health at the exposures it considered. A 2025 paper by Health Canada scientists measured cyclic siloxanes moving out of 25 silicone bakeware products during baking. The abstract reports exposure. It does not issue a safety conclusion. Some baking mats are fiberglass coated with silicone. Others are sold as fiberglass coated with PTFE. For a baking surface that is not a fluoropolymer coating, the listings checked below are glass, uncoated aluminized steel, cast iron, ceramic stoneware, one food-grade silicone mat, and unbleached parchment.

Pan materials are compared in the [PFAS-free cookware guide](/post/best-pfas-free-cookware-nonstick-pan-alternatives-compared). How to read a parchment listing is in the [parchment and food-storage guide](/post/is-parchment-paper-pfas-free-safer-food-storage-and-baking-swaps).

## Is silicone bakeware PFAS-free?

The polymer Health Canada describes is not a PFAS fluoropolymer. A product sold as silicone bakeware can still be a different material, or a silicone material with something else added, and this guide does not have a fluorine test of every mold on a shelf.

Health Canada's page on the safe use of cookware and bakeware says silicone is a synthetic rubber that contains bonded silicon, which it calls a natural element abundant in sand and rock, and oxygen. The same page says many nonstick coatings are made with PFAS, and that the most commonly used PFAS in those coatings is PTFE. PFOA, it says, is a PFAS that was formerly used in the production of nonstick coatings and has been phased out because of health and environmental concerns. Silicone and PTFE are different paragraphs on that page because they are different materials.

The October 1, 2024 Health Canada science blog puts the same chemistry in polymer language. Silicones are formed from siloxanes, which contain silicon and oxygen and join into long chains. PDMS is an example of a silicone. The blog also says short chains can be left over from manufacturing, and that there have been concerns about whether PDMS may be released during baking into food and air. Those leftover chains are the reason for the research below. They are not fluorine.

Minnesota Statutes section 116.943 defines PFAS as a class of fluorinated organic chemicals containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom. The Minnesota Pollution Control Agency says Teflon is the trademarked brand name for PTFE, one member of the PFAS family, and that Teflon will be prohibited from use in cookware. A silicon-oxygen polymer, as Health Canada describes silicone and PDMS, does not meet that fluorine definition. PTFE does. The practical shopping line is the coating, not the word silicone alone. A mat whose own page says it is coated with PTFE is a PFAS mat even if it is flexible and reusable.

## What heat limits do Health Canada and the listings give?

Health Canada tells people not to use silicone bakeware above 220°C (428°F), and not to use nonstick cookware or bakeware above 260°C (500°F). A product listing can print a higher number. The household page and the listing are then two different instructions.

On silicone, the household page says bakeware made from food-grade silicone has become popular because it cools quickly, tolerates extreme temperatures, and is colorful, nonstick, stain-resistant, and hard-wearing. The minimizing-risk lines on that same page are narrower. Use it according to the manufacturer's intended use and instructions. Do not use it above 220°C (428°F), because it will melt if exposed to high temperatures. Be careful removing hot food from flexible silicone, because the food may slide out very quickly. Use an exhaust fan or otherwise ventilate the kitchen during baking.

On nonstick, the same page says nonstick cookware or bakeware may produce irritating or poisonous fumes if heated to high temperatures, and it gives an empty pan left on a burner as the example. The minimizing-risk lines say not to preheat an empty nonstick pan, not to use nonstick cookware or bakeware above 260°C (500°F) on the stove or in the oven, to ventilate, and to take any cookware or bakeware out of the oven before a self-cleaning cycle.

The page also says that in March 2025 the government published the final State of PFAS report, and that PFAS meeting the definition of fluoropolymers, which includes PTFE, are not addressed in that report and will be considered in a separate assessment. The 220°C and 260°C lines are use instructions. They are not that separate fluoropolymer assessment.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration's page on authorized PFAS uses in food contact puts nonstick coatings in their own group: nonstick on pots and pans, gaskets for food-processing equipment, manufacturing aids, and grease-proofing for paper packaging. For nonstick on pots and pans, the FDA says the PFAS molecules are polymerized and applied at very high temperatures so the coating is tightly bound, that the process drives off virtually all of the smaller molecules that could migrate, and that studies show negligible amounts of PFAS in this coating can migrate to food. A footnote says polymerized or large-molecule PFAS are not absorbed by the human body when ingested. The page says current data indicate that only the paper and paperboard grease-proofers would result in dietary exposure that may raise a potential safety concern, and that those grease-proofers are no longer sold for U.S. food-contact use. The nonstick row names pots and pans. It does not name a silicone mold, and it does not name a baking mat.

One listing checked for this guide prints several oven limits for the same mat. The [Silpat half-sheet mat](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00008T960?tag=nomoreforev05-20), ASIN B00008T960, says in a bullet that it can be used from -40°C to 250°C (-40°F to 500°F). Another block on that Amazon page says the oven and freezer range is -40°F to 428°F. The product description says -40°C to 250°C (-40°F to 482°F). The manufacturer blurb on the same page says heat-resistant up to 480°F. Those figures are not one temperature. Health Canada's household instruction remains 220°C (428°F). This guide does not pick a winner among the listing's own numbers.

## What did Health Canada study in silicone bakeware?

Scientists at Health Canada measured cyclic siloxanes in silicone bakeware, in a baked food simulant, and in air. The 2024 blog described the work while it was underway. A 2025 paper reports the measurements. Neither one is a finding that every silicone mold is free of PFAS, and the paper's abstract does not say the measured levels are safe or unsafe.

The blog, published October 1, 2024, says Health Canada is researching silicone bakeware to see whether it poses a potential health concern. The lab bought bakeware from stores and online retailers in Canada, including loaf pans and muffin trays from different brands. The food simulant was a mixture of oil and sand. Bakeware was heated to common baking temperatures while the air was monitored, and then the simulant was extracted so the lab could see whether PDMS compounds had moved into it. The blog says a 2023 Health Canada assessment found that siloxanes are not harmful to human health or to the environment, and that the bakeware research would add information for further assessment. It also says that if products were found to pose an unacceptable risk, Health Canada could take further action, including recalls. The blog does not publish concentrations.

The paper is Zhu, Wawrzynczak, Niu, and colleagues, "Silicone bakeware as a source of human exposure to cyclic siloxanes via inhalation and baked food consumption," *Journal of Hazardous Materials*, volume 497, article 139591, published online August 17, 2025. The PubMed record lists those authors at Health Canada bureaus in Ottawa. Adam Wawrzynczak is the laboratory chemist named in the 2024 blog. The abstract says the bakeware was purchased on the Canadian market. Total cyclic siloxanes, D4 through D16, in 25 products ranged from 680 micrograms per gram to 4,300 micrograms per gram. Migration was measured with Miglyol 812 N oil mixed with sand, during 60-minute bakes at 177°C, with air measured at the same time. Analytes were measured by gas chromatography and mass spectrometry. The average sum of D4 through D16 in the baked food simulant was 105 micrograms per gram. A one-hour average of that sum in indoor air reached 646 micrograms per cubic meter and declined rapidly after baking. Migration depended on bakeware type, surface area, and fat content. Repeated baking showed a steady decline in migration and emissions, which the abstract reads as depletion of siloxanes in the products over time. On a per-body-weight basis, the abstract says young children had the highest exposure both from eating the baked simulant and from breathing room air during baking. Heavier congeners, D7 through D16, contributed most of the intake from the baked food. The closing sentence says the findings highlight silicone bakeware as a source of cyclic siloxane exposure for the general population in Canada.

Read those numbers as the abstract's results for 25 products, an oil-and-sand simulant, and a 177°C bake. The abstract does not estimate a health risk, and it does not say the levels fall after a specific number of uses. The 177°C bake is below the 220°C limit on the household page. That comparison is arithmetic. It is not a sentence in the paper.

The 2023 assessment the blog summarizes is narrower than the blog's one-sentence version. The Siloxanes Group information sheet, for the assessment published December 9, 2023, covers five substances: cyclotrisiloxane (D3), divinyltetramethyldisiloxane (dvTMDS), hexamethyldisiloxane (L2), decamethyltetrasiloxane (L4), and dodecamethylpentasiloxane (L5). It says people in Canada may be exposed to D3 by eating baked goods made in silicone baking moulds, and by inhaling residual D3 from use of those moulds. It says laboratory studies associated D3 with effects on the liver and with decreased food consumption and body weight. Comparing the exposures it considered with levels associated with health effects, the government concluded that these five substances are not harmful to human health at those exposures, and that they are not entering the environment at levels that are harmful. The sheet treats three other siloxanes, D4, D5, and D6, as already assessed. In those earlier assessments, it says, D5 and D6 were concluded not harmful to human health or the environment, and D4 was concluded harmful to the environment but not to human health. The 2025 bakeware paper measured D4 through D16. The 2023 conclusion about five named substances is not a conclusion about that D4-to-D16 mixture.

## Can a nonstick baking mat be PTFE instead of silicone?

Yes. A flexible reusable mat is not, by those words, a silicone mat. One maker's page says its mat is fiberglass coated with PTFE. Another listing says its mat is fiberglass coated with food-grade silicone and does not mention PTFE.

[LOYAL's Bake & Line mat](https://loyalbakeware.com/products/bake-line-non-stick-mat-585x385mm) says it is made from nonstick woven fibreglass coated with 100% PTFE, with a stated range of -40°C to +260°C. The page also says the mat is food grade and European LFGB and FDA compliant. Those are the maker's words for a product it names as PTFE. This guide did not match that mat to an Amazon listing, and it is not one of the swaps below. It is the example that "nonstick baking mat" can mean a fluoropolymer coating on fiberglass. Health Canada's 260°C line for nonstick bakeware is the same Celsius figure this page prints as the top of its range.

The Silpat listing describes a different construction. The bullets say the mat is made of fiberglass mesh and food-grade silicone, that Silpat products conform to U.S. regulations on food-grade silicone, and that they are NSF and Kosher certified. The description says the mats are made of fiberglass and food-grade silicone, and it says not to cut the mat because fiberglass mesh particles might migrate into the food. The same description says life varies, about 2,000 to 3,000 uses depending on use and care. The listing does not say PFAS-free, PTFE, or platinum-cured. When this page was opened for this guide, Amazon said the item was currently unavailable. The material sentences are still the listing's sentences.

This guide did not find a Health Canada or FDA page, among the pages read here, that treats "platinum-cured" as its own safety class for bakeware. The instruction those pages do give is food-grade silicone, used as the manufacturer directs, and not above 220°C on the Health Canada household page.

## Which state rules cover PFAS in bakeware?

Minnesota and Colorado restrict the sale of cookware that contains intentionally added PFAS. Their cookware definitions include baking sheets, baking molds, and cooking utensils. California's cookware sections, as read here, require disclosure of designated-list chemicals and limit "free of" claims. They do not ban the sale.

**Minnesota.** Section 116.943, subdivision 5, says that beginning January 1, 2025, a person may not sell, offer for sale, or distribute for sale in the state cookware if it contains intentionally added PFAS. Subdivision 1 defines cookware as durable houseware used to prepare, dispense, or store food, foodstuffs, or beverages, including pots, pans, skillets, grills, baking sheets, baking molds, trays, bowls, and cooking utensils. "Intentionally added" means PFAS deliberately added where the continued presence is desired in the final product or a component to perform a specific function. Subdivision 5 does not apply to a product that contains intentionally added PFAS only in electronic or internal components. Subdivision 8 says the section does not apply to the sale or resale of a used product. The Pollution Control Agency's 2025 prohibitions page says it considers cookware to include any product used to prepare, dispense, or store food, foodstuffs, or beverages, and that Teflon, meaning PTFE, will be prohibited from use in cookware. A PTFE-coated baking sheet is the kind of product those sentences are about. A silicone mold whose base polymer is not a fluorinated chemical is not, by the chemistry above, that coating. The statute is a sales rule. It is not a test of a used pan already in a kitchen.

**Colorado.** Colorado Revised Statutes section 25-15-603 defines cookware as a durable houseware product used in residences or kitchens to prepare, dispense, or store food or beverages, including pots, pans, skillets, grills, baking sheets, baking molds, trays, bowls, and cooking utensils. Senate Bill 24-081, the signed act, adds that cookware does not include food equipment intended primarily for commercial settings, including equipment sold to a business with a retail food establishment license. The same act adds section 25-15-604(3.5): on and after January 1, 2026, a person shall not sell, offer for sale, distribute for sale, or distribute for use in the state cookware that contains intentionally added PFAS chemicals. It also repeals, effective that same date, the earlier subsection that had required a PFAS statement on cookware labels. The label rule this guide read in the older codification ran from January 1, 2024, and required the manufacturer to list intentionally added PFAS in the handle or in a food-contact surface, in English and Spanish, with a web address and a machine-readable code. That disclosure subsection is the one the signed act repeals on January 1, 2026. This guide is dated after that repeal. The sale prohibition is the rule the act puts in its place.

**California.** Health and Safety Code section 109010 defines cookware the same way Minnesota does for the product list: durable houseware used in homes and restaurants to prepare, dispense, or store food, including baking sheets, baking molds, and cooking utensils. The designated list is the Department of Toxic Substances Control's candidate-chemicals list. Section 109011 says that beginning January 1, 2024, a manufacturer of cookware sold in the state that contains an intentionally added designated-list chemical in the handle, or in any surface that touches food, must list those chemicals on the product label after the words "This product contains:", plus an English and Spanish line, a web address, and a machine-readable code. The statement also has to appear on the online listing. Section 109013 says a manufacturer shall not claim the cookware is free of a specific chemical if that chemical belongs to a chemical group or class on the designated list, unless no individual chemical from that group is intentionally added. The website version of that limit began January 1, 2023, and the package version began January 1, 2024. DTSC says candidate chemicals are drawn from authoritative lists, including the California Environmental Contaminant Biomonitoring Program priority chemicals. [Range Kleen's chemical-information page](https://rangekleen.com/pages/chemical-information) lists polytetrafluoroethylene for named oven liners and cookware and names that CECBP list, with a link to a CalSAFER candidate-chemical record. When this guide opened that record, the page did not display a chemical name. The statute is a disclosure and claims rule. The sections read here do not say cookware with PTFE cannot be sold.

None of these three laws is a federal ban, and none of them is a fluorine test of a silicone spatula. The FDA page above still describes polymerized nonstick coatings as an authorized food-contact use with negligible migration. State sale rules and that FDA migration statement answer different questions.

## What should you use instead of a nonstick baking pan?

Use a surface that is not described as a fluoropolymer coating, and read the listing for what it actually is. Glass, uncoated metal, seasoned cast iron, and ceramic are the materials Health Canada's household page discusses separately from nonstick coatings. A food-grade silicone mat and unbleached parchment are liners, not pans.

Health Canada says ceramic, enamel, and glass cookware are easily cleaned and can be heated to fairly high temperatures. It also says pigments used in making, glazing, or decorating ceramic, enamel, or glass can contain lead or cadmium, and that glazed ceramics and glassware sold in Canada must not release more than trace amounts under the Glazed Ceramics and Glassware Regulations. Ware brought in from abroad may not meet those Canadian limits. Stainless steel, on that page, is iron with other metals, commonly chromium and nickel, and cast iron is primarily iron. The page says not to cook or store acidic foods such as stewed rhubarb or stewed tomatoes in uncoated cast iron for long periods, and not to use nickel-containing cookware if you are allergic to nickel.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is silicone the same chemical family as Teflon?

No. Health Canada describes silicone as bonded silicon and oxygen, and it describes PTFE as the most commonly used PFAS in nonstick coatings. The Minnesota statute defines PFAS as fluorinated organic chemicals containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom. PDMS, on the Health Canada science blog, is an example of a silicone. PTFE is the fluoropolymer.

### Does food-grade silicone mean a mold was tested for PFAS?

No. Health Canada uses "food grade silicone" for the bakeware it tells people not to heat above 220°C (428°F). The Silpat listing says food-grade silicone and cites U.S. food-grade silicone rules, plus NSF and Kosher certifications. None of those sentences is a fluorine test, and the Silpat listing does not say PFAS-free.

### Should silicone bakeware go above 428°F if the package says it can?

Health Canada's household page says not to use silicone bakeware above 220°C (428°F), because it will melt. The Silpat Amazon page prints higher figures in some blocks, including 250°C and 500°F, and those blocks do not agree with each other. This guide does not treat a higher number on a listing as a revision of the Health Canada line.

### Did the 2025 Health Canada study say silicone bakeware is unsafe?

It did not say that, and it did not say the opposite. The abstract reports cyclic siloxanes in 25 products, an average of 105 micrograms per gram in a baked oil-and-sand simulant, and a one-hour air level of 646 micrograms per cubic meter at 177°C. It says young children had the highest exposure per body weight. It does not compare those levels with a health benchmark.

### Did the 2023 siloxane assessment clear every chemical that can leave a silicone mold?

No. The information sheet's conclusion covers D3, dvTMDS, L2, L4, and L5, at the exposures considered in that assessment, including D3 from silicone baking moulds. D4, D5, and D6 were pointed back to earlier assessments. The 2025 paper measured D4 through D16 from bakeware. Those are different sets of chemicals.

### Are Minnesota and Colorado sale bans the same as a recall of pans you already own?

No. Both rules restrict selling or distributing cookware that contains intentionally added PFAS. Minnesota's definition includes baking sheets, baking molds, and utensils, beginning January 1, 2025, and the statute does not apply to sale or resale of a used product. Colorado's enacted ban on cookware begins January 1, 2026. Neither sentence, in the pages read here, orders a household to discard a pan.

### Does California ban PTFE bakeware?

The sections read here do not. They require a label and an online list when an intentionally added designated-list chemical is in the handle or the food-contact surface, and they restrict "free of" claims for a chemical group on that list. Range Kleen's disclosure page lists PTFE under the CECBP priority-chemicals list. That is a manufacturer's disclosure page, not a sale ban.

### Is a compostable or unbleached parchment sheet PFAS-free?

Not by those words. The If You Care listing, as the parchment guide records it, says unbleached, totally chlorine-free, FSC certified, and certified compostable. It does not say PFAS-free. Use it as a liner. Read any PFAS sentence on its own.

## Bottom line

Silicone bakeware, as Health Canada describes it, is a silicon-and-oxygen rubber, and PDMS is an example of that silicone. PTFE nonstick is a PFAS fluoropolymer. Keep silicone bakeware at or below 220°C (428°F). Keep nonstick cookware and bakeware at or below 260°C (500°F), and do not preheat an empty nonstick pan. The household page says high heat can produce irritating or poisonous fumes, and an empty pan left on a burner is the example it gives. The 2023 siloxane assessment did not find a health risk for five named siloxanes at the exposures it considered. The 2025 Health Canada paper shows that cyclic siloxanes do leave new silicone bakeware into food simulants and air. Read a baking mat for the word PTFE before you treat it as silicone. For the pan itself, glass, uncoated aluminized steel, cast iron, and ceramic are the surfaces in the listings above that are not sold as a fluoropolymer coating.
