**Quick answer:** A water filter removes PFAS only when the technology and the certification match the chemical, and a standard Brita pitcher is a weak match in the tests below. The U.S. EPA says granular activated carbon, reverse osmosis, and ion exchange can reduce PFAS, and it tells buyers to look for certification to NSF/ANSI 53 or NSF/ANSI 58 for PFAS reduction. EPA also says those certifications, as of April 2024, focus on two chemicals, PFOA and PFOS, and do not yet show that a filter will reach the 4.0 parts-per-trillion limits in the 2024 drinking-water rule. In a 2023 Environmental Working Group test of single pitchers, a Brita cut the measured PFAS sum by 66% and a Brita Elite by 22%, while ZeroWater, Clearly Filtered, and a Berkey reached 100% that day. A 2024 lab study of 75 PFAS put ZeroWater and Clearly Filtered near the top and both Brita filters well below them, and the percentages moved when the tap water changed. A 2020 study in North Carolina homes found under-sink reverse osmosis and dual-stage filters near the top, and ordinary carbon filters much less consistent. Replace the cartridge on the maker's schedule. An old filter can give PFAS back.

Below is what those pages and papers actually measured, what a certification covers, and how the filters already in this directory line up. Household swaps outside the kitchen tap are in the [room-by-room guide](/post/your-room-by-room-guide-to-removing-forever-chemicals-from-your-home).

## Do water filters remove PFAS?

Some do. Many filters sold for taste do not make that claim, and a claim on the box is not the same thing as a certification.

EPA's page on home filters, last updated August 3, 2026, says a home filter can be an effective way to reduce PFAS, and that not all filters address PFAS. The types it names as able to reduce PFAS are granular activated carbon, also called charcoal, reverse osmosis, and ion exchange resins. Carbon traps chemicals as water passes through. Reverse osmosis pushes water through a very thin barrier. Ion exchange uses small beads that hold contaminants. A filter can cost as little as $20 or more than $1,000 before maintenance.

The same page says to start with the water you have. Many public systems already have PFAS results. Contact the water provider, or use EPA's monitoring data and PFAS analytic tools. A state health or environmental agency may have a local recommendation. EPA says that if PFAS have not been measured, or if they have been measured below federal limits, a filter may not be useful.

NSF's consumer page adds one thing people try at the stove. Boiling water that contains PFAS does not reduce exposure. Follow the water utility if there is an advisory. Do not boil the water to deal with these chemicals.

## What does a certified PFAS filter actually promise?

Certification is a third-party check that the filter does what the label says, for the contaminants named on that certificate, for the gallons named on that certificate. It is not a promise about every PFAS, and it is not a promise about a cartridge past its replacement date.

EPA says five American National Standards Institute-accredited bodies evaluate drinking-water filters for PFAS reduction: CSA Group, IAPMO R&T, NSF, UL, and the Water Quality Association. The mark on the package should be one of theirs. EPA's instruction is to look for certification to NSF/ANSI 53 or NSF/ANSI 58 for PFAS reduction, then confirm the model in that body's directory. A sentence that only says "NSF certified" can be a certification for chlorine taste, lead, or materials. Those are real tests. They are not a PFAS test.

NSF/ANSI 53 covers health-effect claims for filters that use activated carbon or anion exchange. NSF/ANSI 58 covers reverse osmosis systems. NSF's own history page says the earlier PFAS method was NSF Protocol P473, written after EPA's 2016 health advisory of 70 parts per trillion for PFOA and PFOS combined. A 2017 task group folded that method into Standards 53 and 58. NSF says products and references to P473 were then removed and replaced by those two standards. A box that still says only "P473" is using the old protocol name.

In 2022, NSF says, the standards added a "Total PFAS" claim covering PFHpA, PFHxS, PFNA, and PFBS along with PFOA and PFOS, and lowered the combined limit in the test from 70 parts per trillion to 20. NSF's consumer page says a filter must reduce PFAS to below 20 parts per trillion to make a PFAS reduction claim, and that certified products are retested and the factory is inspected. EPA's home-filter page, describing the standards as of April 2024, says those certifications focus on PFOA and PFOS and do not yet indicate that a filter will remove PFAS down to the levels in EPA's drinking-water rule. NSF says task groups have been working on updates to match that rule. The September 27, 2024 NSF article says the ballot had not been finished. A certified filter can still lower exposure. The certificate's pass line and the legal limit are different numbers.

Maintenance is part of the result. EPA says filters work only when they are maintained on the manufacturer's schedule, and that not replacing a filter on that schedule can increase the risk of PFAS exposure. A saturated carbon bed can release what it caught. That shows up in the home study below.

## What is the status of the EPA 4 ppt limit?

On April 10, 2024, EPA announced a final drinking-water rule for six PFAS. The enforceable maximum contaminant levels are 4.0 parts per trillion for PFOA and 4.0 parts per trillion for PFOS. The health-based goals for those two are zero. The same rule set 10 parts per trillion each for PFHxS, PFNA, and HFPO-DA, also called GenX, and a hazard-index limit of 1 for mixtures of two or more of PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and PFBS. EPA's rule page says public water systems had until 2027 to finish initial monitoring and until 2029 to install treatment if the averages exceed a limit. The Federal Register notice for a later proposal states the compliance date in the 2024 rule as April 26, 2029.

That rule is still the one described on EPA's PFAS drinking-water page. On May 18, 2026, EPA announced two proposed changes. One would keep the PFOA and PFOS limits and let a water system request two more years, to April 26, 2031. EPA's page on that proposal says the 4.0 parts-per-trillion limits would not change. The other proposal would rescind the limits for PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and the hazard-index mixture that includes those three plus PFBS. Both comment periods closed on July 20, 2026. The EPA pages reviewed for this guide still call both actions proposals. They are not a repeal of the PFOA and PFOS limits, and they do not change what a home filter has to do today.

The proposed extension, as written in the Federal Register, is not automatic. A system that does not receive it would still face the April 26, 2029 date. The notice also says systems granted the extra time, with results at or above 12 nanograms per liter, would have to use interim steps. One of those steps is providing pitchers or other devices certified to reduce PFOA and PFOS. That is a proposal about public water systems. It is not a test of the pitcher in a kitchen.

## What did EWG's home test of Brita, ZeroWater, and other pitchers show?

EWG's filter guide is a snapshot, and the authors say so. Sydney Evans, Tasha Stoiber, and Anthony Lacey published it on July 11, 2023. Staff bought 10 filters, prepared each one by the instructions, ran 10 gallons of unfiltered tap water through it, and compared one filtered sample with tap water taken the same day. The lab was SimpleLab's GenX and PFAS water test, aimed at 25 PFAS. The percentage is the drop in the sum of the PFAS the lab detected. EWG tested one unit of each filter and one water sample. It did not run the filters to the end of their rated life, and it did not test every PFAS.

The filters were split across two tap samples. Tap sample 1 had a reported total of 49.0. Tap sample 2 had 44.1. The table does not print a unit on those totals. The percentages are:

| Filter | Tap sample | Percent reduction of the PFAS sum | Filtered total in the table |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brita | 1 | 66% | 16.6 |
| Clearly Filtered | 1 | 100% | none detected |
| Epic | 1 | 98% | 1.2 |
| Seychelle | 1 | 48% | 25.7 |
| ZeroWater | 1 | 100% | none detected |
| Amazon Basics | 2 | 54% | 20.2 |
| Aquagear | 2 | 65% | 15.5 |
| Berkey | 2 | 100% | none detected |
| Brita Elite | 2 | 22% | 34.4 |
| PUR | 2 | 79% | 9.3 |

Read Brita and Brita Elite as two tests, not as a ranking on the same glass of water. EWG's tester also wrote that the Elite filter did not seal well, and that leaks could be one reason it had the lowest reduction. A leak around a cartridge is untreated water mixing back in. It is not a measurement of the carbon by itself.

On the individual chemicals, the table shows PFOA and PFOS as not detected after ZeroWater, Clearly Filtered, Berkey, PUR, and Epic. The Brita sample still listed PFOA at 1.2, against 4.0 in tap sample 1, and listed PFOS as not detected. Brita Elite still listed both: PFOS 1.8 against 2.7 in tap sample 2, and PFOA 2.7 against 3.7. "Removes PFAS" and "removes PFOA and PFOS" are different lines in that table.

EWG's costs are the authors' shopping notes, not a lab result. They assumed a household of four filtering 2 gallons a day. On that assumption, ZeroWater's filters were the short-lived ones: about 20 gallons, on the order of 10 days, with a one-year cost they put at $646.06 after a pitcher priced at $24.99. Clearly Filtered was about 100 gallons and about 50 days, with a one-year cost of $436.50 from a $90 pitcher. The low purchase price and the cost of replacements are different questions.

## What did the 2024 lab study find after 160 liters?

Teymoorian, Dinh, Barbeau, and Sauvé published the pitcher study on June 4, 2024, in *Frontiers in Environmental Chemistry*. They ran Brita Elite, Brita Standard model OB03, a ZeroWater 5-stage pitcher, Aquagear, and Clearly Filtered on unspiked tap water from two Quebec systems. Saint-Donat water, from untreated groundwater, had a sum of 56 nanograms per liter across the 75 PFAS on the target list. Montreal water, from the St. Lawrence River, had 13 nanograms per liter. They sampled after 20, 40, 80, and 160 liters and reported a volume-weighted average, which is closer to what a person would drink across that stretch than a single cup at the end.

On Saint-Donat water, that average removal of total PFAS was 99% for ZeroWater, 99% for Clearly Filtered, 77% for Aquagear, and 20% for Brita Elite. Brita Standard was not run on that water. On Montreal water the averages were about 100% for ZeroWater, 96% for Clearly Filtered, 60% for Aquagear, 48% for Brita Elite, and 38% for Brita Standard. The same filter did not post the same percentage in both cities. The paper points at the water, including organic carbon, dissolved solids, and anions, and at which PFAS were in it. Saint-Donat had more short-chain PFAS, which carbon holds less well.

Brita Elite got worse as the liters added up. On Saint-Donat water it removed 36% of the PFAS sum after 20 liters and 8% after 160 liters. On Montreal water it removed 76% after 20 liters and 39% after 160 liters. The volume-weighted averages, 20% and 48%, hide that drop. For PFOA plus PFOS, the Elite average was 29% on Saint-Donat water and 64% on Montreal water. Brita Standard, on Montreal water only, averaged 38% for the PFAS sum and about 42% for PFOA plus PFOS. The authors say Brita was not NSF/ANSI 53 certified for PFAS removal. Their table gives both Brita filters NSF/ANSI 42 and 53, and leaves the PFOA/PFOS claim blank. Standard 42 is taste and odor. Standard 53 is a health-effect standard only for the claims on the certificate.

The paper's table, as of that study, marks ZeroWater as NSF-certified for a PFOA/PFOS reduction claim and Clearly Filtered as certified by the Water Quality Association for that claim. Those are the certifications the authors recorded. They are not a 2026 directory check, and the company pages below show how a certification can move.

ZeroWater's PFAS result stayed high, and another measurement did not. On Saint-Donat water, after 80 liters, the authors saw dissolved solids and organic carbon coming back off the filter and a clear drop in pH. PFAS removal was still about 99%. They write that ZeroWater treats acidic filtered water as the signal that the cartridge is exhausted and should be replaced. A PFAS test on a young cartridge does not describe the week the pitcher tastes sharp. On Montreal water they did not see that release. A home test of ZeroWater on Montreal water matched the lab. The water, not the brand name, was the variable.

## What did the North Carolina home study find for reverse osmosis?

Herkert, Merrill, Peters, Bollinger, Zhang, Hoffman, Ferguson, Knappe, and Stapleton published the home study online on February 5, 2020, in *Environmental Science & Technology Letters*. Duke University and North Carolina State University sampled real filters in use. The set was three perfluoroalkyl sulfonic acids, seven perfluoroalkyl carboxylic acids, and six per- and polyfluoroalkyl ether acids, in 61 homes in central North Carolina and 12 in the southeast. The filters were whatever residents already owned: pitchers, faucet mounts, refrigerator carbon blocks, under-sink carbon, under-sink dual-stage, under-sink reverse osmosis, and a few whole-house systems. Reported filter age ran from under a month to more than five years, with an average of 7.5 months. The authors did not find a statistical link between removal and brand, the water, or filter age in that sample. That is not a reason to skip a replacement. EPA's warning about a late cartridge is about the cartridge that has filled up. This study is about the spread they happened to measure.

Every under-sink reverse osmosis filter and every dual-stage filter they tested removed the perfluoroalkyl acids at 90% or better. In central North Carolina the reverse osmosis group was 11 units. Average removal was 100% for PFOS, greater than 92% for PFOA, and greater than 98% for PFBA, the short four-carbon acid that carbon often misses. Dual-stage filters, four units in that region, had table averages from greater than 92% to greater than 99%. The authors say it is not clear why the dual-stage units did that well, because they tested few of them. Reverse osmosis is the less surprising half: a membrane after a prefilter stops more than carbon does.

Pitchers were a different story, and the paper does not name the brands. Thirteen pitcher filters in central North Carolina averaged 71% for PFOS, 67% for PFOA, and 36% for PFBA. Refrigerator filters, 22 of them, were in the same neighborhood, about 61% for PFOS and 71% for PFOA. Across the activated-carbon filters that were not dual-stage, the abstract's summary is the one to remember: long-chain PFAS were removed at about 60% to 70%, and short-chain PFAS at about 40%. Eighty-five percent of the pitchers showed a statistically significant drop. Fifteen percent did not. A pitcher is not one product.

Whole-house carbon was the result that cuts against a common assumption. The abstract says eight whole-house activated-carbon systems were evaluated, and that in four of the eight, PFAS levels were higher after the filter. In the central North Carolina table, six whole-house carbon systems averaged a 34% increase for PFBA and an 85% increase for PFPA, the five-carbon carboxylic acid in that table, while PFOS still fell by 67% and PFOA by only 19%. A negative percent means the water left the filter with more of that chemical than it entered with. The authors' reading is that a full carbon bed can let go of what it adsorbed. A whole-house tank is not automatically the thorough option.

## Does a Brita remove forever chemicals?

Brita's own answer is narrower than the advertising around "filters contaminants." On Brita's page about what pitcher filters reduce, the question "Do Brita filters remove all PFAS or forever chemicals?" is answered this way: Brita Elite filters reduce certain PFAS compounds such as PFOA and PFOS, but they are not certified to reduce all PFAS. The Elite performance-data page for filter model OB06 lists certification to NSF/ANSI 42, 53, and 401 for the substances on that sheet, including lead. The page does not use the words PFAS, PFOA, or PFOS. A lead certification is not a PFAS certification. Brita's pitcher FAQ says the Standard filter is due every 40 gallons, about two months, and the Elite filter every 120 gallons, about six months.

Put that next to the two studies. EWG measured 66% and 22% reductions of a PFAS sum, on one day, on two different tap samples, with a seal problem noted for the Elite unit. The Montreal lab measured 38% and 48% as volume-weighted averages over 160 liters, and 20% for Elite on the higher, shorter-chain water. Brita can lower some PFAS. In these tests it did not clear them, and the Elite name was not the better PFAS result. The certification to look for is the PFAS line, not the brand line.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a Brita pitcher remove PFAS?

It reduces some of them in the published tests, and it is not certified for that reduction. EWG measured a 66% drop in the PFAS sum for one Brita pitcher and a 22% drop for one Brita Elite, on different tap samples, on one day. The 2024 lab study, over 160 liters, found 38% for Brita Standard on Montreal water and 20% or 48% for Brita Elite depending on the city. Brita says the Elite filter reduces certain PFAS such as PFOA and PFOS and is not certified to reduce all PFAS.

### Does ZeroWater remove PFAS?

In these studies, the 5-stage pitcher did. EWG found no detected PFAS after one ZeroWater unit. The Montreal lab found about 99% to 100% removal of 75 targeted PFAS on two tap waters through 160 liters. ZeroWater's certifications page says the 5-stage ion exchange filter is IAPMO certified to reduce PFOA and PFOS, along with lead, chlorine, chromium, and mercury. The same lab saw the filter dump dissolved solids and turn acidic after 80 liters of the higher-anion water, which ZeroWater treats as a spent cartridge. There is no ZeroWater pitcher in this directory.

### Do reverse osmosis systems remove PFAS?

The North Carolina home study found that every under-sink reverse osmosis filter it tested cut the measured perfluoroalkyl acids by at least 90%, including short-chain PFBA. EPA lists reverse osmosis among the home technologies that can reduce PFAS, and NSF/ANSI 58 is the reverse osmosis standard that can carry a PFAS claim. A system certified to NSF/ANSI 58 for dissolved solids or lead is not automatically certified for PFAS. Check the claim list.

### Does boiling water remove PFAS?

No. NSF says boiling water that contains PFAS does not reduce the exposure risk. Use a certified filter, or follow the water utility's advisory. Do not boil the water as the treatment.

### Does NSF/ANSI 53 or 58 mean the water meets the 4 ppt limit?

Not by the text EPA and NSF have published. NSF says a PFAS reduction claim means the filter reduced PFAS to below 20 parts per trillion. EPA says the certifications, as of April 2024, focus on PFOA and PFOS and do not yet show removal down to the 4.0 parts-per-trillion maximum contaminant levels. The AquaTru Carafe sheet's allowed level for PFOA and PFOS is 0.02 micrograms per liter, which is 20 parts per trillion.

### Is NSF P473 still the certification to look for?

NSF says P473 was the earlier protocol and that its method was moved into NSF/ANSI 53 and NSF/ANSI 58. NSF says references to P473 were replaced by those standards. A current listing should name 53 or 58 and the PFAS claim. P473 on an old box is the previous label for a PFOA and PFOS test, not a test against the 4.0 parts-per-trillion rule.

### Did EPA repeal the 4 ppt drinking-water limits?

The EPA pages reviewed for this guide do not say that. The April 10, 2024 rule set 4.0 parts per trillion for PFOA and 4.0 for PFOS. In May 2026 EPA proposed to keep those two limits and to let systems request a compliance date of April 26, 2031 instead of April 26, 2029. A separate proposal would rescind the limits for PFHxS, PFNA, GenX, and the hazard-index mixture. Both were still proposals after the July 20, 2026 comment deadline.

### Why did some whole-house carbon filters increase PFAS?

In the North Carolina study, four of eight whole-house activated-carbon systems had higher PFAS after the filter. The authors' explanation is desorption: a carbon bed that is full can release PFAS back into the water. Short-chain acids were the ones that rose most in the central North Carolina whole-house table. A large carbon tank still has to be changed before it saturates.

### Is the Clearly Filtered pitcher NSF-certified for PFAS?

The company's performance page says the NSF certifications are NSF/ANSI 42 and 372. The PFAS numbers on that page are laboratory removal results, including 99.64% for PFOA and more than 99.80% for PFOS at a spiked dose, reported as maximums. The help center says NSF/ANSI 53 is still being pursued. EWG's tap test and the 2024 study are separate evidence of high removal. They are not an NSF PFAS certificate.

### Is the AquaTru Carafe certified for PFOA and PFOS?

AquaTru's Carafe performance data sheet says models AT100 through AT140 are certified by IAPMO R&T to NSF/ANSI 53, among other standards, and it prints a PFOA and PFOS reduction with a 99.9% average at 300 gallons. The allowed treated-water level on that row is 20 parts per trillion, not 4. AquaTru's FAQ still says Carafe certification was pending. The directory unit is part number AT100ATA01. Confirm that part number against the sheet and the IAPMO listing.

## Bottom line

A Brita pitcher is not, on this evidence, a PFAS filter in the way a certified reverse osmosis system or a tested ion-exchange pitcher is. EPA's technologies are carbon, reverse osmosis, and ion exchange, backed by an NSF/ANSI 53 or 58 claim that names the PFAS. EWG, a 2024 lab comparison, and a 2020 home study all found ordinary pitchers and carbon filters partial and uneven, especially for short-chain PFAS and especially as the cartridge aged or the water changed. ZeroWater and Clearly Filtered were the strong pitchers in the brand-named tests. Reverse osmosis was the strong installed system in the home study. The certificates still stop at about 20 parts per trillion, while the 2024 drinking-water limits for PFOA and PFOS are 4.0, and those limits were still the rule on EPA's pages when this guide was written.
