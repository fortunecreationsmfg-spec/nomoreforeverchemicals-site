**Quick answer:** Fluorinated ski wax contains PFAS. Switzerland's Federal Office for the Environment describes the old categories as low-fluoro, high-fluoro, and pure fluorocarbon. A low-fluoro block is still fluorinated. Freberg and colleagues reported a median serum PFOA of 50 ng/mL in 13 professional waxers in 2010, about 25 times the background level in that paper. Nilsson and colleagues reported a median blood PFOA of 112 ng/mL in eight World Cup technicians, compared with 2.5 ng/mL in the general Swedish population in their paper. The International Ski and Snowboard Federation and the International Biathlon Union banned fluorinated wax at their events from the 2023/24 season. Crawford and colleagues, in a 2026 paper, still detected PFAS in every wax-room dust wipe after those bans. Concentrations were much lower than in 2020, and they were not gone. Minnesota has prohibited sale of new ski wax with intentionally added PFAS since January 1, 2025. A current wax whose maker says fluoro-free, fluorine-free, or PFAS-free is the sentence to look for. Stop using a block labeled LF, HF, FC, Cera, or fluoro, and take it to a local household-hazardous-waste program or a take-back such as the ones in Duluth and Park City.

The same class of chemicals shows up in waterproof finishes. That product is covered in [Do rain jackets contain PFAS?](/post/do-rain-jackets-contain-pfas-pfas-free-rain-jackets). Other rooms in the house are in the [room-by-room guide](/post/your-room-by-room-guide-to-removing-forever-chemicals-from-your-home).

## Do fluorinated ski waxes contain PFAS?

The fluorinated ones do. A wax sold as fluorine-free is a different product, and a 2024 lab comparison found both kinds on the shelf.

PFAS, per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, are a large class of manufactured fluorinated chemicals. EPA's page on health and environmental risks says there are thousands of PFAS, some studied more than others, and that many break down very slowly and can build up in people, animals, and the environment. Minnesota's statute uses the class definition "a class of fluorinated organic chemicals containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom." Ski wax is one of the products that statute names.

The fluorinated glide waxes were built to shed water. FIS's October 31, 2024 account says fluorocarbon waxes became common in cross-country and alpine speed events because the fluor is hydrophobic, and that over the years they evolved toward purer forms of PFAS. Kathryn Crawford's June 3, 2026 essay in The Conversation says manufacturers used PFAS in many, and not all, ski and snowboard waxes from the late 1980s through the early 2020s, especially in waxes for warm, wet, or dirty snow. Her research group's 2026 paper says consumer ski and snowboard wax can contain very high concentrations of PFAS, up to 100 percent by mass.

A fluorine measurement and a short list of named PFAS are different tests. Gonzalez de Vega and colleagues, in Analytica Chimica Acta in 2024, measured 10 commercial ski waxes. In the fluorinated waxes, total fluorine ran from 1,040 to 51,700 micrograms of fluorine per gram. Extractable organic fluorine accounted for 3 to 8.8 percent of that total. Fifteen individual PFAS showed up in the extracts, and the sum of those targeted chemicals was only 0.01 to 1.0 percent of the extractable organic fluorine. The targeted chemicals were a thin slice of the extractable fluorine, and the extractable fluorine was a thin slice of the total. Targeted PFAS were detected in all seven waxes whose labels said they contained fluorine. Three waxes labeled fluorine-free, a Toko FF, a Swix CH8, and a Red Creek FF, showed no detectable targeted PFAS, and their total fluorine was negligible and comparable to the blanks. Those three results belong to the blocks purchased for that study. They are a reason to read the fluorine words on a label. They are a certificate for those blocks.

Three fluorinated products in the same study exceeded the 25 nanograms per gram regulatory limit for PFOA that the paper applies: Toko HF at 206, Red Creek HF at 271, and a powder called LDR at 2,820. The paper says Red Creek HF and the LDR powder were purchased before 2020, and that the Toko HF block had no manufacturing date on it. A later brand decision to drop fluorine, below, is a different production from these purchased blocks.

## What do LF, HF, and fluoro mean on an old wax?

They are fluorine categories. Low-fluoro means less fluorine than high-fluoro. It does not mean no PFAS.

The Federal Office for the Environment, in its January 2025 report on fluorinated ski waxes in Switzerland, says manufacturers classify fluorinated glide waxes in three groups. Low-fluoro, abbreviated LF, is about 0.5 to 1.5 percent fluorine. High-fluoro, abbreviated HF, is about 4 to 12 percent. Pure fluorocarbon products, often labeled FC or Cera, contain only organofluorine compounds. The report says the main PFAS in LF and HF waxes, in the scientific literature it cites, are semifluorinated alkanes, and that the fully fluorinated part of the chain in a cited patent is six or eight carbons. Higher-fluorine waxes were the ones used in warmer, wetter snow. FC products were racing waxes.

The words "high" and "low" are not one scale across brands. In the Gonzalez de Vega study, a Swix block labeled LF8 contained 8,740 micrograms of fluorine per gram, and a Toko block labeled HF contained 5,820. The same manufacturer's HF8 contained 12 targeted PFAS. Its LF8 contained 7. Both lists included PFAS. Crawford's essay puts the shopping point under a photograph of three low-fluor waxes: being low-fluor does not mean PFAS-free.

An earlier rule drew the same line inside the fluorinated family. On June 1, 2021, the International Biathlon Union prohibited C8 fluorocarbons and PFOA at its events, in line with the EU POPs regulation and REACH, and said low-chain fluor C6 products were still allowed to be produced, sold, and used. FIS published the same C8 and PFOA event prohibition on June 7, 2021, and said it was still working toward a full fluor ban. A wax that cleared the C8 rule by moving to C6 chemistry was still a fluorinated wax. The full event ban came later.

## What did blood tests of ski-wax technicians find?

Two 2010 papers found perfluorinated carboxylates in professional waxers at levels well above the comparison groups those papers used. The papers are occupational studies of people who waxed with fluorinated products. They are not a blood test of a person who owns one fluoro-free block.

Freberg, Haug, Olsen, and colleagues published "Occupational Exposure to Airborne Perfluorinated Compounds during Professional Ski Waxing" in Environmental Science & Technology in 2010. They measured 11 perfluorinated carboxylic acids and eight perfluorinated sulfonic acids in serum from 13 professional ski waxers, and the same chemicals in workroom aerosols and in fluoro-containing solid waxes and wax powders. The highest median was PFOA, at 50 ng/mL, which the paper says is around 25 times the background level. The paper reports the first finding of perfluorotetradecanoic acid in human serum. Years of work as a waxer were positively associated with seven of the carboxylic acids. During an eight-month interval without that exposure, serum levels of the C8 to C11 acids fell by about 5 to 20 percent on average, and shorter and longer chains fell by more. The authors tie the serum acids to the workroom aerosols, and they write that amateur skiers and waxers are potentially exposed as well.

Nilsson, Kärrman, Westberg, and colleagues published "A Time Trend Study of Significantly Elevated Perfluorocarboxylate Levels in Humans after Using Fluorinated Ski Wax" in the same journal, online on February 16, 2010. Eight technicians gave monthly blood samples in the 2007/08 season: before the season, at four FIS cross-country World Cup events, and through five unexposed months afterward. The paper says PFHpA, PFOA, PFNA, PFDA, and PFUnDA bioaccumulate, and that continued exposure can add to levels already higher than in the general population. The technicians' median blood PFOA was 112 ng/mL. The comparison figure in the paper is 2.5 ng/mL in the general Swedish population. Working years correlated with the carboxylates. Three technicians who started the season under 10 ng/mL of PFOA rose by 254, 134, and 120 percent. Five who started above 100 ng/mL were described as being at steady state. The paper suggests PFHxA has a short half-life relative to the other carboxylates, and it says the perfluorosulfonates did not change with the wax exposure.

FIS's 2024 article describes the job that produced those exposures. Technicians applied fluor with a hot iron, often in unventilated cabins, and breathed fluor fumes. The article says that practice was later phased toward rolling machines, and that a risk remained. Crawford's essay says waxing releases particles and chemicals, including PFAS, into the air. Her essay also describes a pilot study of skiers, in her words: people who waxed more skis tended to have higher PFAS in blood and higher total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol. That sentence is her summary of the pilot. This guide did not review a separate paper for those cholesterol results, so it does not add a number the essay does not print.

EPA's health page is the agency summary for the class, and it is broader than ski wax. CDC surveys, EPA says, show that most people in the United States have been exposed to some PFAS. Most known exposures are relatively low. Some can be high when a person is exposed to a concentrated source over a long time. Some PFAS can accumulate in the body. The routes EPA lists include working with the chemicals, drinking water, food including fish, swallowing contaminated soil or dust, breathing air that contains PFAS, and using products made with PFAS or packaged in materials that contain them. Current peer-reviewed studies, on that page, have shown that exposure to certain levels of PFAS may lead to reproductive effects such as decreased fertility or increased high blood pressure in pregnant women, developmental effects or delays in children, an increased risk of some cancers including prostate, kidney, and testicular cancers, a reduced ability of the immune system to fight infections including a reduced vaccine response, interference with the body's natural hormones, and increased cholesterol and/or risk of obesity. The same page says research is still determining how different levels of different PFAS lead to those effects, and that the effects are hard to specify because there are thousands of PFAS, exposures differ, and the uses change. The wax-technician papers measure blood levels in people who worked with fluorinated wax. They do not, by themselves, assign one of those EPA outcomes to a single afternoon of recreational waxing.

## Do fluoro bans clear PFAS out of a wax room?

They cut the dust levels sharply in the rooms Crawford's group sampled. PFAS were still in every sample afterward.

The paper is Crawford, Hartmann, Andersen, Alden, Gudur, and Chandramouli, "Effects of fluorinated ski wax bans on per- and polyfluoroalkyl substance (PFAS) concentrations in indoor dust from ski waxing workspaces," Environmental Science: Processes & Impacts, first published April 29, 2026. The group wiped dust in waxing work areas in 2020, before fluorinated-wax bans, and in 2023, after bans and cleaning. PFAS were detected in every wipe. Summed PFAS were about two orders of magnitude higher before the bans, 8.08 to 150 nanograms per square centimeter, than after, 0.013 to 0.735. Legacy carboxylic acids dominated most samples. The replacement compound GenX was the most abundant chemical in some samples from both years. The paper says the source of the PFAS in the 2023 wipes is unknown, and that measurable PFAS in every sample implies an ongoing, greatly reduced exposure risk for people who use those rooms. Crawford's essay puts the same finding in plain language: bans reduced exposure, and they did not finish the job, because PFAS already in a room and in a body stay there. She also writes that some skiers still have fluoro wax and may keep using it.

That dust result is why a ban on the next race, or a new fluoro-free block on the bench, does not vacuum yesterday's wax. The cleaning steps are in the practical section below. They come from Crawford's essay and from Swix's own waxing rules.

## What do FIS and the IBU actually ban?

They ban fluorinated wax at the competitions they control. The ban is a start rule and a disqualification rule. It is not a law that empties a garage.

FIS's October 31, 2024 article says the federation announced in 2019 that it would outlaw fluorinated ski waxes, and that a full ban was declared in March 2023 for the 2023/24 season, with the IBU doing the same. The 2024/25 season was the second season of that ban. The IBU's March 22, 2023 release says the full ban of ski-preparation products containing fluor applies at all of its event series from 2023/24 onward. Its fluor-test protocol for that season covers the World Cup, the IBU Cup, World Championships, Open European Championships, and the Olympic Winter Games, and it says skis are tested by FTIR before competition. The Swiss environment office's account of the same history says the FIS executive board first aimed at the 2020/21 season and that the ban took effect in 2023/24 because the testing system took longer to build. Use of a fluorine-containing wax, that report says, leads to disqualification under the competition rules.

Testing, in the FIS article, uses Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy on Bruker Alpha II instruments. A reading is compared with a model built from fluor-free waxes. At or below 1.0 absorbance units, the spot is negative. Above 1.0, it is positive, and three positive spots disqualify the skier. An October 2023 FIS alpine note says the council raised the finish-line threshold from 1.0 to 1.8 until December 31, 2023, and left the pre-race threshold at 1.0 for cross-country and Nordic combined. The 2024 article describes the 1.0 line as the working comparison after that opening season.

The first season's cases, as FIS tells them, were mostly contamination and procedure, which is why an old brush matters as much as an old block. At the first Alpine World Cup of 2023/24 in Sölden, Ragnhild Mowinckel was disqualified from the giant slalom. Her supplier, Head, said the same preparation had tested clean two days earlier. Later tests by Head and the Norwegian Ski Federation blamed a fluor-contaminated waxing tool. FIS confirmed the wax itself did not contain fluor. The article says fluor is hard to remove and recommends replacing soft brushes and roto fleeces rather than trying to clean them. At the Engadin Skimarathon, 12 skiers were disqualified, including winner Maelle Veyre. FIS then reinstated Veyre and two others after what Operational Fluor Manager Augusto Gillio called a miscommunication between the controller and the jury. Gillio said a team of eight people made 60,000 measurements that season and that the World Cup had fewer than ten cases across all disciplines. For 2024/25 he described a team of 12, coverage of every World Cup and the Europa Cup, and more than 7,000 races under the FIS umbrella over a season. He also said some people still hold expensive old wax and use it because they do not want to throw it out. His own line in that interview is that he has old wax in a garage that he will never use again.

A recreational ski day is outside that rulebook. The practical question for a garage or a rental shop is which block goes on the iron.

## Which laws cover PFAS in ski wax?

Minnesota restricts the sale of ski wax that contains intentionally added PFAS. The European PFHxA rule covers mixtures for the general public, and the Swiss environment office says C6 ski waxes are among the products that rule reaches. A universal EU restriction on PFAS as a class is still a proposal. None of these rules is a recall of a block already in a toolbox.

**Minnesota.** [Section 116.943](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/116.943), Amara's Law, says that beginning January 1, 2025, a person may not sell, offer for sale, or distribute for sale in the state ski wax if the product contains intentionally added PFAS. Ski wax means a lubricant applied to the bottom of snow runners, including skis and snowboards, to improve grip or glide, and it includes related tuning products. Intentionally added means PFAS deliberately added during manufacture where the continued presence is wanted in the finished product, or in a component, to do a job. The section does not apply to the sale or resale of a used product. A fluorinated glide wax sold new in Minnesota is the product this subdivision describes. A wax the maker formulates without adding PFAS is a different product claim. The statute does not order a household to discard a block it already owns.

**Europe, PFOA and longer-chain acids.** FIS and the IBU both pointed at Regulation (EU) 2019/1021, the POPs regulation, and at REACH, when they barred C8 and PFOA products at events from the 2021/22 season. A Nordic enforcement project published in 2022 checked chemical products and articles against that POPs regulation and reported that 14 ski waxes contained PFOA at or around the limit values, out of 158 products and articles inspected, of which 15 were non-compliant. Switzerland's Federal Office for the Environment says that, under Swiss chemicals law, ski waxes since June 1, 2021 may not contain more than 0.025 ppm of PFOA or 1 ppm of PFOA precursors, and that since October 1, 2022 they also may not contain long-chain perfluorocarboxylic acids, C9 through C14, and their precursors above 0.025 ppm for the sum of those acids or 0.26 ppm for the sum of the precursors. Those sentences are the Swiss report's account of Swiss law. A PFOA limit leaves shorter-chain fluorinated wax on the table. That is the gap the IBU described in 2021 when C6 wax was still allowed.

**Europe, PFHxA.** Commission Regulation (EU) 2024/2462 adds a REACH restriction on undecafluorohexanoic acid, PFHxA, its salts, and PFHxA-related substances. From October 10, 2026, they may not be placed on the market or used at or above 25 ppb for the sum of PFHxA and its salts, or 1,000 ppb for the sum of PFHxA-related substances, in mixtures for the general public, and also in listed textiles, footwear, food-contact paper and cardboard, and cosmetics. The regulation text does not print the words "ski wax." The Swiss report, describing EU restrictions that Switzerland was consulting on adopting, says the general-public mixtures rule will prohibit those PFHxA mixtures by October 10, 2026, and that ski waxes based on C6 technology will be affected, among other products. The same report says the EU ban, since October 17, 2023, on the first placing on the market of preparations that contain non-biodegradable microplastics concerns ski waxes that contain powdered fluoropolymers in a wax matrix.

**The class-wide EU proposal.** A restriction dossier from five countries would cover PFAS as a group, including uses that are not yet on the market, across manufacturing, sale, and use. ECHA's March 2026 questions and answers on that process list ski wax among the sectors in the original proposal, alongside textiles, cosmetics, and other consumer uses, and they name ski waxes as one example of a consumer product that releases PFAS. The proposal, as that document describes it, would allow continued use of mixtures and articles already on the market. ECHA's news of March 26, 2026 says the Risk Assessment Committee had adopted a final opinion and the Socio-Economic Analysis Committee a draft opinion, both supporting an EU-wide restriction with derogations, and that the Commission would propose a restriction after the committees' final opinions. This guide did not find a Commission regulation that has adopted that class-wide restriction. Until that regulation exists, the binding European texts for a shopper are the ones already in force, including the PFOA rules and, on its own date, the PFHxA restriction.

**Park City.** The city's [ski-wax take-back page](https://www.parkcity.gov/services/public_utilities/water/ski_wax_take_back_program.php) says PFAS have been detected in local groundwater wells and wastewater, and that fluoro ski wax is the probable source of that low-level contamination. The page says a 2023 Drinking Water Source Protection Ordinance banned fluoro wax use in Park City. This guide is relying on the city's page for that ordinance. It did not read the ordinance text. The page also asks residents, visitors, waxers, and ski shops to turn fluoro wax in.

## How do you tell a fluoro-free wax from a low-fluoro wax?

Read the fluorine words. "Fluoro-free," "fluorine-free," "PFAS-free," "non-fluoro," and "no fluoros" are the class claims this guide could confirm on brand pages. "LF," "HF," "FC," "Cera," and "fluoro" are the fluorinated labels.

The environment office says a Nordic Swan glide wax, under the Nordic Ecolabel criteria it describes, must not contain PFAS. That is a label with a written chemical rule behind it. The office also says that, as far as it knew, ski wax was the one product group among the ecolabels it listed that had published award criteria, and that one German hot wax carried the Swan. It does not name that wax in the passage this guide is using.

Brand pages are the other place the words show up, and they have dates.

Swix's PFAS page says the company stopped selling PFAS-containing waxes to the mass market, meaning wholesale, its own stores, and its webshop, by the 2021–22 winter, and stopped selling PFAS waxes to professional athletes by the 2022–23 winter. Its waxing-rules page says, in its own heading, "All of our ski wax we sell is fluoro free." That sentence is about wax Swix sells. A Swix block from an earlier cupboard labeled LF, HF, or fluoro is the fluorinated category above. The CH8 in the 2024 study was one hydrocarbon block with no detectable targeted PFAS. A different old Swix label is a different product.

Toko's October 27, 2022 FAQ says the brand eliminated fluorine from all of its products, that all fluorine products had been destroyed, and that nothing fluorinated was in production. The FAQ answers "Are products with fluorine still sold?" with that destruction sentence.

Purl's FAQ says its wax lines are fluoro-free. The Original Formula collection page says "No Fluoros." mountainFLOW's race-wax page says the wax is plant-based and non-fluoro, and the same site says the company prioritizes PFAS-free and PFC-free alternatives. Hertel's FAQ answers "Is Hertel wax non-fluoro?" with yes, and says the core formulas have not relied on fluorinated compounds. Those are the brands' sentences. This guide did not test a block, and it did not review a laboratory report for any of them.

A shop tune uses whichever wax the shop puts on the base. Ask which product that is, and ask to see the fluoro-free, fluorine-free, or PFAS-free line on it. Swix's statement covers the wax Swix sells now. It does not answer for a different brand's old LF brick in a shop drawer.

## What should you do with old fluoro wax, and how should you wax?

Stop using the fluorinated block. Wax with a product whose maker uses a fluoro-free sentence. Ventilate, keep the iron at the temperature on the box, control the dust, and wash your hands. Take the old fluoro wax to a program that accepts it.

Crawford's essay lists the steps she recommends: stop using fluoro waxes, wax in a well-ventilated space or outside, wear an N95 or a respirator with organic-vapor cartridges, do not eat or drink in the wax space, wash hands and change clothes afterward, and clean the area with a HEPA vacuum and a wet cloth.

Swix's waxing rules for private waxing match the exposure points with its own details. Use fluoro-free products. Give the room a fan that exhausts air and an inlet for fresh air. When ironing or scraping, wear a mask with at least an A1P3 filter, and change the filter at least before each season. A roto brush with built-in suction, connected to a vacuum, is how Swix says to keep brush dust out of the air. Do not set the iron hotter than the wax needs. The temperature is on the box. Swix says the particle concentration rises as the gap grows between the wax's melting temperature and the iron temperature, and it says to wipe the iron after you apply the wax. Wear gloves with liquid products. Do not eat, drink, or smoke in the wax space. Collect the scrapings. Wash your hands when you finish.

The iron line is the "lower temperature" step, and it is Swix's, tied to dust rather than to a universal degree setting. The Purl listing below prints its own iron range for that wax and says to stay below the smoking point. Follow the box in your hand.

Disposal depends on the town. Resource Renew, the household-hazardous-waste guide for the Western Lake Superior Sanitary District, says waxes marked "fluoro" contain PFAS and should go to the Household Hazardous Waste Facility, and that ski waxes without fluorinated compounds can go in the household trash. Park City asks people to turn fluoro wax in at Recycle Utah, which the city says accepts it around the clock, or at White Pine Nordic Center. The city page describes the drop-off as mostly Nordic and alpine race waxes. An LF, HF, FC, or Cera block is a fluorinated wax under the environment office's categories, even when the only word on the sleeve is the abbreviation. Call the local program before you assume the trash can is the right place, because these two programs do not write the rule for every county. The Minnesota sale ban does not require you to throw out a used block. Leaving it on a shelf, where someone may iron it later, is the outcome Gillio described.

1. Treat LF, HF, FC, Cera, and fluoro as fluorinated labels. Low-fluoro is a smaller share of fluorine, and it is still PFAS chemistry.
2. Look for fluoro-free, fluorine-free, PFAS-free, non-fluoro, or no fluoros on the current product. A PFOA-free or C8-free line left C6 fluoro wax on sale, which is what the IBU said in 2021.
3. Ask a shop which wax it will use on a tune, and ask for the fluoro-free sentence on that product.
4. Wax with the ventilation, mask, iron temperature, dust control, and handwashing steps Swix and Crawford publish.
5. Stop using old fluoro wax. Resource Renew tells its service area to take wax marked fluoro to household hazardous waste. Park City collects it at Recycle Utah and White Pine Nordic Center. Ask the program where you live.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is ski wax toxic?

Fluorinated ski wax contains PFAS, and professional waxers in two 2010 studies carried PFOA in blood well above the comparison groups in those papers. EPA says studies have shown that certain levels of PFAS may lead to reproductive, developmental, immune, and cholesterol effects, and to a higher risk of some cancers, and that research is still working out which levels of which PFAS do that. A wax whose maker states it is fluoro-free is a different product. This guide does not have a dose for one recreational wax job.

### Does low-fluoro mean the wax is PFAS-free?

No. The Swiss environment office puts low-fluoro wax at about 0.5 to 1.5 percent fluorine and high-fluoro at about 4 to 12 percent. Crawford's caption on a set of low-fluor blocks says low-fluor does not mean PFAS-free. The 2024 lab study found targeted PFAS in both an LF and an HF wax.

### Did FIS ban fluoro wax for everyone?

FIS and the IBU banned fluorinated ski-preparation products at their own competitions from the 2023/24 season. A positive fluor test at those events can disqualify a racer. The rule does not, by itself, make it illegal to own an old block at home. Minnesota's sale ban is the U.S. statewide rule this guide confirmed, and it starts at the cash register for new ski wax with intentionally added PFAS.

### Is the wax on a shop tune fluoro-free?

Ask. Swix says the ski wax it sells is fluoro-free. A shop can still iron a different brand, or an older block labeled LF, HF, or fluoro. The fluoro-free sentence has to be on the product they name.

### Can I throw old fluoro wax in the trash?

Resource Renew, for the Duluth-area sanitary district, says wax marked fluoro should go to the household hazardous waste facility, and that non-fluorinated ski wax can go in the trash. Park City collects fluoro wax at Recycle Utah and at White Pine Nordic Center. Another county can write a different rule. Check before you use the trash, and do not keep using the block in the meantime.

### Will a new fluoro-free wax remove PFAS from the wax room?

The new wax does not clean the room. Crawford's 2026 study found PFAS in every dust wipe in 2023, after bans and cleaning. The 2020 sums were 8.08 to 150 ng/cm². The 2023 sums were 0.013 to 0.735 ng/cm². The paper calls that drop about two orders of magnitude. Her essay says to stop using fluoro wax and to vacuum with a HEPA filter and wipe with a wet cloth. Swix says to capture brush dust with a vacuum and to wash your hands.

## Bottom line

Fluorinated ski wax is a PFAS product. LF, HF, FC, Cera, and fluoro are the labels that mark it, and low-fluor is the low end of that scale, not the exit. Professional waxers who worked with those products had elevated PFOA in the 2010 studies. FIS and the IBU stopped fluorinated wax at their races in 2023/24. Dust in wax rooms fell after bans and was still contaminated. Minnesota stopped the sale of new ski wax with intentionally added PFAS in 2025. The European PFHxA restriction reaches mixtures for the general public in October 2026, and the Swiss environment office says C6 ski waxes are in that group. A class-wide EU ban is not a regulation this guide could find in force. For a block to buy, use the fluoro-free, fluorine-free, or PFAS-free sentence the maker actually prints, including the Purl, Toko, Swix, mountainFLOW, and Hertel statements above. For a block to retire, stop ironing it and hand it to the local hazardous-waste or take-back program. Waterproof clothing is a separate PFAS question, answered in the [rain jacket guide](/post/do-rain-jackets-contain-pfas-pfas-free-rain-jackets).
